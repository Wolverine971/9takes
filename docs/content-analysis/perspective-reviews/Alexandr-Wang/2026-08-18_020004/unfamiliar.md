## Bottom-line verdict

I came in knowing nothing about Alexandr Wang and left able to state who he is, why he matters, and
what the article claims about him, without opening a single tab. That is the bar for this
perspective and the draft clears it comfortably. The Disney World opening is the best kind of hook
for a reader with no prior knowledge: it needs no context, it is concrete, and it turns out to be
the actual argument rather than decoration.

Trust is strained rather than broken, and it is strained by a specific and fixable pattern. The
piece is meticulous about attribution everywhere the reader can check it — outlet and date beside
almost every quote — which trains me to read it as reported. Then three passages break that
contract: a duration that contradicts the article's own dates, an invented interior monologue with
no cue that it is invented, and a totalizing claim at the emotional climax that the article's own
earlier facts refute. Each is small. Together they teach a first-time reader that the piece
sometimes stops reporting without telling me, and I do not know which sentence is next.

The comprehension gaps are mostly one-clause fixes: the Enneagram is never actually defined, the
benchmark table's most jargon-dense row is asked to carry an argument, and the single fact that
would make the Yann LeCun section click for a newcomer — that LeCun was made to report to Wang — is
missing from a paragraph that explicitly promises to declare LeCun's interest.

## What landed

**UNFAM-H1 — The opening needs zero prior knowledge and is the whole thesis.**

> "There was a math competition in New Mexico. The top four finishers in the state won an
> all-expenses-paid trip to Disney World. Alexandr Wang was in sixth grade, and he has said that
> the trip was the most motivating thing he could imagine. He came fourth."

Four sentences, no proper noun I have to look up, no jargon, and a complete emotional situation. I
did not know who this person was and I still wanted the next line. Most personality pieces open by
telling me the subject is important; this one opens by making me care first. Must survive revision:
everything downstream is a callback to it, and removing it would leave the article with no
newcomer-legible entry point.

**UNFAM-H2 — The Alex Ren / LeCun pairing does the reader's analytical work in one sentence.**

> "Both men are describing the same person accurately. Only one of them thinks it is a problem."

This is the moment the article stopped being a list of things people said about a stranger and
became something I could think with. As an outsider I have no way to adjudicate whether Wang is
qualified; this line tells me I do not have to, and reframes the entire dispute as being about
criteria rather than facts. It is also the clearest possible statement of the article's thesis in
plain English. Must survive: it is the only passage that makes the LeCun section comprehensible
without industry knowledge.

**UNFAM-H3 — The Enneagram claim is delivered functionally, not by jargon.**

> "What defines a Three is where their sense of existing comes from: the scoreboard, and the
> standing on it."

I have never studied the Enneagram and I understood this immediately, and could still restate it an
hour later. The draft resisted the obvious temptation to explain the system before applying it.
Must survive: this sentence is doing the work that a definitions paragraph would do worse.

**UNFAM-H4 — The type theory that could have lost me is fenced off and labelled.**

> "For the Enneagram nerds. Skip if you're not deep into the system. The rest of the analysis stands
> on its own."

Every term I could not have parsed — 3w2, instinctual subtype, disintegration, stress and growth
arrows — is behind that door with an explicit permission slip to skip. This is the single biggest
reason the article is readable by someone like me. Must survive exactly as structured; moving any of
that vocabulary into the body would cost the general reader the piece.

**UNFAM-H5 — Unfamiliar proper nouns are given a function, not a definition.**

> "In March 2016, AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol. 'I remember feeling like, oh wow, AI, it's happening,'
> Wang said. He left school."

I could not have told you what AlphaGo was. I did not need to, because the quote tells me what it
meant to him, which is the only thing the paragraph requires. Same technique works on Project
Thunderforge ("a military planning program worth roughly $500 million") and the Turing Award ("for
the work that made the current era possible"). Must survive: this is the house style that makes the
rest of the density survivable.

**UNFAM-H6 — The closing image explains the entire business model to a newcomer in one move.**

> "That is Scale AI. Same machine, new building. Human judgment goes in, labeled signal comes out,
> and the model gets a little better at the thing the humans were good at."

I finally understood what the man actually does for a living, in the last section, via a product I
could picture. It also retroactively clarified the Remotasks material. Must survive: it is the
payoff and the plain-English glossary at the same time.

**UNFAM-H7 — The article tells me what would prove it wrong.**

> "Two findings would overturn the call. One: a documented stretch where pressure made him go quiet
> and stay quiet. Two: a decision where he chose knowing something over being seen to have delivered
> it. Neither is in the record so far."

As someone with no way to evaluate the typing, this is the passage that made me trust the typing. An
argument that names its own kill conditions is not trying to win me. Must survive.

## What missed

**The interest declaration promises a full accounting and delivers a partial one.** The paragraph
opens "Declare the interest, because it is large," then gives me two reasons LeCun might be
motivated (he founded a competitor; he has an architectural axe to grind). It omits the one a
newcomer would find most explanatory. See UNFAM-C1.

**The benchmark table is the section where I stopped reading and started skimming.** It is
introduced as "the test, run on the man who sells them," so I approached it as the article's
evidence. One of its four rows is written in a language I do not speak, and the section's closing
turn ("Three of those four will resolve with more data") counts that row as one of the three. See
UNFAM-C2.

**The article never tells me what the Enneagram is.** It tells me what a Three is, which is most of
what I need, but "Enneagram" appears in the title, the H2, and the TL;DR before anything establishes
that it is a nine-type model of motivation. See UNFAM-C3.

**Section 3 promised one idea and delivered four.** Under "Why Alexandr Wang built a company out of
other people's expertise" I got the business model, the Remotasks labour investigation, the Pentagon
contracts, Lucy Guo's exit and ranking, and the MEI hiring philosophy. Each is interesting. The
heading only prepared me for the first. See UNFAM-C5.

**A promised effect that did not land: the closing heading.** "Fourth place out of four" is meant to
mean "the last of the four who qualified." On first encounter it read to me as "last of four
competitors" — dead last, the opposite of the article's point. See UNFAM-C6.

## Red flags

### UNFAM-R1 — "Twenty years" is contradicted by the article's own dates, in the opening and in FAQ 1 (BLOCKER)

- **Location:** Body ¶2, "Twenty years later Theo Von asked him about it." And FAQ 1, "He has spent
  the twenty years since building the machinery that decides whose expertise counts."
- **Reader effect:** The article supplies every number needed to falsify this within four
  paragraphs. Paragraph 1 says sixth grade; paragraph 2 dates the interview to February 2025;
  paragraph 3 says "He was born in January 1997." A reader born-in-1997 arithmetic gives sixth grade
  around 2008–09, which is about sixteen years before the interview, not twenty. The FAQ version is
  worse than imprecise: he has spent *ten* years building that machinery, since founding Scale in
  2016, not twenty. For a reader deciding whether to trust an article dense with dates and dollar
  figures, a self-refuting number in the hook is the cheapest possible reason to stop believing the
  expensive ones.
- **Evidence:** Draft's own text (¶1, ¶2, ¶3). Evidence packet confirms birth January 1997 (CLM-04,
  verified) and the *This Past Weekend* episode aired 2025-02-18 (S-04), and
  confirms the sixth-grade framing is Wang's own ("I remember as a sixth grader," packet line 112).
- **Minimum viable repair:** Replace both instances with a figure consistent with the article's own
  dates, or with a non-numeric construction. Body: "Sixteen years later Theo Von asked him about it."
  FAQ: "He has spent the decade since building the machinery that decides whose expertise counts."
- **Expected benefit:** Removes the only reader-checkable arithmetic error in the piece, and repairs
  an FAQ answer that can surface standalone as a search result where no surrounding context defends it.
- **Confidence:** High that the numbers are wrong. High that it is worth fixing regardless of how
  many readers catch it, because the FAQ is a standalone artifact.
- **Acceptance test:** Every duration claim in the reader-visible text can be derived from the dates
  the article itself states, with no step requiring outside knowledge. Specifically: no reader-visible
  sentence claims twenty years between the math competition and either the interview or the present.

### UNFAM-R2 — Invented interior monologue is presented with no cue distinguishing it from the sourced quotes around it (BLOCKER)

- **Location:** The `<p class="inner-thought">` paragraph in the LeCun section: "Don't do this. Not
  here. Not in front of them. Finish the sentence, get to the part about the pool house, they'll
  laugh, you can sit down."
- **Reader effect:** On an unaided first read I took this as reportage — a paraphrase of something
  he said, or something an attendee described. The article has spent two thousand words training me
  that quoted, set-off material is sourced material; every other quotation in the piece carries an
  outlet and a date, often in the same sentence. That discipline is precisely what makes this block
  read as reported. It is only afterwards, on reflection, that I realised no one could know it. The
  sourced material that follows it ("I literally teared up," he told Forbes; an attendee heard him
  ask "This is so stupid. Why am I doing this?") arrives *after* the invention, so by the time I
  learn what is actually on the record, I have already banked the invented version as fact.
- **Evidence:** Evidence packet CLM-23 independently classifies this: "The `inner-thought` paragraph
  is authored interiority with no evidentiary basis at all." A returning 9takes reader may recognise
  `inner-thought` as a house device; a reader arriving cold from search — the reader this article is
  optimised for, per its keyword set — has no way to.
- **Minimum viable repair:** One framing clause immediately before the block that marks it as
  imagined rather than observed, e.g. opening the block with "It is not hard to imagine the thought:"
  or introducing the scene first and the imagined line second. Do not delete it — see the Preserve
  list; the beat itself is the best writing in the section. Alternatively, invert the order so the
  Forbes quote and the attendee account come first and the imagined interior arrives explicitly as
  the article's reconstruction.
- **Expected benefit:** Keeps the emotional payload while removing the only passage where a
  first-time reader is likely to mistake invention for reporting about a living person.
- **Confidence:** High that it is unmarked; high that a cold reader misreads it, since I did.
- **Acceptance test:** A reader with no prior exposure to 9takes, shown only that page, can correctly
  identify which sentences in the all-hands scene are on the record and which are the author's
  reconstruction, without checking sources.

### UNFAM-R3 — The climax rests on a generalisation the article itself has already disproved (BLOCKER)

- **Location:** LeCun section, "He has made every room he has ever been in by exactly one spot.
  Ranked, seeded, cut lists, top four in the state."
- **Reader effect:** This is the sentence the entire final movement turns on, and I could refute it
  from memory of this article. The piece has told me he became the youngest self-made billionaire in
  the world at 24, that Zuckerberg called him "the most impressive founder of his generation," and
  that Meta paid $14.3 billion for 49% of his company specifically to acquire him. None of those are
  squeaking in by one spot; the $14.3 billion is the opposite of a narrow admission. Because the
  claim overreaches at exactly the moment the article asks for emotional assent, the effect is to
  make me suspicious of the reading rather than moved by it — which is a shame, because the softer
  version of the claim is well supported and genuinely affecting.
- **Evidence:** Draft's own H2 1 ("became the youngest self-made billionaire in the world at 24") and
  H2 1 ($14.3 billion for 49%). Evidence packet CLM-27 rates the sentence "unsupported as written"
  with one documented instance, and flags it as "the highest-exposure sentence in the piece for a
  hostile reader."
- **Minimum viable repair:** Scope the claim to the formative period the evidence actually covers,
  and let the reader carry it forward. The following sentence already does the real work
  ("A person who grows up on the qualifying side of a threshold learns something specific"), so the
  generalisation can shrink to the childhood record — ranked, seeded, cut lists, fourth of four —
  without losing the turn.
- **Expected benefit:** The emotional payoff survives and stops being contradicted by the article's
  own biography section. Also removes the piece's most quotable line for a hostile reader.
- **Confidence:** High. The contradiction is internal and does not require outside knowledge.
- **Acceptance test:** No sentence in the piece asserts a pattern about "every" room, admission or
  threshold that the article's own biography section contradicts. A reader can hold the claim and the
  billionaire/$14.3B facts in mind simultaneously without one refuting the other.

## Specific improvements

- **Reader effect:** The paragraph explicitly promises a full interest declaration and gives me two
  reasons, both abstract (a competing company, a decade-old architectural position). Neither explains

  Wang. It turns out the missing fact is the most human one available: Meta's reorganisation placed

  that LeCun reported to Wang after the June 2025 reorganisation, having previously reported to Chris
  Cox. The evidence packet has LeCun's November 2025 exit and his interest as a competitor

  draft's own research file does not currently supply.
- **Minimum viable repair:** One clause in the interest-declaration paragraph: that the reorganisation
  put LeCun under Wang before he left. Keep the existing "None of that makes the criticism false"
  handling, which already models the right posture and now has a third, stronger interest to absorb.
- **Expected benefit:** The section becomes comprehensible to a reader with no org-chart knowledge,
  and the article's own declare-the-interest move stops being falsifiable by a reader who searches
  LeCun's name afterwards. It also strengthens the thesis rather than weakening it: being ranked by

- **Confidence:** High that the fact is correct and material. High that a cold reader currently

- **Acceptance test:** A reader who has never heard of either man can, after that paragraph, state at
