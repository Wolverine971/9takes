## Bottom-line verdict

The draft would remain recognizable and substantially fair even if Johnson rejected the Type Four call. It uses his own language, acknowledges the experiment's limits, separates medical determination from outcome, and gives close relationships room to complicate the public caricature. Two interpretive sentences press farther into causality than the public record can establish, but neither breaks trust.

## What landed

- **SUBJECT-H1:** "He is not asking only whether a protocol works. He is asking whether a person can become the symbol that makes a new value system feel real." This preserves Johnson's stated philosophical ambition instead of reducing him to vanity or fear. It must survive because his performance-art and prototype language directly supports it.
- **SUBJECT-H2:** "His body can generate hypotheses. It cannot turn one person into a controlled trial." This is an unusually fair boundary: it neither repeats Johnson's sales frame nor dismisses his project as useless.
- **SUBJECT-H3:** The Tolo and Talmage section preserves warmth, disagreement, patience, and unresolved family distance. It makes the subject human without converting favorable testimony into total vindication.

## What missed

Reader effect: the article briefly sounds as if it knows why Blueprint exists more confidently than Johnson's testimony allows.
- **SUBJECT-C2:** The closing line "At 48, he has repeated the gesture on a planetary scale" presents the childhood-to-mission connection as settled fact. It is powerful, but the certainty exceeds the article's otherwise careful distinction between evidence and inference.

I expected the article to distinguish Johnson's stated goal from the immortal-tech-bro caricature, address the commercial conflict, and acknowledge the scientific uncertainty. It does all three. I also expected his self-description as performance art to matter, and the draft makes it central.
