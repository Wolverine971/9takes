# Perspective Review — One-Year-Later Durability (August 2027)

## Bottom-line verdict

Read from August 2027, roughly ninety percent of this article is still standing, and it is standing
for a structural reason rather than a lucky one: almost every load-bearing beat is either a dated
quote or a completed event with a year attached to it. Quotes do not decay. "On March 3, 2024,"
"On June 1, 2024," "In 2025 the fire had nothing to burn," "The hotel room where Caitlin Clark
spent the 2025 playoffs" — none of that rots. The Type 1 argument is built out of archival material
and does not depend on any event of the last three weeks. If you deleted the newest thing in the
piece, the article would still work.

The decay is real but it is concentrated, and it is concentrated almost entirely in one section.
"Caitlin Clark is still going to the gym about it" is a live mid-season snapshot published seven
weeks before the 2026 regular season ends and the Fever — first in the East as this publishes —
begin a postseason the article never once anticipates. Within two months, every number in that
section is history. Within twelve, it is two seasons stale.

That is a revise, not a hold. Nothing in the article is false today; I verified the one live number
the evidence packet left open and it checks out. What the article lacks is the small amount of
scaffolding that lets a dated snapshot age into an archive instead of into an error: past-tensing
the perishable clauses, stamping the live ones, and leaving the next editor a note and enough word
budget to act on it. The piece's own production notes record that it is a snapshot and that it has
fifteen words of headroom. It recorded the problem and shipped without solving it.

No blockers. Nine concerns, of which the first two are the only ones I would insist on.

---

## What landed

**FUTURE-H1 — the absolute date stamp that saves the most perishable paragraph.**

> "It is August 2026, and she is spending the hours again."

This is the single best durability decision in the draft and it must survive revision untouched. A
reader in August 2027 hits "It is August 2026" and immediately reframes everything after it as a
dated snapshot rather than a claim about the present. Without that clause the same paragraph would
read as a wrong assertion about today; with it, it reads as reportage. Every fix I propose below is
an extension of the instinct already present in this sentence, not a correction of it.

**FUTURE-H2 — headings that carry their own dates.**

> "## The hotel room where Caitlin Clark spent the 2025 playoffs"

A heading with a year in it never goes stale, and it survives the scan path, the table of contents,
and any future search snippet. Compare it to "## Caitlin Clark is still going to the gym about it,"
which is the one heading that will read oddly in 2027. Whoever wrote H2 8's heading understood the
problem.

**FUTURE-H3 — a sentence that anticipates its own future.**

> "Both are true, she has not solved it, and there may be no version of her career in which she
> can."

This is the rare closing move on a live controversy that cannot be embarrassed by later events. It
declines to resolve on current information and explicitly holds the door open. Most articles about
active public figures state the current state of a dispute as though it were the end of it; this one
names it as unresolved and says so in a form that stays true whether or not it ever resolves.
Preserve verbatim.

**FUTURE-H4 — past-perfect on the record claim.**

> "No player in WNBA history had ever put up 40 and 10 in the same game."

Anchored to July 17, 2026 by the preceding sentence and phrased in the past perfect, so it remains
literally true even after somebody else does it. This is the correct grammatical form for a record
in an evergreen page, and the article uses it here and then abandons it three paragraphs later (see
FUTURE-C7).

**FUTURE-H5 — the archival spine.**

The thesis rests on the June 2026 self-rating, the 2026 obsession quote, the December 2024 TIME
material, Bluder's January 2026 Gazette line, and the February 2025 perfectionist quote. Every one
is a fixed utterance at a fixed date. The article's central argument is therefore effectively
timeless, and the 2026 season material functions as illustration rather than as load. That is the
right architecture for this subject and it is why my verdict is revise rather than anything harsher.

## What missed

The article treats its final section as a conclusion when it is functionally an appendix with a
seven-week fuse. Everything else in the piece is written to last; that section is written to be
current, and nothing in the surrounding text signals to a future reader — or a future editor — that
the two have different half-lives.

The second miss is quieter. The draft's own FORMULA FINGERPRINT LEDGER contains a field called
"Current-tense anchor: August 2026," and the SECOND PASS NOTES contain the line "Word count is 4485
against a 4500 ceiling. No headroom for the next refresh; something has to go before anything is
added." The pipeline identified the perishability and identified the constraint that will block the
fix, and then produced no refresh instruction, no as-of convention, and no reserved word budget. The
diagnosis exists in the file. The remediation does not.

## What I expected

From a one-year standpoint, four things are reasonable to expect from a piece about an athlete whose
season is in progress at publication:

1. An "as of" convention on every number that moves — season averages, team record, league ranks,
   injury status.
2. Some acknowledgment that the season is unfinished. The article states the Fever are 19-11 and
   draws no line at all toward the postseason that starts seven weeks later. A reader in 2027 will
   notice the omission immediately, because they will know how it ended.
3. Awareness of the scheduled change points that are already on the calendar — not predictions, but
   known milestones (the September 24 season end, the September 27 playoff start, her contract
   running through 2027, restricted free agency and supermax eligibility after it).
4. A refresh note left for the next editor, in the production pretext where the pipeline already
   keeps `requires` and `blockers` arrays.

The article delivers a partial version of (1) — the "It is August 2026" stamp — and none of (2),
(3), or (4).

## What surprised me

**Welcome.** The heading architecture is unusually durable for an SEO-driven personality piece.
Seven of nine H2s are anchored to completed events (childhood, the two title games, the turnover
record, the race fight, the smile assignment, the 2025 playoffs). The search spine and the stat
spine decay at completely different rates, which means the page keeps its search value even when its
numbers go stale. That is not typical and it is worth naming as a pattern to repeat.

**Jarring.** The commercial-seam paragraph is written as a static, permanent tension — "She also
keeps the check" — at exactly the moment her compensation is being restructured. A new CBA produced
a substantial raise for her in 2026, and she becomes eligible for the league's new maximum in 2027
and a restricted free agent after that season. The paragraph's argument survives all of this; its
factual furniture does not. The article is holding open the right question while standing on numbers
that are mid-transformation.

**Also jarring, structurally.** A piece that structurally requires an update within seven weeks
shipped with fifteen words of headroom. That is not a content problem, it is a maintenance problem,
and it will be discovered by whoever tries to make the October edit under a 4,500-word fail gate.

## Specific improvements

### FUTURE-C1 — The final section is a live snapshot published seven weeks before the season ends, and never acknowledges the postseason (highest priority)

**Passage.** H2 9, "Caitlin Clark is still going to the gym about it," entire opening paragraph:
"It is August 2026, and she is spending the hours again. She is averaging 21.5 points and 8.0
assists, third in the league in scoring and second in assists. The Fever are 19-11. She just took
Eastern Conference Player of the Week…"

**Reader effect.** A reader arriving in October 2026 — let alone August 2027 — reads a present-tense
account of a season that has finished, from an article that gives no sign of knowing a postseason
was imminent. The 2026 WNBA regular season ends September 24 and the playoffs begin September 27.
The Fever are described as 19-11 and, per the team's own release, first in the Eastern Conference.
The article puts a contending team into the present tense and then stops, seven weeks short.

**Evidence.** WNBA 2026 key dates (regular season ends Sept 24, playoffs tip Sept 27); Indiana Fever
release, 2026-08-04, confirming 19-11 and first in the East (packet S-47).

**Minimum viable repair.** Two changes, no new research. First, past-tense the perishable clauses and
let the existing date stamp govern them: "In August 2026 she was averaging 21.5 points and 8.0
assists… The Fever were 19-11 and leading the Eastern Conference." Second, add one clause
acknowledging the unfinished season — something to the effect that the season still had two months
and a postseason left in it. That single clause converts the section from "stale" to "a snapshot
taken deliberately at a known point," and it costs about twelve words.

**Expected benefit.** The section ages into archive rather than into error, and the piece stops
looking unaware of the most obvious upcoming event in its subject's life.

**Confidence.** High.

**Acceptance test.** Read H2 9 aloud imagining it is August 2027 and the 2026 postseason has
happened. If any sentence asserts something about "now" that a 2027 reader knows to be false, it has
not been fixed. Every number in the section must sit behind either a past-tense verb or an explicit
date.

### FUTURE-C3 — An unresolved injury stated in the open-ended present continuous

**Passage.** H2 9: "…while appearing on the injury report with a back that keeps threatening to sit
her down."

**Reader effect.** "Keeps threatening" is a status with no resolution and no end date, describing a
medical situation that will certainly have resolved one way or another long before a 2027 reader
arrives. If the back cost her the rest of 2026, the sentence reads as an article that stopped
watching mid-story; if it healed, it reads as a false alarm preserved in amber. This is the exact
failure mode of a temporary status written as an ongoing one.

**Evidence.** Packet CLM-29 records the back issue as verified for the first week of August 2026
only; the packet's own framing notes these claims "are still moving."

**Minimum viable repair.** Bind it to the date and close the grammar: "…while appearing on the
August injury report with a back problem." Drop "keeps threatening to sit her down," which is the
clause that makes an open-ended promise the article cannot keep.

**Expected benefit.** The detail survives — it is a good detail, and it earns its place by showing
her playing through — without committing the article to a story it does not follow.

**Confidence.** High.

**Acceptance test.** No sentence in the article describes an injury, dispute, or status in a
continuous present tense without a date bounding it.

### FUTURE-C4 — "Third in the league in scoring" is accurate today by one tenth of a point

**Passage.** H2 9: "third in the league in scoring and second in assists."

**Reader effect.** Verified correct as of 2026-08-05, so no trust problem at publication. But third
place is held over Breanna Stewart by 0.1 points per game (21.5 to 21.4) with Kahleah Copper at
21.2 behind her. That is a claim whose half-life is one game, stated in the present tense in a page
intended to live for years. The assists rank (second, behind Alyssa Thomas) is comparatively safe
but has the same form.

**Evidence.** StatMuse 2026 WNBA PPG leaders, checked 2026-08-05: Wilson 26.6, Mitchell 24.0, Clark
21.5, Stewart 21.4, Copper 21.2. Assists rank confirmed by packet CLM-29.

**Minimum viable repair.** Either bind it to the date along with the rest of the paragraph under C1's
fix, or — better, and cheaper in words — replace the ranks with the durable version of the same
point: "third in the league in scoring and second in assists" becomes "top three in scoring, top two
in assists," which survives a 0.1 swing and says the same thing to a reader.

**Expected benefit.** Removes the article's most fragile single number at no rhetorical cost.

**Confidence.** High. The rank is right; the exposure is the margin.

**Acceptance test.** No league-relative rank appears in the article without either a date binding it
or a tolerance that absorbs a one-game change.

### FUTURE-C5 — Relative "now" on a status-sensitive employment fact

**Passage.** H2 7: "Stephanie White, who now coaches her in Indiana, has landed on the same
prescription Iowa did."

**Reader effect.** "Now" is unanchored, and head-coaching tenure is among the least stable facts in
professional sport. If White is not the Fever coach in 2027, the sentence is simply wrong, and it is
wrong in a place where the article is establishing the credibility of a key witness — the second of
the two coaches whose independent use of "perfectionist" is load-bearing for the whole typing.

**Evidence.** Packet S-23 dates White's quote to 2025 in the context of Clark's return from a groin
injury. The article's argument needs only that White coached her, not that she does now.

**Minimum viable repair.** Replace "who now coaches her in Indiana" with "who took over as her head
coach in Indiana in 2025." Same length, no expiry.

**Expected benefit.** The two-coach corroboration — the strongest external evidence in the piece —
stops depending on a fact that can change in an offseason.

**Confidence.** High.

**Acceptance test.** The word "now" does not appear anywhere in the body attached to a role, title,
status, or ranking.

### FUTURE-C6 — An unanchored rolling duration in the cold open

**Passage.** Cold open: "By then she had been Rookie of the Year on 66 of 67 first-place ballots,
and had spent two years as the most-watched athlete in her sport."

**Reader effect.** "Two years" is counted forward from an unstated origin, so a reader cannot tell
which window is claimed, and the number is arithmetically obliged to change. By 2027 the honest
figure is three, and the article still says two. The packet separately flags the superlative as
unsourced and notes that a 13-game 2025 season complicates it (CLM-30) — a sourcing question outside
my lane, but it compounds the temporal one, because an unsourced claim that also drifts has nothing
holding it in place.

**Evidence.** Packet CLM-30, status unresolved.

**Minimum viable repair.** Bind it to the completed period the sentence is actually about: "…and had
spent her first two seasons as the most-watched athlete in her sport." "First two seasons" is fixed
forever; "two years" is not.

**Expected benefit.** The opening credential stops aging, and the phrasing incidentally sidesteps the
13-game-season objection by naming seasons rather than elapsed time.

**Confidence.** Medium-high on the temporal fix; I take no position on whether the superlative
itself is defensible, which is the packet's open question, not mine.

**Acceptance test.** No duration in the article is counted forward from an implicit present.

### FUTURE-C7 — Breakable records and league-relative superlatives in the timeless present

**Passages.** Three instances, one repair pattern:
- Box-score table: "Fastest to 200 career threes in league history, 74 games"
- H2 3: "which Nielsen recorded as the most-watched basketball game at any level, men's or women's,
  since 2019"
- TL;DR: "The most exacting player in the league is also the most error-prone"

**Reader effect.** Each states in the present a fact that a future event can falsify. The third is
the sharpest: "the most error-prone" is a present-tense character description derived from a 2024
season record, and a 2027 reader will read it as a claim about her current play, which may be flatly
untrue — the article's own argument is that she has improved substantially since. The first is
breakable by any future rookie. The second is breakable by any sufficiently watched game.

**Evidence.** Packet CLM-23, CLM-10, CLM-24. Note that the article already demonstrates the correct
form elsewhere: "No player in WNBA history *had ever* put up 40 and 10" (FUTURE-H4).

**Minimum viable repair.** Apply the H4 pattern. "Fastest to 200 career threes in league history"
→ "the fastest anyone had reached 200 career threes." "The most exacting player in the league is
also the most error-prone" → "the most exacting player in the league also set its single-season
turnover record," which is permanently true and actually sharper. The Nielsen line is the least
urgent; it is already attributed to a 2024 measurement.

**Expected benefit.** Records become historical statements rather than standing claims, and the
TL;DR stops asserting something about her present play that the article itself later contradicts.

**Confidence.** High on the TL;DR instance, medium on the other two.

**Acceptance test.** Every record or superlative in the article is either bound to the date it was
set or phrased so that a later event cannot falsify it.

### FUTURE-C8 — The commercial seam stands on compensation facts that are mid-transformation

**Passage.** H2 6: "Clark is one of the highest-earning athletes in women's sports, fluent inside
commercial machinery that runs partly on the very rivalry she is calling fabricated… She also keeps
the check."

**Reader effect.** The argument is durable and I would not touch it — this is the article's most
future-proofed idea (FUTURE-H3). But its factual footing is moving fast. The league's new collective
bargaining agreement produced a substantial 2026 raise for her; the Fever exercised her fourth-year
option in April 2026, contracting her through 2027; she becomes eligible for the league's new
maximum in 2027, a year earlier than previously expected under the new CBA; and she becomes a
restricted free agent after the 2027 season. Within this review's twelve-month window, "keeps the
check" is describing a materially different check.

**Evidence.** WNBA/Fever contract reporting (option exercised April 2026, contracted through 2027,
restricted free agency and supermax eligibility thereafter); new CBA raise reporting, 2026. Also
relevant: the Rabbit Hole's "from the CBA to expansion" reference now describes a settled agreement
rather than a live negotiation.

**Minimum viable repair.** Keep the paragraph and add nothing; simply avoid pinning it to a number.
It currently does not cite a figure, which is why this is a concern and not a defect — the exposure
is that a future editor will be tempted to add one. Record it instead as a refresh trigger: revisit
this paragraph after the 2027 season, when free agency and maximum eligibility land.

**Expected benefit.** The best paragraph in the article stays the best paragraph, and the next editor
knows when to look at it rather than discovering it late.

**Confidence.** Medium-high.

**Acceptance test.** The commercial-seam paragraph contains no salary figure or earnings rank, and
the refresh list names the post-2027 free-agency window.

### FUTURE-C9 — A forward-looking prediction in the closing (lowest priority)

**Passage.** H2 9: "Ask her again where she falls out of ten and the number is not going to move

**Reader effect.** A prediction is the most perishable sentence form available, and a 2027 reader may

**Evidence.** None required; this is a structural observation about the sentence's form.

**Minimum viable repair.** Optional, and I would accept leaving it. If touched, shift it from
forecast to disposition: "Ask her again and the answer will come from the same arithmetic," which
claims something about how she computes rather than about what she will say.

**Confidence.** Low, and deliberately so. I am recording it because a prediction in an evergreen page
is a real temporal exposure, not because I think this one is likely to embarrass the piece. Treat it
