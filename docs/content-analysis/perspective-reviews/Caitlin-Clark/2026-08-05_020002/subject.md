## Bottom-line verdict

This article treats Clark with more care than most writing about her, and on the two topics where
it could have done real damage — the racialized fight around her fame, and her commercial position
inside it — it behaves better than the coverage she has publicly objected to. It criticizes her
without erasing her. It refuses to convict her of hypocrisy and refuses to exonerate her. The
turnover argument dignifies the single most embarrassing number in her record without excusing it.
She could read most of this and recognize herself.

But the piece has one systematic representational fault, and it is the kind a subject notices
immediately because it happens to her constantly: **every time Clark pairs self-criticism with
self-assertion in a single breath, this draft keeps the criticism and drops the assertion.** It
happens four separate times, in four different sources, always in the same direction:

| Where | What she said | What the draft keeps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ESPN 2026, the obsession quote | "…because I want to be the best player in the world and I want to help this team win" | Cut, unmarked, mid-quote |
| ESPN 2026, the six | "We'll have to see" — she declines to cap herself at six | Absent |
| TIME 2024, the Olympic snub | She knew she was good enough; the snub "woke a monster"; a "blessing" that "will definitely motivate me my entire career" | Only "I gave them a lot of reasons to keep me off the team" |
| ESPN 2026, her fire | "The fire and the passion, that's what makes me, me"; "nobody should ever try to take that away from me" | Only "I can always say allow myself grace, but…" |

Individually each is arguable compression. Together they manufacture a more self-flagellating
person than the record contains, and they do it in the direction the thesis needs. The article's
own subject went on national television three weeks before this draft's publication date to say
"don't make accusations about my character and who I am when you don't know me" — and the draft
quotes that line while performing a quieter version of the thing she is describing.

The fix is unusually cheap: in every one of the four cases, the restoring material is already in the
evidence packet or one fetch away, and in three of the four it makes the Type 1 read *harder-won
and more credible*, not weaker. A One who believes she is good enough and is still auditing is a
more interesting and more defensible claim than a One who only ever indicts herself.

Trust is strained, not broken. Nothing here is defamatory, nothing alleges misconduct she did not
commit, and the hardest topic in her life is handled with real integrity. Revise.

## What landed

**SUBJ-H1 — The turnover resolution.** H2 5:

> "Ones are not organized around avoiding mistakes. They are organized around doing the thing
> correctly, and Clark grades correctness at the moment of decision. Whether the ball arrives is a
> separate question, settled a half-second later by four other people. If the skip pass is the
> right pass, it is the right pass on the possession it gets deflected and on the possession it
> gets a layup."

This is the single most respectful thing in the article. 223 turnovers is the number every hostile
account of her leads with, and the draft neither hides it (it opens the section with a `key-stat`
block and the phrase "removed it from the conversation by 86") nor uses it to convict her of
sloppiness. It offers a reading in which the number is a cost of a defensible standard. It must
survive revision because it is the only place in the corpus of writing about Clark where her worst
statistic is taken seriously as information rather than as ammunition.

**SUBJ-H2 — The character/output filter.** H2 6:

> "'You are not dominating' is a claim about output, and output is a number she is already grading
> more harshly than Swoopes ever could, so it bounces: the internal auditor got there first with a
> worse verdict. 'You are a bad person' cannot be handled that way, because the audit is the moral
> instrument… An accusation about her character says the ledger is a performance, and there is no
> gym she can go to about that."

Built entirely from her own words and her own documented behavior — she has never litigated the
basketball criticism and did litigate the character criticism, on camera, twice. It explains
something she has said out loud that she finds hard to explain ("I think there's just like a false
characterization of who I am sometimes"). This is the beat she would most plausibly recognize.
Preserve verbatim.

**SUBJ-H3 — Holding the commercial seam open.** H2 6:

> "She says the credit belongs to the Black women who built the place. She also keeps the check.
> Both are true, she has not solved it, and there may be no version of her career in which she can."

The fairest available handling of the most weaponizable fact about her. It does not accuse her of
bad faith and does not launder the tension away. Naming an unresolved thing as unresolved, and
locating the irresolvability partly outside her control, is exactly what a subject needs from a
writer on this topic.

**SUBJ-H4 — Separating her from what is done in her name.** H2 6: "The hardest test of any read on
Clark is not her game. It is the war being fought in her name," followed by "What happened around
her instead she had no standing to argue with at all." The draft quotes her disavowal and her
privilege statement in full. It never assigns her responsibility for her fanbase's conduct, and it
never uses her as a prop for either side. Given that this is the axis on which she is most often
misrepresented, this section is the strongest evidence that the writer was trying to be fair.

**SUBJ-H5 — Criticism explained without being excused.** H2 7: "The reframe changes what the
behavior means without changing what it cost." This sentence is the whole difference between an
analysis and an apologia, and it appears immediately after the section's most sympathetic move.
Preserve it; it is the article's license to be sympathetic elsewhere.

**SUBJ-H6 — Conceding the critic.** H2 6 grants that Swoopes was partly right and specifies what
about ("The claim was about the timeline, and on the timeline she was right"), and states plainly
that "Clark's rookie efficiency was ordinary by superstar standards." A piece that will say that
has earned the right to say the generous things.

## What missed

**The fire is only ever a problem.** H2 7 is titled "Why Caitlin Clark looks angry when she plays"
and moves: entitlement read → a psychologist assigning her a facial expression → Bluder's three
targets of anger → the practice technical → White prescribing grace → Clark unable to take the
advice. Every quote in the section positions her as failing to fix a defect. In the same July 2026
ESPN interview the draft mines for its character quote, she says the opposite about the same trait:
"The fire and the passion, that's what makes me, me," and "when I step in between the lines, I love
it… This was always my dream. And nobody should ever try to take that away from me." The section's
promised effect — you have been reading this face wrong — half-lands, because it replaces one
external verdict (entitlement) with another external verdict (a manageable pathology) and never
lets her hold the trait as hers. See SUBJ-C2.

**The Olympic beat is the draft's named tiebreaker and it is the draft's most incomplete quote.**
H2 3's closing paragraph is doing the heaviest lifting in the entire typing argument. It is built
on half of what she said. See SUBJ-R2.

**The childhood section explains a person the sources don't quite describe.** H2 2 glosses the
Clark house as one "where keeping pace was the price of admission" and concludes "there is a
correct amount of effort, everyone else is applying less of it, and the gap is yours to close." The
only evidence offered is her mother's "they were just awful to her." Her own account of that
household is reciprocal and affectionate, and in the most-reported physical incident from it she
was the aggressor. See SUBJ-C4.

## What I expected

- **Her own words about her competitive fire, in the section about her competitive fire.** The
  draft has her on the record about grace, about being "at times maybe difficult" to coach, and
  about people not understanding "how I was wired" — all in the register of a problem. The
  self-claiming register exists, is recent, and is from a source already cited.
- **The other half of the Olympic answer.** A subject who says "I gave them a lot of reasons to keep
  me off the team" *and* "it woke a monster" is not being modest; she is doing something more
  interesting, and the draft only reports the first half.
- **A marker on the invented interior monologue.** Two passages are written in her first person
  about her private thoughts on specific dated nights. See SUBJ-C5.
- **Sourcing proportional to specificity in the practice scene.** The most vivid paragraph about her
  conduct toward teammates is the least sourced paragraph in the article. See SUBJ-R3.
- **Her coaches' actual register.** Both are quoted warmly elsewhere in the public record; the draft
  tells the reader what they meant, and what it says they meant is contradicted by their own
  sentences. See SUBJ-C1.

## What surprised me

- **Welcome:** the article does not make her a victim of her fanbase or a villain of it. Most
  writing on this subject picks one. Holding "she disavowed it" and "she still profits from the
  attention it generated" in the same paragraph, without resolving toward either, is rarer than it
  should be.
- **Welcome:** "That is not grace." The draft explicitly refuses to flatter her for the Olympic
  concession, which is the read most fan-facing coverage takes. The refusal is well-motivated even
  though the underlying quote is incomplete.
- **Jarring:** "Threes do not get called for a T by their own coach at a closed practice. Ones do."
  (H2 1.) This is presented as evidence and is not evidence about anything. It is a claim about the
  Enneagram asserted as a claim about basketball, resting on a scene whose particulars are unsourced.
- **Jarring:** the article quotes one sentence of hers twice, in two different sections, and cuts it
  at the same comma both times — immediately before the clause where she names an outcome goal. See
  SUBJ-R1.

## Red flags

### SUBJ-R1 — BLOCKER — The load-bearing quote is silently cut at the clause that contradicts the argument built on it

**Passage.** H2 1 block quote (draft lines 135–136) and its unpacking (line 138):

> "I'm obsessed with the work. And then you get in the game and then you're competitive with it
> because you've been so obsessed with the time that you've put in. I don't think it's like an
> obsession with winning…"

followed by: "She is sitting in front of a camera being invited to say she is obsessed with winning,
which is the safe and flattering answer. She corrects the premise instead. A player driven by
winning can be paid off by a scoreboard. A player driven by hours cannot be paid off at all."

**Evidence.** The packet's full transcript text (S-03, evidence packet "On what she is obsessed
with") contains a sentence between the second and third sentences quoted above: *"I come in here
every single day and I want to put in the work and get everything done **because I want to be the
best player in the world and I want to help this team win.**"* The draft excises it with no ellipsis
and no marker.

**Why this is the strongest finding.** The excised clause states an outcome goal ("the best player
in the world") and a team-result goal ("help this team win") — the two things the surrounding
paragraph asserts she declined to say. And the draft *does* use that sentence: the Rabbit Hole
subtype section (line 333) quotes "I come in here every single day and I want to put in the work and
get everything done" and stops at the identical comma. The same sentence is quoted twice and
truncated at the same point both times, both times removing the outcome language. That pattern is
what makes this a quotation-fidelity problem rather than ordinary compression.

**Reader/trust effect.** A reader who later finds the full quote concludes the article edited her to
win an argument. The subject concludes she was made to say the opposite of what she said.

**Minimum viable repair.** Restore the sentence inside the block quote, then absorb it — one clause
is enough: she names the outcome goal and still locates the engine in the hours. That is a *better*
version of the same argument, because it survives the disconfirming clause instead of deleting it.
Fund the words from the unsourced superlative at line 103 (see SUBJ-C6 repair) and from "Threes do
not get called for a T by their own coach at a closed practice. Ones do." (line 140), which is not
evidence.

**Expected benefit.** Removes the article's only true quote-distortion exposure and strengthens the
Three-vs-One argument by showing it holding under pressure.

**Confidence.** High. The full text is in the shared packet; the double-truncation is verifiable
inside the draft itself.

**Acceptance test.** Paste the draft's block quote and the packet's full transcript passage
side by side. Every word between the first and last words of the draft's quotation appears in the
draft, or is marked with an ellipsis. Separately: the two renderings of "I come in here every single
day…" (H2 1 and Rabbit Hole) no longer both terminate immediately before "because I want to be the
best player in the world."

### SUBJ-R2 — BLOCKER — The Olympic paragraph presents concession-only and erases her self-belief

**Passage.** H2 3, lines 190 and 195: "Her own position, given to TIME later that year, was that the
people who cut her had a point," and then: "Clark argued the committee's case for them, in public,
and specified the evidence against herself. That is not grace. She had already run the numbers and
reached the same verdict, and **she has no mechanism for pretending otherwise.**"

**Evidence.** TIME, December 10, 2024, fetched directly for this review. In the same interview:
TIME states "She knew she was good enough to be on the team. And she had heard rumblings that she
would be." Clark called the omission a **"blessing"**, said the snub **"woke a monster"**, named the
2028 team "a huge, huge goal," and said being left off Paris "will definitely motivate me my entire
career."

**Reader/trust effect.** The draft's sentence "she has no mechanism for pretending otherwise" is a
totalizing psychological claim — it says she is constitutionally incapable of self-defense — and the
same interview shows her defending herself in the next breath. She did not agree with the verdict;
she granted the roster arithmetic while maintaining her own merit and converting the outcome into
fuel. The draft's version is not a softer reading of her; it is a different person.

**Load-bearing status.** The draft's own pre-write memo names this as the tiebreaker: "her public
agreement with the Olympic selection committee's case against her… A Three protects the visible
result and manages the room; Clark disclaimed the result, spent the room down, and then argued the
case for the people who cut her." The decisive evidence for the typing is a half-quoted position.

**Minimum viable repair.** Add one sentence before "An Eight treats that as a slight": she also told
TIME she believed she was good enough, and called the snub a blessing that woke a monster. Then
adjust the conclusion from *she cannot pretend otherwise* to something the record supports: she
conceded the committee's arithmetic without conceding her own merit — the audit and the self-belief
run simultaneously. Cut "she has no mechanism for pretending otherwise."

**Expected benefit.** Converts a fairness liability into the article's most precise observation about
her: she is capable of holding a verdict against herself and a high estimate of herself at the same
time, which is a far more specific portrait than pure self-indictment.

**Confidence.** High. Verified directly against the primary source this session.

**Acceptance test.** A reader of the revised H2 3 can state both halves of her TIME position from
the paragraph alone: that she agreed with the roster math, and that she believed she should have
been on the team. No sentence in the section asserts she is incapable of defending herself.

### SUBJ-R3 — BLOCKER — The scout-team scene is specified far beyond its sourcing, and depicts her mistreating identifiable low-status teammates

**Passage.** H2 7, lines 291–293:

> "Iowa ran its starters against a scout team in practice, the blue squad, and Clark got into it
> with them. These were the reserves and walk-ons whose entire job that week was to imitate somebody
> else's offense, and she went at them hard enough that Bluder blew the whistle, called a technical
> foul on her own All-American in the middle of a drill, and sent her off the floor.
> She was back in for the next drill."

**Evidence.** Per the packet (CLM-05, S-17/S-21/S-22), the only located account is Jan Jensen on
*Talkin' Hawks with The VandeBergs*: Bluder "kicked her out of practice and gave her a technical and
those things" — plural and habitual. Bluder separately describes practice technicals as a recurring
management tool. **No located source contains** the scout team, the "blue squad," reserves and
walk-ons, the week's assignment, a mid-drill whistle, or a return for the next drill. The packet
also notes the draft's SECOND PASS rewrite moved *away* from the one named source, which says she
was ejected, to "back in for the next drill." Clark's own only located comment on the practice
technicals is "That hasn't happened in a while."

**Reader/trust effect.** This is the article's most vivid depiction of her behaving badly, and the
people she is depicted behaving badly toward — walk-ons whose job was to be a practice dummy — are
the most sympathetic possible counterparties. Invented specificity in a scene of misconduct is the
exact fairness failure a subject would name. It is also referenced twice more (H2 1 line 140, Rabbit
Hole line 327), so the unsourced version propagates.

**Minimum viable repair.** Two options, both cheap. (a) De-specify to what Jensen and Bluder support:
Bluder called technical fouls on Clark in practice, more than once, when her emotions ran past what
the room could carry, and on at least one occasion sent her off the floor — attributed inline to
Jensen. Drop the scout team, the walk-ons, and the next-drill return. (b) Source it: listen to the
VandeBergs episode and keep only the details Jensen actually gives. Do not keep both the ejection
and the immediate return unless a source carries both — they currently contradict the one named
account.

**Expected benefit.** Removes the article's only unverifiable allegation about her conduct toward
teammates while keeping the beat's function intact (the fire cost the room something real, and her
own coach had a tool for it).

**Confidence.** High on the sourcing gap; the packet's compiler could not reach either underlying
source and neither could this review (both refuse direct fetch).

**Acceptance test.** Every concrete noun in the revised paragraph — who she went at, what drill,
what happened after — traces to a named source in the packet's ledger, or is gone. The H2 1 and
Rabbit Hole references describe the same event as the H2 7 version.

### SUBJ-C1 — CONCERN — "Neither of them means it as a compliment" attributes an intent both coaches' quotes contradict

**Passage.** TL;DR bullet 4 (line 118): "Lisa Bluder at Iowa and Stephanie White in Indiana,
separately, both land on 'perfectionist.' Neither of them means it as a compliment."

**Evidence.** White (S-23): "She's a perfectionist **as most elite players are** and she needs to be
able to give herself grace in those moments" — explicitly normalizing and sympathetic, said while
Clark was returning from injury. White has separately said "we are very much alike in terms of our…
perfectionism." Bluder's 2026 Gazette material is affectionate: "I'm amazed at how she handled it.
She didn't get a big head. She was still a goofy kid instead of becoming a prima donna."

**Reader/trust effect.** The TL;DR is the highest-read block in the piece and it tells the reader
that two people who know her well were criticizing her. Recasting warm testimony as indictment
damages her twice: it makes her sound harder to be around than her coaches say, and it puts words in
the mouths of two named third parties.

**Minimum viable repair.** Replace with what is true and still interesting: both reached for the same
word without prompting, four years and one league apart, and both followed it with the same
prescription. The convergence is the finding; the hostility is not.

**Expected benefit.** Keeps the bullet's force, removes a misattribution of intent to two named
people. Also saves words.

**Confidence.** High.

**Acceptance test.** No sentence in the draft characterizes Bluder's or White's attitude toward Clark
in terms their own quoted sentences do not support.

### SUBJ-C2 — CONCERN — Her own defense of her competitive fire is absent from the section about her competitive fire

**Passage.** All of H2 7, and specifically its closing (line 302), which leaves her at: "I can always
say allow myself grace, but you always have that fire and that hunger inside of you… and then you
get irritated when it doesn't go your way."

**Evidence.** ESPN / Malika Andrews, July 2026 — the same interview the draft quotes at line 269:
"The fire and the passion, that's what makes me, me," and "when I step in between the lines, I love
it. And that's what's so fun about it for me is I get to compete. This was always my dream. And
nobody should ever try to take that away from me."

**Reader/trust effect.** The section's argument is that the reader is misreading her face. It
delivers that by substituting a clinical frame (a defect that required professional intervention and
still requires "grace") for a hostile one (entitlement). Her own frame — this is joy, it is the
thing I am, and it is not up for confiscation — is the one first-person account of the trait that
exists, and it is missing from the only section about the trait.

**Minimum viable repair.** Close H2 7 on the "makes me, me" line instead of, or immediately after,
the grace quote. One sentence. It also improves the section's logic: the reason she cannot take
White's advice is not that she is failing at it, it is that she does not fully accept the premise.

**Expected benefit.** Restores her agency over her own defining trait and gives the section an ending
that is hers rather than her coaches'.

**Confidence.** High — quote verified this session via two independent write-ups of the interview.

**Acceptance test.** H2 7 contains at least one first-person Clark quote in which she claims the fire
as hers rather than describing it as something she is managing or failing to manage.

### SUBJ-C3 — CONCERN — "She did not realize it happened" overstates her own account of the Maravich record

**Passage.** H2 3, line 177: "It arrived on a free throw at the end of the second quarter, and she
said afterward that she had not realized it happened. The biggest number in the sport went by and
she did not feel it, because a career scoring total is an accounting somebody else keeps."

**Evidence.** Packet CLM-08 / S-31. Asked whether she was aware of the record *when she stepped to
the line*: "Not really. When they announced it and everybody screamed, that's when I knew." She was
not tracking it at the line; she knew immediately when it happened. Separately, the free throws came
from a dead-ball technical on Ohio State's Cotie McMahon with 0.3 seconds left — the draft's "arrived
on a free throw" omits the mechanism.

**Reader/trust effect.** Small in isolation, but it is the fourth instance of the same directional
edit: her stated experience is adjusted toward greater detachment from achievement than she
described. It also asserts an interior state ("she did not feel it") she did not report.

**Minimum viable repair.** Use her actual answer, which serves the thesis at least as well: she was
not tracking it at the line, and only knew when the building told her. Drop "she did not feel it."

**Expected benefit.** A verbatim detail that is more specific and more evocative than the paraphrase
that replaced it, at no cost to the argument.

**Confidence.** High.

**Acceptance test.** The sentence describes what she was aware of *at the free-throw line*, not what
she felt about the record afterward.

### SUBJ-C4 — CONCERN — The childhood origin rests on the single harshest available family fragment

**Passage.** H2 2, lines 155–162: "a house where keeping pace was the price of admission," the
water-balloon quote, and "The lesson available in a house like that is not 'you are loved when you
win.' It is quieter and more durable: there is a correct amount of effort, everyone else is applying
less of it, and the gap is yours to close."

**Evidence.** Packet CLM-32 / S-25 records brother Blake's account of the same episode as protective:
in hot weather he had her wear the snow gear *so she would not feel* the balloons, and she wanted to
play with the brothers anyway. Clark's own framing, located this session, is reciprocal and warm:
"They're two of my biggest supporters. And probably two of my biggest haters, too… They humbled me
all the time," and on her own competitiveness, "No matter what I did… I wanted to win. I feel like
that was always my mindset." Brother Colin's most-reported memory of her competitiveness has her as
the aggressor: a loose ball in the basement, "Split my head open, got four staples."

**Reader/trust effect.** The section reads as a wound-origin story assembled from the one quote that
supports a wound. Her family is characterized on the strength of a single line by her mother whose
other participant tells it as care, and Clark is cast as the object of a household dynamic in which
she was, by every other account including her own, a full and often escalating participant. A
subject sees her family made into a mechanism.

**Minimum viable repair.** Add Blake's framing in a clause, or replace the mother's line with her own
"biggest supporters… biggest haters… they humbled me all the time," which carries the same formative
force and is hers. Soften "the price of admission" to something the sources support: she was the
smallest person in a competition nobody was staging for her benefit and she entered it anyway.

**Expected benefit.** Same origin, better sourced, and it stops implying mistreatment that the family
does not describe.

**Confidence.** Medium-high. Blake's counter-account reaches the packet through an aggregator and is
flagged there as unverified; Clark's own quotes are the safer restoration.

**Acceptance test.** H2 2 contains at least one first-person or sibling account of the household, and
no sentence characterizes the household on the strength of the mother's quote alone.

### SUBJ-C5 — CONCERN — Invented first-person interior monologue carries no reader-visible marker

**Passage.** Two `inner-thought` blocks. H2 4, line 218: "Forty-five, and it still came down to me at
the line with seventeen seconds left… I will find them tomorrow." H2 8, line 310: "Every hour I put
in this year bought me nothing. I did the work. I did the work and I am up here."

**Evidence.** Packet CLM-38 records both as explicitly authorial invention with no sourcing claimed
or possible. For the Seattle block specifically, CLM-23 and the Three-discriminator table note that
**no located source has Clark commenting on the Seattle margin at all** — the article invents her
dissatisfaction with a win and then treats it as the section's payoff and as a Three-vs-One
discriminator ("She followed the greatest scoring night in league history by finding a three-point
margin to be unhappy about," Rabbit Hole line 347).

**Reader/trust effect.** These are written in her voice, in the present tense, about her private
thoughts on two specific dated nights. A reader has no way to distinguish them from reported
interiority. The hotel-room block is closer to safe — she described that room, that night, and that
feeling in her own words, so the invention is elaboration. The Seattle block invents both the
occasion and the emotion. The subject just told a national audience "don't make accusations about
my character and who I am when you don't know me"; unmarked ventriloquism is the most literal
version of that complaint available in this format.

**Minimum viable repair.** Two parts. (a) Ensure the rendered `inner-thought` treatment carries a
visible cue that this is the writer imagining, not Clark speaking — a label, a lead-in phrase, or a
one-time note at first use. (b) In the Rabbit Hole, stop citing the invented Seattle reaction as
evidence against Type Three; an authored interior monologue cannot discriminate between types.
Rephrase to what is observable: the record night arrived inside a three-point win over a 6–21 team,
and the article's claim is about where her attention would go, stated as the writer's inference.

**Escalation rule for the editor:** if the rendered `inner-thought` class is visually
indistinguishable from a pull quote in production, this becomes a blocker rather than a concern.
Worth one look at the live component before publishing.

**Expected benefit.** Keeps the device — it is one of the piece's better textures — while making the
authorship legible and removing an invented fact from the evidentiary chain.

**Confidence.** High on the Rabbit Hole misuse; medium on the rendering, which this review cannot
inspect.

**Acceptance test.** A reader who skims the article can tell, without prior knowledge of 9takes
conventions, that the italic first-person blocks are the writer's construction. No type argument
anywhere in the draft cites the content of an `inner-thought` block.

### SUBJ-C6 — CONCERN — The cold open's hinge is an unsourced tempo claim, and the same exchange's confidence beat is dropped

**Passage.** Cold open, line 109: "Every word of that is defensible. None of it explains how fast the
number came out." TL;DR bullet 1 (line 115): "she answered instantly and low… The speed is the
interesting part." Also line 103: "had spent two years as the most-watched athlete in her sport."

**Evidence.** Packet CLM-02: no located text source characterizes her delivery; the auto-transcript
records words, not tempo, and neither ESPN video was viewed. Packet CLM-30: "two years as the
most-watched athlete in her sport" is an unsourced superlative complicated by a 13-game 2025 season.
Packet S-02 records that Andrews immediately followed up by noting Clark was not capping herself at
six, and Clark replied **"We'll have to see"** with a wry smile.

**Reader/trust effect.** The article's opening move and its TL;DR both rest on a delivery detail
nobody has verified. And the exchange it draws from contains a confidence beat sitting one line
later that the draft does not mention — the fourth instance of the pattern in the bottom line. The
six reads as a ceiling in this article; in the interview she explicitly declines to make it one.

**Minimum viable repair.** Either watch the ESPN segment and keep the tempo claim if it holds, or
re-hinge the open on something checkable — the *number* against the résumé is already strange enough
without the speed. Separately, add "We'll have to see" somewhere in the article; it costs six words
and it is the difference between a self-critical player and a self-critical player who thinks she is
going to be great. Cut "and had spent two years as the most-watched athlete in her sport" to fund it.

**Expected benefit.** Removes the piece's most prominent unverifiable claim, funds two other repairs,
and makes the spine (the six) more honest rather than less striking.

**Confidence.** High on the sourcing status; the tempo claim may well be true and is simply unchecked.

**Acceptance test.** Either a reviewer has watched the segment and logged the timestamp, or no
