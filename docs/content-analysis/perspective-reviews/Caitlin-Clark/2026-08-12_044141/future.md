## Bottom-line verdict

The spine of this piece — the six-out-of-ten self-audit, the turnover-record reasoning, the two lost title games, the Olympic snub, the practice technicals, the race controversy — is built on events dated across four seasons (2020–2026) and will still make sense and still support the Type 1 reading a year from now, regardless of what happens to Clark between now and then. That is the durable 80% of the draft, and it earns its "clear hit" on the turnover-record paragraph specifically (H2 5), which is argued from a permanent structural fact (223 turnovers, a broken record) rather than from anything time-sensitive.

The risk is concentrated almost entirely in the last section and the frontmatter's stat anchor. H2 9 ("Caitlin Clark is still going to the gym about it") builds its closing image out of a single day, August 4, 2026 — a Player of the Week announcement collapsed against an injury-report entry — and the surrounding sentence explicitly frames it as mid-season ("two months and a postseason still in front of them"). None of that is inaccurate today. All of it is a snapshot the article does not mark as a snapshot for a reader arriving in mid-2027, after that postseason has come and gone, the injury has resolved one way or another, and the "second in assists" and "19-11" numbers have been overtaken by a full season's final line. This is a revise, not a hold: nothing here is a factual or trust problem now, and no blocker exists. The fix is a refresh discipline, not a rewrite.

## What landed

- **The turnover-record reasoning (H2 5, lines 285–297)** is the one section built to survive any future state of the world. "Ones are not organized around avoiding mistakes. They are organized around doing the thing correctly... Whether the ball arrives is a separate question, settled a half-second later by four other people." This doesn't depend on Clark's 2026 stat line, her injury status, or the outcome of any pending season — it explains a permanent record (223 turnovers, 2024) with a mechanism that holds regardless of what she does next. It would read exactly the same in 2030.
- **The stated falsifier in the same section** — "Watch for the night she takes the safe pass to protect a number. This reading does not survive it." — is genuinely future-proof: it gives a reader a live test they can apply at any point in Clark's career, not just August 2026.
- **The commercial-seam paragraph (H2 6, lines 328)** is explicitly held open rather than resolved ("she has not solved it, and there may be no version of her career in which she can"), which is the correct move for an unresolved tension — it won't age into a broken claim because it never claimed resolution.

## What missed

- **The closing paragraph (H2 9, lines 428–430)** has no seam marking it as a moment-in-time snapshot. "It is August 2026, and she is spending the hours again... The Fever are 19-11, with two months and a postseason still in front of them" reads, to a reader arriving after that postseason, like the article stopped paying attention rather than like a deliberately chosen anchor point.
- **The stat line that funds the ending** ("averaging 21.5 points and 8.0 assists, second in the league in assists per Basketball-Reference," line 428) is a live-season number with no framing that tells a later reader it was a snapshot rather than a settled career fact.

## What I expected

For an active-athlete subject with a season in progress, I expected either (a) an ending anchored to something already closed (a completed season, a career-total record) so it ages cleanly, or (b) an ending built on a moving anchor but explicitly marked as one, the way a news article dates its lead. The draft's own internal ledger (FORMULA FINGERPRINT LEDGER, line 160) notes the "ending swap-test" passed because no other subject produces this exact August 4 collision — a good uniqueness check, but uniqueness to the subject isn't the same as durability across time, and the ledger doesn't ask the second question.
