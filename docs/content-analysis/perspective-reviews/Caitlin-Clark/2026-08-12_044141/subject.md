## Bottom-line verdict

Even if Caitlin Clark rejected the Type 1 label, most of this draft would still be recognizable as an attempt to represent her rather than flatten her. It restores her self-assertion beside her self-criticism, lets her describe her own fire, names the commercial contradiction without pretending she has solved it, and keeps responsibility attached to the cost of her behavior. The turnover mechanism is especially valuable because it is identified as the article's reading and given a real falsifier.

Trust is nevertheless **strained**, and the frozen draft needs revision before it is fair enough to publish. Three passages cross the line from interpretation into preventable distortion: the H2 8 first-person invention assigns Clark a thesis-shaped private thought that her real account does not support; the Olympic-snub sentence fuses two separately reported remarks into a quotable-sounding composite; and the Monica McNutt sentence drops a material condition from the criticism it summarizes. Four additional concerns are meaningful but reparable without changing the article's thesis.

The task-mandated H2 4 passage is not one of those blockers. The exact adjacent cue — **“The imagined tape session sounds like this:”** — unambiguously identifies the following first-person prose as an authorial construction. ## What missed

The hotel-room passage does not achieve the same responsible separation as the H2 4 passage, even though “gives enough to imagine” technically signals invention. It writes a private first-person script whose key claim — “Every hour I put in this year bought me nothing” — is not in Clark's account and is more useful to the article's hours thesis than it is faithful to her stated grief. Her actual words focus on not putting on the uniform while her team prepares for Game 5. The invented version makes her sound transactional and self-focused immediately before the real quotation shows that her pain was relational and participatory.

Two source compressions also miss in ways that matter to subject fairness. “Called the omission a blessing that woke a monster” sounds like Clark used that exact compound phrase; TIME instead reports separately that Clark told Christie Sides the snub “woke a monster” and that she called the exclusion a “blessing.” Likewise, the McNutt paragraph turns a conditional statement — “if she sees it that way, in the WNBA” — into an unqualified declaration about Clark's role in “the larger sisterhood.” Both compressions make the prose cleaner by making someone else's words less exact.

## What I expected

I expected every first-person interior device to be unmistakably labeled and limited to what Clark's public record can responsibly support. H2 4 meets that expectation under the task's binding constraint; H2 8 does not because its most thesis-serving sentence cannot be traced to her account.

I also expected the draft's careful distinction between facts and interpretations in the turnover section to carry into its structured-data FAQs. The direct type answer can remain direct, but the two FAQ answers that state inferred motivation as fact need the same “this article's reading” boundary the body already uses.

Finally, I expected childhood material to remain possibility rather than origin verdict. “The lesson available” begins carefully, but “Which is why the pattern never confined itself to basketball” closes the uncertainty and presents a causal chain the public record cannot establish.

## What surprised me

The strongest surprise is how much dignity the draft gains by letting Clark be confident. Her response to the Olympic omission is not made into meek agreement; her stated ambition to be the best player in the world remains inside the work quote; and her competitive fire closes its own section in her vocabulary. Those decisions make the article less flattering in a simplistic way and more recognizable.

The commercial-seam paragraph also surprised me positively. “She also keeps the check. Both are true, she has not solved it” neither launders the benefit nor assigns Clark sole responsibility for the racialized economy around her. That unresolved tension is fairer than a forced moral resolution.

### SUBJ-R1 — Blocker: H2 8 invents a private grievance that Clark's account does not supply

- **Location:** “The hotel room where Caitlin Clark spent the 2025 playoffs,” from “Her account gives enough to imagine the night before Game 5” through the `inner-thought` paragraph.
- **Reader effect / trust problem:** The first-person voice places the article's hours thesis inside Clark's private consciousness. “Every hour I put in this year bought me nothing” can be repeated as if it captures her internal state, even though the real quotation that follows emphasizes the uniform and her team. It risks making her grief sound transactional and self-absorbed.
- **Evidence / reasoning:** The packet's S-03/S-15 account supports that the night before games “ate at” her because her team was preparing and she could not put on her uniform. It does not support the invented claims that the hours “bought me nothing” or that she was mentally separated from teammates “downstairs.”
- **Minimum viable repair:** Remove the H2 8 `inner-thought` paragraph and replace its setup with a plain disclosure that Clark has not described her private thoughts beyond the account that follows. Go directly into her sourced ESPN quotation.
- **Expected benefit:** Keeps the emotional force in Clark's own language and prevents the thesis from commandeering a vulnerable moment.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Acceptance test:** H2 8 contains no invented first-person prose, and the first interior account the reader encounters there is Clark's attributed ESPN quotation.

### SUBJ-R2 — Blocker: the Olympic sentence creates a false compound attribution

- **Location:** End of “Why Caitlin Clark never won a national championship”: “She told the same magazine she knew she was good enough for that roster, and called the omission a blessing that woke a monster.”
- **Reader effect / trust problem:** The syntax presents “a blessing that woke a monster” as one Clark formulation. It intensifies her response and invites quotation-like repetition while obscuring who received which statement.
- **Evidence / reasoning:** TIME reports two distinct facts: Clark told her coach that the snub “woke a monster,” and elsewhere she called the exclusion a “blessing.” The source supports both ideas but not the fused phrase. Primary source: https://time.com/7200904/athlete-of-the-year-2024-caitlin-clark/
- **Minimum viable repair:** Separate the two reports and their attribution, for example by stating that she called the exclusion a blessing and that TIME reported she had told her coach the snub “woke a monster.”
- **Expected benefit:** Preserves the self-assertion the section needs without manufacturing Clark's wording.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Acceptance test:** “Blessing” and “woke a monster” no longer appear as a single attributed phrase; the latter is clearly reported as something she told her coach.

### SUBJ-C2 — Concern: childhood anecdotes become a causal origin story

- **Location:** “Caitlin Clark's childhood in Des Moines, and the Christmas she ruined”: “The lesson available in a house like that...” followed by “Which is why the pattern never confined itself to basketball.”

- **Confidence:** High.
