## Bottom-line verdict

The article has earned its sympathetic interpretation in _posture_ and not yet in _evidence_.

The posture is genuinely strong. This draft repeatedly does the thing sympathetic profiles of convicted people almost never do: it states the excuse and then refuses it in its own voice ("That sequence is why ambition cannot work as an alibi here"), it concedes to its hostile critics rather than balancing them away ("an interview granted on the subject's terms... is a friendly instrument"), and it explicitly firewalls the Enneagram reading from the verdict ("the jury's finding about what she knew stands untouched by it"). On the excuse-making test that usually sinks this genre, the draft passes.

It fails a different test. The draft's central move — that Holmes "could know the lab was failing and still experience herself as the most honest person in the building, because what she was being faithful to was the promise rather than the machine" — is never set against the parts of the fraud record that cannot be described that way. The page carries exactly two pieces of the fraud: the pharma logos and the Murdoch binder omission. Both are deceptions _by omission and attribution_, which is to say both are the two items in the entire record most hospitable to a self-deception reading. The Ninth Circuit opinion the draft cites five times also contains, on the pages immediately adjacent to the ones the draft quotes, three items that are hostile to it: fabricated forward financials, a fabricated _historical_ revenue figure, and staged demonstrations. None appears on the page. A thesis that is only ever shown the evidence it explains has not been tested; it has been curated.

The same selection pattern recurs at three other nodes: patient harm is retired on the acquittal while the regulatory record that establishes it independently is absent; the section about employees who challenged the story omits what happened to them afterward; and the article's warmest passage — the 31-cents-an-hour reentry clerk with "no audience" — is sourced entirely from a publicity channel whose instrument-character the draft itself diagnoses forty lines earlier when the subject is the New York Times.

None of this is fabrication, and none of it is exoneration. It is a consistent one-directional thumb on the evidence scale, in a piece whose whole rhetorical claim is that it is weighing fairly. Trust strained, not broken. Every repair I name below is available from sources the draft already cites, with page numbers supplied.

## What landed

**CRITIC-H1 — The alibi refusal.** "That sequence is why ambition cannot work as an alibi here. Unwelcome information reached her, and the version that reached investors was edited. A personality reading can ask why protecting that version mattered enough to do it."

This is the sentence that makes the whole page legitimate rather than apologia. It states the boundary of what psychology is allowed to do, and it states it immediately after the most damning evidence on the page rather than in a methodological caveat far away. It must survive revision verbatim. The one change I would make to it is agentive, not structural (see CRITIC-C8).

**CRITIC-H2 — The endorsements section is the best-built argument in the piece.** Kissinger's "Others will judge the technical aspects of Theranos, but the social implications are vast," then Gardner's early technical objection, then the November 2014 "disaster zone" text and the binder it was kept out of. That is a three-beat structure that moves from the borrowed authority, to the available expertise that was bypassed, to the moment of concealment — and it lands on "That supports the Type 3 reading, and it implicates the institutions that rewarded the story."

That last clause is why this section earns critic trust: it distributes responsibility to the board and the press _without_ diluting hers. Most pieces do one or the other. Preserve the sequence and the ordering.

**CRITIC-H3 — Conceding to the hostile reading.** "The critics get something right that is worth conceding: an interview granted on the subject's terms, at her house, with her children in the frame, is a friendly instrument."

A sympathetic piece naming the mechanism by which sympathetic pieces get manufactured is an unusual and disarming move. Keep it exactly. My concern CRITIC-C1 is that this standard is then not applied to the 2025 PEOPLE interview — the fix is to extend this sentence's logic, never to weaken it.

**CRITIC-H4 — The film is not allowed to launder the verdict.** "That is a critic's reading of a film nobody writing here has seen, and the jury's finding about what she knew stands untouched by it." Correct disclosure of the epistemic position, correct refusal to let a reviewer's phrase do evidentiary work. (The quotation it attaches to has a separate sourcing problem — CRITIC-C6.)

**CRITIC-H5 — The power asymmetry inside the company is named.** "For an employee, being told to believe harder cannot answer a technical objection. It turns a professional duty into a loyalty test... the person raising a problem starts to look like the problem." This is the right analysis. My blocker CRITIC-B3 is that the section then declines to show what the loyalty test actually cost the people who failed it.

**CRITIC-H6 — The ending's symmetry survives a swap test.** "Theranos benefited when admiration for Holmes answered questions about the technology. Her downfall invites the reverse mistake, where contempt for Holmes answers every question about the person." This refuses both available cheap positions and it is specific to a subject whose product was belief. It is the one passage where the piece's standpoint is fully earned.

## What missed

**The self-deception hypothesis is asserted, then never stress-tested.** The documentary section builds to the claim that this is "the point where the type lens does work no verdict can do." For that claim to be true, the lens has to survive contact with the conduct least compatible with it. The draft instead places the hypothesis beside the two most compatible items and declares the jury's finding "untouched." Untouched by what? The reader has never been shown the finding in the detail that would make the tension real. The result is a hypothesis floating above an abstraction. See CRITIC-B1.

**"Both halves of that verdict matter" is promised and then only one half does work.** The intro flags the acquittals as significant. In the employee section the acquittal is deployed: "so those women are not the basis of her conviction." That is true, and it is also the point at which a reader stops thinking about patients — because nothing else about patient exposure is ever supplied. The regulatory half of the record does the work the criminal half cannot, and it is missing. See CRITIC-B2.

**The loyalty-test section stops at the moment the loyalty test begins.** "Balwani's subsequent response demanded an apology" is the last thing that happens to Tyler Shultz on this page, before a reconciliation beat. What actually happened to him ran for years and cost his family several hundred thousand dollars. See CRITIC-B3.

**The prison passage is the piece's one unguarded moment.** "31 cents an hour and no audience is the least glamorous work available." Two sentences earlier: "nobody writes about it." PEOPLE wrote about it, because she sat for an exclusive in which she also maintained her innocence. The passage's emotional payload depends on a solitude that its own sourcing disproves. See CRITIC-C1.

**The strongest counter-thesis is filed where most readers will not find it.** "Founder incentives, legal strategy and gender expectations also explain some of the same behavior" sits inside a collapsed `<details>` accordion. The main body never states the ordinary explanation — that this was a calculated fraud for money and status, and that the personality frame is decoration on it — in its own voice. A steelman that requires a click is not a steelman. See CRITIC-C9.

## What I expected

As a skeptical reader coming to a personality analysis of a convicted fraudster, I expected five things. The draft supplies one and a half.

1. **The fraud, in enough specificity to argue with.** Expected: the categories of misrepresentation the indictment actually alleged, and at least one item from each of the two families — concealment (which the draft has) and affirmative fabrication (which it does not). Got: logos and the binder. The Ninth Circuit lists seven categories at page 11; the draft's readers see evidence bearing on two.
2. **The regulatory record.** Expected: CMS. A clinical laboratory's CLIA certificate being revoked and its CEO being barred from owning or operating a lab is the finding of the body that regulates the actual risk, and it is not subject to the "she was acquitted" answer. Got: nothing.
3. **What it cost the objectors.** Expected: the machinery deployed against people who raised technical concerns, because the article's own thesis is about what happens to the person who raises a problem. Got: one email demanding an apology, attributed to Balwani.
4. **The scale of the harm, once.** Expected: a number. $452M restitution, twelve investor victims, Safeway and Walgreens. Got: the phrase "restitution order" inside a sentence about the appeal. A piece that gives "$9 billion" and "$4.5 billion" for the rise owes the fall a figure.
5. **Ordinary explanation stated in the body.** Expected: the competing non-psychological account, in the main narrative, before the type reading gets the last word. Got: it in an accordion.

The half-credit: the draft _does_ supply the concealment family of evidence rigorously, with primary citations and page anchors, and it does supply two patient witnesses with the acquittal distinction intact. That is real work. It is a subset.

## What surprised me

**Favorably:** the draft argues against its own interest twice without being asked to. "The reported role by itself cannot establish rehabilitation, and her continued denial does not make the help insincere" is a two-directional hedge where a one-directional one would have been easier. And the Balwani section's "Anyone who needs Holmes to be only a mastermind, or only a victim, has to leave out sworn testimony to get there" refuses the reduction that both the prosecution-minded and the sympathetic reader want. I came in expecting to find motive laundering; I found a piece that names motive laundering as a risk and mostly avoids it.

**Unfavorably, and the thing I did not expect at all:** the omissions are asymmetric in a single direction while the _hedges_ are scrupulously symmetric. That combination is more persuasive than either alone and it is harder to catch. A reader is repeatedly told "this cannot establish that," which signals rigor, while the evidentiary base those hedges operate on has been narrowed upstream. I do not read this as intentional — the pipeline notes show a draft that cut aggressively for a word budget — but the net effect on a reader is a piece that feels more balanced than its evidence is.

**Jarring:** "The claims outran the technology." It is the topic sentence for the entire fraud and it describes a gap opening by itself. The Ninth Circuit's own summary of the same facts, page 8: "the vision sold by Holmes and Balwani was nothing more than a mirage... The grandiose achievements touted by Holmes and Balwani were half-truths and outright lies." The draft's register at the moments of maximum specificity is consistently softer than its primary source's.

## Red flags

### Blockers

---

**CRITIC-B1 — The central hypothesis is never tested against the three record items that contradict it. (Blocker)**

- **Passage:** Documentary H2 — "Under that reading, Holmes could know the lab was failing and still experience herself as the most honest person in the building, because what she was being faithful to was the promise rather than the machine." Supported upstream only by Intro ¶4 (logos) and Endorsements ¶5 (the Murdoch binder).
- **Reader effect / trust problem:** The article stakes its unique value on this hypothesis ("this is the point where the type lens does work no verdict can do") and then shows the reader only evidence the hypothesis digests easily. A reader who knows the record will conclude the evidence was selected to fit; a reader who does not will accept an untested claim as demonstrated.
- **Evidence:** Ninth Circuit amended opinion, 23-1167 (the draft's own S-03, cited five times), text extracted and read directly this session:
  - **p. 13** — "Holmes and Balwani also shared false financial projections with investors." Theranos "did not have any revenue in 2012 and 2013 and, in fact, suffered $57 million and $92 million in net losses"; "In 2015, Theranos reported only $1,944,948 in total income to the IRS." Investor-victim Peterson testified the financial documents she received "showed that Theranos was projecting a profit of $230 million by the end of 2015."
  - **p. 13** — investor-victim Grossman testified that before investing, "Holmes and Balwani told him that Theranos had generated $200 million in revenue from the Department of Defense." General Mattis testified (**pp. 13–14**) the Edison "was never put into the field by the military, it was never installed on a military medevac, and it was never used to treat servicemembers on the battlefield."
  - **p. 13** — the demonstrations: "VIP guests would be placed in a room with an Edison or 'minilab' and would be led to believe that the device was running a sample of their blood. In reality, the device was running a 'null protocol' while some of the VIP samples were surreptitiously run on third-party devices."
  - **p. 11** — the seven alleged categories, of which the draft's evidence touches two.
    A fabricated figure about revenue _already received_ is not fidelity to a future promise. A staged demonstration is an act performed in real time on a specific person in a specific room. Neither has an inward half for the self-deception reading to occupy.
- **Minimum viable repair:** Add one paragraph to "How Elizabeth Holmes made powerful endorsements do scientific work" or "How the Theranos story came apart" carrying two of the three items (recommend the $200M DoD claim with Mattis's rebuttal, plus the null-protocol demos), cited to pp. 13–14. Then add one sentence to the documentary section's hypothesis paragraph conceding that these acts sit outside what self-deception describes — something in the shape of: the hypothesis explains the binder; it does not explain a number invented about money already in the door.
- **Expected benefit:** The hypothesis becomes a tested reading rather than a curated one, and the page gains the only thing a skeptical reader wants from it — a personality claim that has been made to survive its worst case.
- **Confidence:** High. Verified against the primary document at the character level.
- **Acceptance test:** A reader who has read the Ninth Circuit opinion can locate, on the published page, at least one affirmative-fabrication item (invented revenue, invented military deployment, or staged demo) _and_ a sentence in the self-deception passage that acknowledges the hypothesis does not cover it.

**CRITIC-B2 — Patient harm is retired on the acquittal, with the independent regulatory record omitted. (Blocker)**

- **Passage:** Employees H2, final ¶ — "The jury acquitted Holmes on the patient counts and convicted her on the investor counts, so those women are not the basis of her conviction. They are the reason the technical objections were worth hearing."
- **Reader effect / trust problem:** The sentence is accurate and the acquittal distinction is a fairness obligation the draft is right to honor. But it is the _last word_ on patients, and it arrives in the criminal frame only. The reader is left with: two witnesses, legally discounted, thesis moves on. The record that establishes systemic patient exposure without needing a jury is never offered, so "acquitted" ends up carrying the weight of "no established patient harm."
- **Evidence:** In July 2016 CMS imposed sanctions revoking the CLIA certificate of Theranos's Newark laboratory and barring Holmes from owning, operating, or directing a clinical laboratory for at least two years (MedCity News; Dark Daily, reporting the March 18, 2016 notice; CMS sanction letter). Separately, Theranos voided or corrected results from Edison devices run in 2014 and 2015 — tens of thousands of corrected reports issued to doctors and patients (TechCrunch, TIME, NBC News, May 2016); the former lab director testified in 2021 that he voided the 2014–2015 Edison results and informed CMS in spring 2016 (Palo Alto Online, Nov 10, 2021). These are agency and testimonial findings, unaffected by the verdict.
- **Minimum viable repair:** One sentence appended to that paragraph, attributed: that in 2016 CMS revoked the lab's certificate and barred Holmes from running a clinical lab for two years, and that Theranos voided or corrected two years of Edison results. Keep the acquittal sentence exactly as written; the point is that it is no longer the only frame.
- **Expected benefit:** The patient question is answered on the evidence that actually governs it, and the draft stops looking like it reached for the one frame in which patients drop out.
- **Confidence:** High on the underlying facts (multiple independent outlets plus the CMS letter); medium on the precise wording of the two-year bar's scope, which should be checked against the CMS letter before publication.
- **Acceptance test:** The published page contains the CMS revocation and the voided-results fact within the same section as the acquittal sentence, and a reader cannot finish the section believing the only patient evidence is two trial witnesses.

**CRITIC-B3 — The section on employees who challenged the story omits what challenging it cost them. (Blocker)**

- **Passage:** Employees H2, ¶4 — "ABC also reported the exchange after Tyler emailed technical concerns. Holmes said the teams would review them; Balwani's subsequent response demanded an apology." Then, two paragraphs later, the reconciliation beat.
- **Reader effect / trust problem:** This section's own thesis is "the person raising a problem starts to look like the problem" — a claim about institutional retaliation. The draft asserts it and then illustrates it with a single rude email, before moving to a warm resolution. A reader closes the section thinking the cost of dissent at Theranos was an unpleasant exchange and a strained family relationship. The actual cost is the strongest single fact against any sympathetic reading of the company Holmes ran, and it is absent.
- **Evidence:** Tyler Shultz's family spent roughly $400,000–$500,000 on legal fees defending against Theranos's legal pressure; counsel from Boies, Schiller & Flexner sent threatening letters on the company's behalf; Shultz has said he believed for years that he was followed by Theranos private investigators (Markkula Center for Applied Ethics interview, Santa Clara University; Lehigh University News; Techdirt's contemporaneous summary of Carreyrou's reporting; Shultz's own public accounts). Holmes was CEO throughout.
- **Minimum viable repair:** Two sentences, carefully bounded — the legal campaign and its cost to the Shultz family, attributed to Shultz's own accounts and Carreyrou's reporting, and framed as conduct by the company under her leadership. Do **not** assert that Holmes personally directed the surveillance or drafted the letters; the public record supports company conduct and her position, not a personal directive.
- **Expected benefit:** The section's thesis becomes earned rather than asserted, and the piece's treatment of power asymmetry stops being the gentlest available version of it.
- **Confidence:** High that the campaign occurred and its approximate cost; deliberately low-confidence on personal attribution, which is why the repair is bounded. A reviewer should confirm the fee figure against a single named source before printing a number, since accounts give both ~$400k and ~$500k.
- **Acceptance test:** The published section states that Theranos's counsel pursued Shultz legally over a sustained period at substantial cost to his family, with attribution, and does so without claiming Holmes personally ordered it.

### Concerns

**CRITIC-C1 — "No audience" is contradicted by the channel that sourced it, and the draft's own skepticism is withheld here.**
_Passage:_ Prison H2, final ¶ — "Helping somebody finish a résumé pays out in one unit: one woman leaves better prepared, and nobody writes about it... 31 cents an hour and no audience is the least glamorous work available."
_Problem:_ We know about the job because Holmes gave PEOPLE an exclusive prison interview (Feb 2025) in which she also maintained her innocence and said she was still working on medical inventions. The draft has already established, correctly, that a subject-granted interview is "a friendly instrument" — but it applies that standard to the 2023 NYT profile and withholds it from the 2025 interview, and the withheld instance is the one producing the article's most sympathetic image. For a thesis about a pattern organized around recognition, the fact that the least-recognition-yielding work reached the public via a publicity exclusive is not a footnote; it is the interesting part.
_Repair:_ One clause — that the role is known because she described it in an interview she granted — and let the existing hedge stand.
_Benefit:_ The passage keeps its empathy and gains the consistency the rest of the piece is built on.
_Confidence:_ High. _Acceptance test:_ The paragraph names the interview as the source of the detail, and "no audience" either goes or is qualified.

**CRITIC-C2 — The one primary-record finding that names Holmes dismissing concerns is omitted, while the same episode's favorable half is kept.**
_Passage:_ Employees H2, ¶4 (as above).
_Problem:_ Ninth Circuit p. 12: employees "conveyed their concerns about the reliability of Theranos's testing to Holmes and Balwani in real time, but Holmes and Balwani dismissed these concerns or shifted blame away from any problems with the Theranos device." The draft's separation of Holmes's and Balwani's conduct is a genuine fairness virtue and should be kept — but the draft prints the exchange in which Holmes appears responsive ("the teams would review them") and omits the court's general finding naming her.
_Repair:_ Add the p. 12 sentence, attributed to the appellate summary, alongside the existing email sequence.
_Benefit:_ The section stops selecting the one moment that flatters and keeps its per-person precision.
_Confidence:_ High. _Acceptance test:_ The published section contains both the individual email exchange and the court's finding that concerns were dismissed by both of them.

**CRITIC-C3 — The SEC deposition's military denial is framed as candor when it is an acknowledgment that the investor pitch was false.**
_Passage:_ Unraveling H2 — "The same testimony holds concrete answers: she denied that Theranos technology had been deployed in the military settings investigators asked about."
_Problem:_ Read against pp. 13–14, that 2017 "No" is not forthrightness; it is Holmes confirming under oath that what investors were told in 2013 was untrue. The draft presents it as a credit against the "600 I don't knows," which inverts its significance.
_Repair:_ Recast the clause so the denial is tied to the representation it contradicts (investors testified they were led to believe otherwise; Mattis testified the device was never fielded).
_Benefit:_ The piece's one attempt to complicate the deposition caricature becomes evidentiary rather than exculpatory.
_Confidence:_ High. _Acceptance test:_ A reader can tell from the passage that the deposition answer contradicts an earlier investor claim rather than demonstrating candor.

**CRITIC-C4 — Two source compressions, both softening in the subject's direction.**
_Passage A:_ Unraveling H2 — "Admiration had carried the story; checking particular claims took it apart." Parloff actually wrote he "was misled—intentionally—but I was also culpable." The draft borrows the authority of his retraction while dropping both the strongest word in it ("intentionally," unfavorable to Holmes) and his self-implication.
_Passage B:_ Employees H2 — "Tyler said they ultimately reconciled and George acknowledged he had been right." CBS supports implicit validation via the Fleet Week story; Shultz has stressed his grandfather never formally apologized.
_Problem:_ Both compressions make the piece warmer than its sources. B is minor; A removes an unfavorable fact from the record.
_Repair:_ Restore "misled — intentionally" in some form; soften B to "came to accept" or add the no-formal-apology clause.
_Benefit:_ Source fidelity at two points a fact-checker will check.
_Confidence:_ High (A), medium (B — a defensible compression, flagged for the editor's call).
_Acceptance test:_ The Parloff sentence carries his "intentionally"; the Shultz sentence no longer claims more than CBS reports.

**CRITIC-C5 — Cheung's testimony is used without the mitigating clause on the same page.**
_Passage:_ Employees H2 — Cheung concluded the machines were "highly unreliable."
_Problem:_ Ninth Circuit p. 28 also records that devices failing quality control "were not used for patient testing." Omitting it is cherry-picking in the draft's own argumentative favor, which is the same defect as B1 pointed the other way — and it leaves the passage exposed to a trivial rebuttal. (The same page sits inside Balwani's Rule 702 discussion, which is worth an editor's awareness.)
_Repair:_ One clause carrying the mitigating fact.
_Benefit:_ The draft demonstrates the two-directional handling it claims; the QC point becomes unrebuttable.
_Confidence:_ High. _Acceptance test:_ The Cheung passage carries the mitigating clause.

**CRITIC-C6 — The only outside voice corroborating the central hypothesis is unverified at the character level.**
_Passage:_ Documentary H2 — Variety's "the epic nature of her willingness to lie to herself."
_Problem:_ Per the shared packet (CLM-26 / S-30) variety.com redirects to a tollbit host returning HTTP 402 and no fetched text exists; the phrase rests on a search snippet. The draft discloses that nobody has seen the _film_ but not that nobody has read the _review_. Using an unread sentence as the external support for the article's strongest claim is the weakest possible footing for the highest-stakes assertion.
_Repair:_ Verify against a fetchable copy before ship, or cut the quotation and let the hypothesis rest on the draft's own evidence — which, if B1 is fixed, it can.
_Benefit:_ Removes the page's single most citable error risk.
_Confidence:_ High that it is unverified; no view on whether the phrase is accurate.
_Acceptance test:_ Either a fetched source is logged, or the quotation is gone.

**CRITIC-C7 — The ending's forward test omits the most obvious disconfirming context.**
_Passage:_ Closing H2 — "That sets a hard standard for any next chapter... Doing work whose credit belongs to somebody else."
_Problem:_ In May 2025 it was reported (NYT, widely followed by NPR, TechCrunch, Fortune) that Billy Evans was raising upward of $50M for Haemanthus, a blood-testing startup; the company has stated Holmes has "zero involvement." The draft separately carries her statement that she is "still working on medical inventions." A reader who knows the Haemanthus reporting reads the ending as either naive or as having looked away. The pipeline's refresh ledger rejected "speculative new-venture details," which is right as to speculation — but a single attributed sentence carrying both the reporting and the denial is not speculation, and the ending is where it is load-bearing.
_Repair:_ One attributed sentence including the "zero involvement" statement.
_Benefit:_ The forward test is applied to the situation that actually exists.
_Confidence:_ Medium-high. Attribution discipline matters here — the denial is not optional.
_Acceptance test:_ The ending acknowledges the reported venture and its denial, or a note records a deliberate editorial decision to exclude it.

**CRITIC-C8 — Agentless and euphemistic register at the three points where the record is most specific.**
_Passages:_ "The claims outran the technology" (Intro ¶3); "the version that reached investors was edited" (Endorsements, final ¶); "Holmes acknowledged adding pharmaceutical-company logos" (Intro ¶4).
_Problem:_ Each describes an act with an actor using a construction that hides or softens the actor. Compare the source's own register at p. 8: "half-truths and outright lies." The individual choices are defensible; the pattern is that the prose goes passive exactly where the evidence goes specific. This is the mechanism by which a piece reads as fair while tilting.
_Repair:_ Make at least "the version that reached investors was edited" agentive; it describes conduct the court attributes to her own texts about what to include.
_Benefit:_ The draft's register stops being systematically gentler than its sources at the decisive moments.
_Confidence:_ Medium — partly a style judgment, but the directional consistency is the evidence.
_Acceptance test:_ No sentence describing a specific act of concealment uses an agentless construction.

**CRITIC-C9 — The strongest counter-thesis is only inside a collapsed accordion.**
_Passage:_ Rabbit Hole — "Founder incentives, legal strategy and gender expectations also explain some of the same behavior."
_Problem:_ The ordinary explanation — calculated fraud for money and status, with personality as decoration — is the main competitor to the entire page and it never appears in the body. A steelman behind a click is not a steelman, and the collapsed element is also the place where most readers never go.
_Repair:_ One sentence in the diagnosis H2 stating the ordinary explanation in the body's own voice and saying what the type reading adds beyond it.
_Benefit:_ The reading is positioned against its rival where readers actually are.
_Confidence:_ High. _Acceptance test:_ A reader who never opens the accordion has encountered the non-psychological explanation.

**CRITIC-C10 — The scale of the harm never gets a number.**
_Passage:_ Prison H2 — "The Ninth Circuit upheld her convictions and restitution order."
_Problem:_ The rise gets "$9 billion" and "$4.5 billion." The fall gets the word "restitution." The packet has the figures: $452M ordered, joint and several, $397M to twelve victims and $54.5M to Safeway and Walgreens, with loss found at $120M. Asymmetric specificity between a subject's peak and the damage is a recognizable tell.
_Repair:_ Add the restitution figure and the victim count once.
_Benefit:_ Symmetry between what she gained and what was lost.
_Confidence:_ High. _Acceptance test:_ A dollar figure for the harm appears once on the page.

**CRITIC-C11 — The Balwani section omits the fact that most limits what the allegations can bear on.**
_Passage:_ Balwani H2 — "The two trials decided fraud charges; neither decided whose account of the relationship was true... the record supports both halves."
_Problem:_ The packet records (S-13n, via Reuters) that Holmes did _not_ claim Balwani controlled her statements to investors, journalists or business partners. That is the single most useful fact for a reader trying to weigh the testimony, and it cuts in a specific direction: it means the allegations, whatever their truth, do not bear on the conduct she was convicted of. Omitting it leaves a paragraph that sounds balanced while withholding the fact that would let the reader actually balance. The current text also does not tell the reader the allegations were introduced as part of her defense at her own trial — context that a reader needs in either direction.
_Repair:_ Add the Reuters-sourced clause. Handle the procedural context only if it can be stated neutrally; if not, the Reuters clause alone is sufficient.
_Benefit:_ The reader gets the limiting fact, and the allegations are neither inflated nor impugned.
_Confidence:_ Medium-high. This is the most delicate item in the review; the repair must not imply the allegations are false. The packet flags this fact as not re-verified this session, so confirm before printing.
_Acceptance test:_ The section states that the testimony did not extend to her representations to investors, without characterizing the abuse allegations' truth.
