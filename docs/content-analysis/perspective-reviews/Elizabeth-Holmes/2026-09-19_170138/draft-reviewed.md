> "I grew up with those stories about greatness, and about people deciding not to spend their lives on something purposeful, and what happens to them when they make that choice." Elizabeth Holmes, [The New Yorker, December 2014](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2014/12/15/blood-simpler)

<p class="firstLetter">Elizabeth Holmes made an extraordinary promise feel like a personal obligation. Believe in the blood test, and you were helping bring affordable medicine to people who needed it. Believe in the founder, and you were recognizing someone who could change the world. For a while, those two decisions became almost indistinguishable.</p>

Then the blood tests had to work.

Holmes founded Theranos in 2003 and left Stanford to build the company. The pitch was easy to understand: run the tests a lab runs, on a drop of blood from a finger prick, faster and cheaper. In the fall of 2013 the company launched that service inside Walgreens stores. Private investors buying in during 2014 valued Theranos at about $9 billion, and Forbes put Holmes at the top of its list of America's richest self-made women at $4.5 billion. [Appeals court account of the pitch](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2025/12/22/23-1167.pdf#page=8) and [of the Walgreens launch](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2025/12/22/23-1167.pdf#page=11), [NPR on the Forbes estimate](https://www.npr.org/sections/thetwo-way/2016/06/01/480342075/net-worth-of-theranos-elizabeth-holmes-plunges-to-0-in-new-forbes-estimate)

The claims outran the technology. In January 2022, a jury convicted Holmes of conspiracy and three counts of wire fraud against investors, and acquitted her on the patient-related counts it considered. Both halves of that verdict matter in a story where the image has so often overwhelmed the particulars. [DOJ case record](https://www.justice.gov/usao-ndca/us-v-elizabeth-holmes-et-al)

One detail makes the fraud concrete: Holmes acknowledged adding pharmaceutical-company logos to Theranos validation reports. Representatives of those companies testified that they had neither validated the technology nor authorized the logos. Investors were handed the appearance of independent scientific endorsement. [Appeals court account](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2025/12/22/23-1167.pdf#page=15)

Twelve years after that New Yorker profile, a camera crew is asking about the same promise. In the teaser for _You Can See Everything_, the documentary A24 puts in theaters in October, Nathan Fielder asks her: **"You're being real right now?"** [A24's official teaser, released September 6, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GGJSRFWALTI), [NBC News, September 7, 2026](https://www.nbcnews.com/pop-culture/movies/elizabeth-holmes-nathan-fielder-documentary-you-can-see-everything-rcna596457)

The [Enneagram Type 3](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-3) reading asks a specific question: **what happens when protecting your identity as a successful founder takes priority over correcting the story your company tells?**

## What is Elizabeth Holmes's personality type?

### Elizabeth Holmes is an Enneagram Type 3, in this interpretation

The case for reading Elizabeth Holmes as an Enneagram Type 3 rests on her pursuit of recognition as a medical innovator and the work she put into sustaining that identity. Confidence is moderate. Holmes has never confirmed a typing, and neither her wing nor her instinctual subtype can be settled from what is on the record.

The emotional pressure in this reading is shame: the possibility that admitting a limit will make the whole person seem inadequate. For Holmes, the specific threat would be losing the identity of the exceptional founder who could solve a problem established medicine had failed to solve. That threat is inferred from her public conduct; she has never described the pressure herself.

That pressure helps explain why confidence might become so difficult to surrender. Admitting that a blood test failed would also puncture the promise attached to her name. Correcting the technology required her to risk some of the standing she had gained by promoting it.

The tradition names the Three's central liability deceit, and it places the first deception inward, before any audience is involved. That ordering matters for Holmes, and the film opening in October is where it gets tested. It stays a hypothesis either way. The jury already decided what she knew, and pressure of any kind does not remove responsibility for it. An identity held that tightly came from somewhere, and Holmes has been specific about where.

## The greatness story Elizabeth Holmes inherited

When Holmes was eight, she was taken on a tour of a Cincinnati hospital named for her father's great-grandfather, a Danish immigrant who studied engineering and became a physician. He had married into the Fleischmann yeast fortune. Ken Auletta's 2014 profile reports that the descendants who did less with it were a regular topic of conversation in the Holmes household. [The New Yorker](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2014/12/15/blood-simpler)

The sentence at the top of this page is the one people quote half of. The half that gets dropped is the second one, where what she absorbed was also about the relatives who decided against spending a life on something purposeful, and what that decision did to them. A hospital with the family name on it at one end of the ledger, a cautionary tale at the other, and an eight-year-old shown both.

A year later her father took a job in Houston. Holmes knew he felt guilty about uprooting them, so she wrote him a letter to console him: what she wanted out of life was **"to discover something new, something that mankind didn't know was possible to do."** Texas would suit her, she added, because it was big on science. The ambition arrived inside a child's effort to reassure a parent.

Most people can feel some version of that demand without building a fraudulent company. What makes Holmes's case distinctive is the scale on which other people came to invest in her answer. She became the visible embodiment of a medical contribution before it had been demonstrated, which is also the point at which criticism of the contribution starts to feel like a verdict on the person making it.

## What Elizabeth Holmes's voice can tell us

Being that embodiment meant being looked at. The baritone and black turtleneck became so recognizable that they now risk doing the thinking for the reader. A photograph supplies the conclusion before anyone asks what Holmes actually said or did.

Amy Chozick's May 2023 New York Times profile reported that Holmes described creating a business persona in order to be taken seriously, and described her speaking in a less distinctive voice than the public one. [Contemporary coverage reproducing the account](https://uproxx.com/viral/elizabeth-holmes-admits-voice-fake-playing-character/)

Her earlier explanation for the clothes was more ordinary. In a 2015 Glamour interview, she said her mother had dressed her in black turtlenecks as a child and described the outfit as a convenient uniform. The resemblance to [Steve Jobs](/personality-analysis/steve-jobs) helped define her public image; her own account complicates the familiar impersonation story. [Glamour interview](https://www.glamour.com/story/theranos-founder-elizabeth-holmes-career-advice)

A young woman entering rooms full of experienced men might reasonably want to look and sound difficult to dismiss. Wanting to be taken seriously makes that choice understandable, and a deep voice defrauds nobody. The consequential question arrives after people start listening: can the authoritative speaker say that a method still needs work? What Theranos told investors about its capabilities is where the scrutiny belongs, and that is where the record gets specific.

## How Elizabeth Holmes made powerful endorsements do scientific work

Henry Kissinger's 2015 TIME tribute offers an unusually clear view of the problem. A Theranos board member, he praised Holmes and then wrote: **"Others will judge the technical aspects of Theranos, but the social implications are vast."** [TIME, April 2015](https://time.com/collections/2015-time-100/3822734/elizabeth-holmes-2015-time-100/)

The sentence sets the technical question aside while enlarging the significance of the answer. Kissinger could recognize a compelling mission without having established that the machinery fulfilled it. Fellow board members George Shultz and James Mattis brought their own prestige. Their accomplishment gave investors reasons to listen; it could not establish laboratory reliability. [Board members in ABC's reporting](https://abcnews.com/Business/theranos-ceo-elizabeth-holmes-600-times-broadcast-deposition/story?id=60576630)

Skepticism existed from the beginning. Stanford professor Phyllis Gardner recalled telling Holmes, **"I don't think your idea is going to work."** That recollection appeared in Nick Bilton's 2016 investigation. It is evidence that technical objections were available, alongside the encouragement Holmes received. [Vanity Fair](https://www.vanityfair.com/news/2016/09/elizabeth-holmes-theranos-exclusive)

Prestigious validation helped Holmes keep advancing past technical objections. That supports the Type 3 reading, and it implicates the institutions that rewarded the story.

Then the court record puts a date on what she knew. Around November 2014, Balwani described the lab to Holmes as a **"disaster zone."** Within weeks the two of them were discussing an investment binder for Rupert Murdoch. The lab problems and the departing lab director's concerns were left out of it, Judge Davila's September 2022 order records. [Order denying acquittal, page 3](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-cand-5_18-cr-00258/pdf/USCOURTS-cand-5_18-cr-00258-25.pdf#page=3)

That sequence is why ambition cannot work as an alibi here. Unwelcome information reached her, and the version that reached investors was edited. A personality reading can ask why protecting that version mattered enough to do it.

## What happened when employees challenged the story

Tyler Shultz encountered a different Theranos from the one its supporters described. Working inside the company gave him reasons to question the claims. His grandfather George sat on the board, so raising those concerns put family trust under pressure as well as professional loyalty.

In deposition footage reported by ABC News in 2019, Tyler described Holmes this way: **"She's really good at telling you what you need to hear to keep going."** [ABC News](https://abcnews.com/Business/theranos-ceo-elizabeth-holmes-600-times-broadcast-deposition/story?id=60576630)

That is the most Type 3 sentence anyone has said about her. It describes reading a room and supplying the exact reassurance it needs: a different answer for the board member, the investor, the lab technician, each one true enough to keep that person believing. The skill is real, and it is not the same thing as telling any of them what was happening in the lab.

ABC also reported the exchange after Tyler emailed technical concerns. Holmes said the teams would review them; Balwani's subsequent response demanded an apology. Erika Cheung, a lab associate hired straight out of college, testified that the Edison device failed quality-control checks so often that she concluded the machines were **"highly unreliable."** Former board member Avie Tevanian said his questions were ignored and another director asked him to resign. Comparing Holmes with Jobs, he said: **"But Elizabeth took it to a new level."** [ABC interviews and emails](https://abcnews.com/Business/theranos-ceo-elizabeth-holmes-600-times-broadcast-deposition/story?id=60576630), [Cheung's testimony in the appeals opinion](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2025/12/22/23-1167.pdf#page=28)

For an employee, being told to believe harder cannot answer a technical objection. It turns a professional duty into a loyalty test. That is the cost of tying a company's promise too tightly to the moral stature of its founder: the person raising a problem starts to look like the problem.

The grandfather story also has an ending the simplest account of Holmes's charisma leaves out. Tyler said they ultimately reconciled and George acknowledged he had been right. [CBS interview, January 2022](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/theranos-whistleblower-tyler-shultz-elizabeth-holmes-conviction/)

Outside the building, the stakes were plainer. At trial, one patient testified that a Theranos HIV test came back positive and two later tests showed she was negative. Another testified that she got incorrect results about her pregnancy. The jury acquitted Holmes on the patient counts and convicted her on the investor counts, so those women are not the basis of her conviction. They are the reason the technical objections were worth hearing. [Court of appeals opinion, page 15](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2025/12/22/23-1167.pdf#page=15)

## How the Theranos story came apart

**October 2015.** John Carreyrou's Wall Street Journal investigation reported what lab staff had been saying inside the company: the proprietary Edison device handled a small share of Theranos tests, most samples ran on commercial analyzers built by other manufacturers, and results diverged from those of outside labs. [Contemporary account of the investigation](https://www.biopharmadive.com/news/bombshell-report-puts-theranos-in-the-hot-seat-alleges-blood-test-inaccura/407446/)

Within a day, Holmes was on CNBC's Mad Money, contesting the reporting rather than the state of the machine. [CNBC, October 15, 2015](https://www.cnbc.com/2015/10/15/theranos-ceo-fires-back-at-wsj-i-was-shocked.html)

**December 2015.** Roger Parloff, whose Fortune cover story had helped make her famous, published **"How Theranos Misled Me."** Admiration had carried the story; checking particular claims took it apart. [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2015/12/17/how-theranos-misled-me-elizabeth-holmes/)

**June 2016.** Forbes revised the $4.5 billion it had credited her with to nothing. Her stake was common stock, behind the preferred shares of everyone who had funded the promise. [NPR](https://www.npr.org/sections/thetwo-way/2016/06/01/480342075/net-worth-of-theranos-elizabeth-holmes-plunges-to-0-in-new-forbes-estimate)

**2017.** Across her SEC testimony, ABC News counted more than 600 instances of "I don't know." The same testimony holds concrete answers: she denied that Theranos technology had been deployed in the military settings investigators asked about, and acknowledged a problem with a published description of the tests. [ABC News, January 2019](https://abcnews.com/Business/theranos-ceo-elizabeth-holmes-600-times-broadcast-deposition/story?id=60576630)

Her willingness to talk never flagged across those years. What changed was what a sentence had to attach to. Onstage a promise could stay large and still move a room. In a deposition, every statement got fastened to a date, a document and a particular representation made to a particular investor, and the ability to inspire belief stopped counting as an answer.

Other people took over the telling from there: Carreyrou's _Bad Blood_ in 2018, HBO's _The Inventor_ in 2019, Hulu's _The Dropout_ in 2022, which won Amanda Seyfried an Emmy for playing her. For most of a decade, the most widely seen Elizabeth Holmes was someone else's performance of her.

## Why the Sunny Balwani relationship resists a simple explanation

Her own account of those years arrived in court. Holmes's relationship with Ramesh "Sunny" Balwani combined romance and business. At her November 2021 trial, she alleged that he subjected her to sexual and emotional abuse and controlled parts of her daily life. Balwani denied the allegations. NPR reported the testimony, including her account that he wanted her to "kill the old Elizabeth." [NPR, November 29, 2021](https://www.nhpr.org/2021-11-29/elizabeth-holmes-testifies-about-alleged-sexual-and-emotional-abuse-at-fraud-trial)

Holmes also testified that she ended the relationship in 2016 as problems at the company came into focus. The two trials decided fraud charges; neither decided whose account of the relationship was true, and no Enneagram reading can explain why anyone stays in an allegedly abusive one.

What the testimony does complicate is the picture of a woman in total command of her own life. It arrived in court beside evidence of the authority she held at Theranos, and the record supports both halves. Anyone who needs Holmes to be only a mastermind, or only a victim, has to leave out sworn testimony to get there.

## What You Can See Everything adds

Sworn testimony did not settle the portrait, and Holmes made the first attempt at a new one herself. In May 2023, weeks before she reported to prison, the New York Times ran Amy Chozick's profile under the headline "Liz Holmes Wants You to Forget About Elizabeth." New name, softer voice, the beach, the babies, the black turtlenecks retired. [TheWrap's account of the profile and the reaction](https://www.thewrap.com/elizabeth-holmes-profile-new-york-times-reactions/)

The rename is the tell. Elizabeth was the founder a jury had convicted. Liz was a mother of two in San Diego who liked the zoo. Nobody has to plan a move like that for it to be exactly the move this reading predicts: when an identity stops earning recognition, build one that can.

Readers pushed back hard. Jezebel called the piece help with laundering her reputation; Fortune read it as a reinvention that was failing. Chozick's closing line, that it is "impossible not to believe her," became the most quoted sentence of the backlash. The critics get something right that is worth conceding: an interview granted on the subject's terms, at her house, with her children in the frame, is a friendly instrument. [Jezebel, May 2023](https://www.jezebel.com/cute-new-york-times-helps-elizabeth-holmes-launder-her-1850413387), [Fortune, May 2023](https://fortune.com/2023/05/08/elizabeth-holmes-theranos-reinvention-failing-new-york-times-profile)

The ordinary explanation is also available. A person can love her children and care intensely about how the public sees her, and a reported scene at the zoo cannot separate those two things.

The documentary puts the same request to be believed under a harder light. Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim's _You Can See Everything_ screened at Telluride on September 6, 2026. Now set that beside the November 2014 text about the disaster zone and the binder it stayed out of. Both are in the record, and this is the point where the type lens does work no verdict can do.

In the Enneagram's account of Three, the deceit starts at home: the image is sold to an audience second and inhabited by the wearer first. Under that reading, Holmes could know the lab was failing and still experience herself as the most honest person in the building, because what she was being faithful to was the promise rather than the machine. Variety's reviewer, writing from Telluride, called it **"the epic nature of her willingness to lie to herself."** That is a critic's reading of a film nobody writing here has seen, and the jury's finding about what she knew stands untouched by it. ## What Elizabeth Holmes does in prison

Outside, the portrait was still being argued over. Inside, her days had a fixed shape. In her first prison interview, published by PEOPLE in February 2025, Holmes called the experience **"hell and torture."** The same interview reported her job: reentry clerk at the federal prison camp in Bryan, Texas, 31 cents an hour, helping women getting out write résumés and apply for benefits. She said she was still working on medical inventions, and she maintained her innocence. [PEOPLE](https://people.com/elizabeth-holmes-breaks-her-silence-in-first-interview-from-prison-it-s-been-hell-and-torture-exclusive-8789737), [ABC7's report on the interview](https://abc7news.com/post/elizabeth-holmes-interview-convicted-theranos-founder-opens-prison-new-people-heres-what-shes/15898456/)

Her legal position has moved only at the margins. The Ninth Circuit upheld her convictions and restitution order in an amended opinion in December 2025, and in March 2026 Judge Edward Davila cut her term from 135 to 123 months under revised sentencing guidelines. As of September 19, 2026, the Bureau of Prisons listed her at FPC Bryan with a projected release date of February 22, 2030, a date that can move. The Justice Department's September 1 update listed her 2025 commutation petition as pending, which is a filing and not a grant. [Appeals court](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2025/12/22/23-1167.pdf), [report and excerpts from the reduction order](https://reason.com/volokh/2026/03/27/theranos-fraudster-elizabeth-holmes-sentence-reduced-by-1-year/), [BOP locator](https://www.bop.gov/inmateloc/), [DOJ clemency status](https://www.justice.gov/pardon/search-clemency-case-status)

The reentry clerk job is harder to score than any of that. Helping somebody finish a résumé pays out in one unit: one woman leaves better prepared, and nobody writes about it. The reported role by itself cannot establish rehabilitation, and her continued denial does not make the help insincere. What makes the job worth noticing is that for a pattern built on recognition at scale, 31 cents an hour and no audience is the least glamorous work available, which is exactly why a sustained version of it would mean something.

## The test Elizabeth Holmes's next chapter has to meet

Reduced to one line, the Type 3 reading says this: the account of her own worth had to keep holding, and she went on offering it after the evidence against her company stopped being arguable.

That sets a hard standard for any next chapter. New clothes, a sympathetic profile, a film that asks whether she is real: none of them can establish change. Specific responsibility would. Correcting one misleading claim without softening it. Accepting a limit on her own expertise. Doing work whose credit belongs to somebody else.

The standard also applies to the people watching. Theranos benefited when admiration for Holmes answered questions about the technology. Her downfall invites the reverse mistake, where contempt for Holmes answers every question about the person.

Both shortcuts let us stop looking. The blood test needed evidence. So does the story we tell about the woman who promised it.
