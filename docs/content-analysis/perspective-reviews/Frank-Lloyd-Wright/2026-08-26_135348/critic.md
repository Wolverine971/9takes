## Bottom-line verdict

The article has earned most of its sympathetic interpretation and then spends the credit in three places where a skeptic is watching hardest.

What it earns: this draft does something rare for a piece built to admire its subject. It catches Wright suppressing evidence in real time (the Okura telegram) and says so in its own voice. It concedes its strongest critic's jab instead of dodging it. It names the leaks, the foreclosure, the Mann Act arrest, the abandoned children, and the $7M repair bill. The Sullivan argument is genuinely original and survives adversarial reading.

Where it does not: the piece's central defensive move is that the leaks were trivial and the cantilever was the real achievement, and it produces Hib Johnson's repeat commissions as "the strongest available answer to Wright's critics." The strongest answer to Wright's critics cannot be assembled while omitting that Fallingwater's cantilever was under-reinforced by design, that Wright rejected the consulting engineers' correction as a personal insult, that the contractor smuggled steel in behind his back, that the slab dropped 1.75 inches the day the formwork came off, and that the house needed a $4M post-tensioning rescue in 2002 to stop it. That is not an architectural quibble tangential to the thesis. It is the same 8-wing dominion reflex the draft admires, with a consequence attached — and its absence is the clearest case in the piece of a sympathetic reading being asserted rather than earned.

The pattern worth naming for the editor: **every one of my findings makes the Type 7w8 case stronger, not weaker.** The cantilever, the Fellowship's documented labor record, the wartime episode — each is better evidence for appetite, dominion, and the compound instinct than what the draft used instead. The omissions are not protecting the thesis. They are protecting Wright. A piece whose entire argument is that this man composed a flattering version of his own life should be the last piece in the corpus to inherit his edits.

Two of the three blockers are cheap one-line factual repairs. The third is a paragraph. None require abandoning the thesis or the voice.

## What landed

**CRITIC-H1 — The Okura telegram, handled against interest.**

> "Wright handed the telegram to the American press. He did not add the missing words 'considerable damage notwithstanding.' Catastrophe converted to marketing copy in one news cycle."

This is the single best piece of evidence-handling in the draft and the reason I read the rest of it in good faith. The reframe thesis is demonstrated on external, documented behavior rather than on Wright's testimony about himself, and the draft volunteers the damning half rather than being caught with it. The packet independently rates this the cleanest item in its Type 7 evidence set (S-03). **Must survive revision verbatim.** If anything in this piece gets cut for the word ceiling, this is the last line to go.

**CRITIC-H2 — The Philip Johnson jab conceded before it is answered.**

> "The jab had teeth. Its point was that he belonged to the century before this one. He knew it, kept performing anyway, and kept winning."

Naming that the strongest critic was substantively right, and that Wright knew it, is the move that separates analysis from fan writing. Most of this corpus dodges here. Keep the concession. (My CRITIC-C3 is about the sentence immediately before it, not about this one.)

**CRITIC-H3 — The Sullivan argument.**

> "The Sullivan story is the only place in the biography where the reframe stalls. Wright reframed his marriage, his career, his murdered lover, his bankruptcies, his children. He did not reframe Sullivan... The thing a Seven cannot reframe is the thing he could not afford to lose."

I tried to break this and could not. It is falsifiable, it is specific, and the "form follows function" → "form and function are one" observation is the only claim in the piece I could not find anywhere else. It also does the honest thing of locating the _limit_ of the theory rather than extending it. It survives even after CRITIC-B1 removes its supporting sentence — see the repair there, which strengthens it. **Protect.**

**CRITIC-H4 — The Fellowship's economics stated without euphemism.**

> "The accounting was unsentimental: at sixty-five, with roughly two commissions in eight years, he needed to keep the lights on, so he sold the only product still moving, which was proximity to himself."

Naming it as a revenue model rather than a pedagogy is correct and unflattering, and the draft does it in its own voice. My CRITIC-C2 asks for the other side of that ledger; it does not ask for this sentence to be softened.

**CRITIC-H5 — The refusal of the grave marker.**

> "He would fill the grave with his own hands and then make certain there was nowhere to go back to."

This is the piece's best inference-from-behavior. It reads a documented act plus Wright's own sentence, and does not claim to know what he felt. Compare it to CRITIC-C6, where the same section stops observing and starts asserting incapacity. The difference between this sentence and that one is the whole craft question in the draft.

## What missed

**CRITIC-M1 — The "answer to Wright's critics" section does not contain the critics' best case.** The H2 "What Wright meant when he told a client to move his chair" is structurally the piece's rebuttal section. It stages the complaint (buildings leaked), gives Wright's reframe, then produces Johnson's fifteen years of repeat commissions as the answer, then Robert Moses as reinforcement. A skeptical reader arrives at this section holding the Fallingwater cantilever, and leaves without ever seeing it named. The section reads as a section that has decided its verdict. See CRITIC-B2.

**CRITIC-M2 — "Cannot tolerate the in-between" is never reconciled with sixteen-year campaigns.** The load-bearing mechanism is stated twice ("Sevens avoid the slow grind and then deliver in a burst. They are not lazy. They are intolerant of the _in-between_"). Then the conclusion says Wright spent "sixteen years fighting for" the Guggenheim, and the Mamah section notes the Prairie Style asked him "to keep iterating on one idea for thirty years." The draft's own facts are the counterexample and the reconciliation lives in a collapsed `<details>` block. See CRITIC-C1.

**CRITIC-M3 — The strongest critic is disposed of by typology before he is answered.** "meant it the way a Type 3 means most things" arrives before the substantive engagement and colors it. See CRITIC-C3.

**CRITIC-M4 — The people harmed are accounted for in Wright's currency.** "The children were the price, and one of them left a receipt." "Catherine, Miriam, and Olgivanna were each necessary and none of them enough." Both sentences evaluate other people by their function in Wright's economy. The draft knows better elsewhere — the John Lloyd Wright paragraph is excellent — but the summarizing sentences default to the subject's ledger. See CRITIC-C10.

## What I expected

- **The cantilever counter-fact**, in the section that adjudicates the leaks. Absent. This is the expectation whose violation costs the most.
- **A named critic of the Fellowship**, given that the draft raises the cult charge itself and then attributes it to "cynics." The archival history exists (Friedland & Zellman, _The Fellowship_, 2006, built on unpublished interviews and Wright-archive documents). Absent.
- **Some acknowledgment that the Guggenheim's fitness for showing art was contested by the artists**, given that the final image turns on how visitors used the building. Absent.
- **An Eight-core rebuttal.** The draft's most vivid evidence is all 8-wing material — contract violation, "move your chair," the sixty-ton column, firing his son. A reader who finds that evidence persuasive has been handed the case for an Eight core and given no reason to prefer a Seven core. The Rabbit Hole rebuts Five, Four, and Three, and never raises Eight. The packet flags this gap explicitly. This is the most consequential _typing_ omission in the piece, and unlike the others it costs the draft nothing to fix — the brittle-shell admission is already the discriminating evidence, it just is not deployed as such.
- **Wright's wartime conduct**, in a piece that argues the compound existed to keep the world out. Absent; see CRITIC-C5.

## What surprised me

**Welcome.** The draft volunteering "He did not add the missing words 'considerable damage notwithstanding'" — I expected to have to find that myself. Also the Vreeland corroboration flagged as paywalled and read only in preview; the temptation to inflate that citation must have been considerable and the draft resisted it. That single hedge did more for my trust than the four sources it cites elsewhere.

**Jarring.** "Cynics called it a cult." Four words, and the only appearance of the Fellowship's critics in a 4,500-word piece that uses the Fellowship as its principal evidence for the subtype call. The construction does the work of a rebuttal without containing one: it names an objection, assigns it to a discrediting class of person, and moves on inside the same sentence. It is the one moment where the draft's technique and its subject's technique become the same technique.

Also jarring, in the other direction: the draft is _harder_ on Wright in its narration than in its summaries. The John Lloyd Wright paragraph and the Okura paragraph are unsparing; the topic sentences and the conclusion soften. Whatever process produced the summaries is more sympathetic than whatever produced the scenes.

## Red flags

### Blockers

**CRITIC-B1 — "Wright never named another influence on his work" is false, and the draft disproves it four paragraphs earlier.**

- **Passage.** Section "Why Wright always called Louis Sullivan 'lieber meister'", paragraph 2: _"Even after Sullivan died broke and alcoholic in a Chicago hotel room in 1924. **Wright never named another influence on his work.** He treated the mentorship the way medieval saints treated relics."_
- **Trust problem.** It is a material factual error, and it is contradicted inside the same article. The preceding section states: _"Wright credited those blocks for the rest of his life"_ and quotes him on Froebel. A reader who notices — and an architecture-literate reader will — concludes the piece is asserting whatever the paragraph needs. The sentence is also load-bearing: it is offered as the proof of Sullivan's singular status, which is the piece's most original argument.
- **Evidence.** Wright, _An Autobiography_, quoted in _Smithsonian_: _"If Japanese prints were to be deducted from my education, I don't know what direction the whole might have taken."_ The same article records the standard summary that Wright acknowledged exactly three influences — the Froebel gifts, Sullivan, and the Japanese woodblock print (packet E-08 / S-30, flagged there as directly contradicting the draft).
- **Minimum viable repair.** Replace the sentence with the true and stronger version: Wright named three influences in his life — the Froebel blocks, the Japanese print, and Sullivan — and only one of them was a person. One sentence, no net word cost.
- **Expected benefit.** Removes a checkable error, removes an internal contradiction, and _improves_ the argument: "the only human being he ever credited" is a sharper claim than "the only influence," and it is the claim the section actually needs.
- **Confidence.** High. Wright's own words, Tier 1, and the draft self-contradicts without reference to any outside source.
- **Acceptance test.** Search the body for "never named another influence" — zero hits. The Sullivan section's uniqueness claim is scoped to persons, and is consistent with the Froebel paragraph and with Wright's documented crediting of Japanese prints. A reader who knows Wright credited Japanese prints finds the article already knows it.

**CRITIC-B2 — The section that promises the strongest answer to Wright's critics omits the strongest fact against him, in the exact feature it is defending.**

- **Passage.** Section "What Wright meant when he told a client to move his chair": _"The leak was weather. The cantilever was the achievement. Complaining was a failure of cantilever-comprehension."_ → _"That is the strongest available answer to Wright's critics, and it is not in Wright's voice."_ (referring to Johnson's repeat commissions).
- **Trust problem.** The draft nominates its own rebuttal as the strongest available and it is not close. It defends the cantilever by asserting the cantilever was the achievement, then omits that Fallingwater's cantilever was under-designed, was corrected against Wright's will and behind his back, deflected immediately and progressively for sixty years, and had to be structurally rescued. A skeptic reading this section concludes the article selected the client anecdote that flatters and skipped the one that does not — in the one section explicitly framed as facing the critics. Everything the section says about Wright's psychology is now suspect by association, including the parts that are right.
- **Evidence.**
  - Kaufmann had Metzger-Richardson perform an independent engineering review; their test cracked the specimen and they proposed roughly **twice** the steel in Wright's design. **Wright rejected it** and treated the review as a personal attack, writing to Kaufmann: _"I have put so much more into this house than you or any client has a right to expect that if I haven't your confidence – to hell with the whole thing."_
  - Contractor **Walter Hall covertly added rebar** — more than Wright specified, less than the engineers wanted.
  - On removal of the first-floor formwork, the cantilever dropped **1.75 inches** immediately; deflection reached **nearly 7 inches** by 1994.
  - Fallingwater's own stewards: _"the concrete and steel of the terraces was overstressed due to **errors in the design of their reinforcement**, which meant they could no longer function as designed."_
  - A **~$4M external post-tensioning intervention in 2002** arrested it. This is separate from, and prior to, the $7M leak project the draft already cites.
- **Minimum viable repair.** One paragraph inside the existing H2, placed after "The cantilever was the achievement" and before the Johnson turn: Wright's own consulting engineers told him the cantilever needed double the steel; he told his client to go to hell; the contractor added steel behind his back; the slab sagged the day the props came out and kept sagging until 2002, when the Conservancy post-tensioned it. Then let the Johnson turn land — it is _more_ impressive, not less, that a client kept writing checks knowing this. Pay for it from the Moses beat (see CRITIC-C7), which is a duplicate dominion anecdote.
- **Expected benefit.** Converts the piece's weakest section into its strongest. The added facts are better 7w8 evidence than what they replace: rejecting a correction as a personal insult _is_ the 8 wing, and it has a documented sixty-year consequence. It also makes the Johnson payoff earn its "strongest available answer" billing, which it currently does not.
- **Confidence.** High on the engineering facts and the deflection figures (institutional steward plus independent engineering accounts, converging). Medium-high on the exact Wright letter wording, which reaches me through secondary reporting — quote it with attribution to the reporting, or paraphrase it, rather than presenting it as archival.
- **Acceptance test.** A reader who arrives knowing about the 2002 post-tensioning finds the article got there first. The phrase "strongest available answer" is either earned or removed. The section names at least one Wright failure that Wright himself refused to concede — currently it names none.

**CRITIC-B3 — The 1914 murder victims are misdescribed.**

- **Passage.** Section "The grave Frank Lloyd Wright refused to mark": _"Mamah died on the lawn. Her two children died with her, along with **three apprentices** and a chore boy of thirteen."_
- **Trust problem.** Material factual error about four murdered men, in the piece's most solemn passage. "Apprentices" flattens three working adults into interchangeable studio juniors — the exact reduction the section is otherwise arguing against when it insists these are people and not color. A reader who checks finds the article careless precisely where care is the point.
- **Evidence.** Taliesin historian Keiran Murphy's victim list, corroborated by Wikipedia's Mamah Borthwick article: **Emil Brodelle, draftsman; Thomas Brunker, foreman; David Lindblom, gardener**; Ernest Weston, son of Wright's carpenter William "Billy" Weston. The packet marks this **settled against "three apprentices"** (D-07, S-02/S-13); only the boy's age (13 vs 14) remains in dispute.
- **Minimum viable repair.** "…along with a draftsman, a foreman, a gardener, and the thirteen-year-old son of Wright's carpenter." Same length. Naming all four is better still and costs about twelve words.
- **Expected benefit.** Removes a confirmed error and restores four men to their own occupations at the one moment the article claims to be treating them as history rather than atmosphere.
- **Confidence.** High. Two independent sources agree; the packet declares it settled.
- **Acceptance test.** Search the body for "three apprentices" — zero hits. Every 1914 victim descriptor in the draft matches Murphy's list. If ages are given, "thirteen" carries a hedge or is dropped, since sources conflict.

### Concerns

**CRITIC-C1 — The core mechanism is contradicted by the two largest facts in Wright's career, and the reconciliation is hidden.**
_Passage:_ "They are intolerant of the _in-between_, the stretch where work is grinding but not yet generative" (Fallingwater section), against "a building he had spent sixteen years fighting for" (conclusion) and "keep iterating on one idea for thirty years" (Mamah section). _Problem:_ the piece's engine claim is falsified by its own narrative unless the growth-line-to-Five mechanism is supplied, and that mechanism sits inside a collapsed `<details>` block most readers will never open. A skeptic reads the conclusion as an unnoticed self-refutation. _Repair:_ one clause in the body, at the Fallingwater paragraph — note that the Seven's intolerance is of the unproductive middle, not of duration, and that Wright sustained sixteen-year campaigns when they stayed generative. _Benefit:_ closes the piece's largest logical hole for the cost of a clause; pre-empts the most obvious informed objection. _Confidence:_ high (the tension is visible in the draft alone; the packet independently flags it under "behavior the Seven hypothesis does not explain well"). _Acceptance test:_ a reader who never opens the Rabbit Hole can state why sixteen years on the Guggenheim does not refute "cannot tolerate the in-between."

**CRITIC-C2 — "Cynics called it a cult" dismisses a documented archival record without engaging it.**
_Passage:_ "Cynics called it a cult." _Problem:_ the Fellowship is the draft's principal evidence for the self-preservation subtype, and its critics appear only as a discredited noun. The best-sourced account — Friedland & Zellman's _The Fellowship_ (2006), built on hundreds of unpublished interviews and Wright-archive documents — describes apprentices who "apprenticed more often as manual laborers than as draftsmen," a community that became a "cult of genius," and young people "inspired and manipulated, often at a staggering personal cost." That is not cynicism; it is the scholarship. _Repair:_ replace "Cynics called it a cult" with one attributed sentence naming the archival charge (manual labor over drafting; Olgivanna's Gurdjieff regime; tuition paid for the privilege), then keep the existing unsentimental-accounting sentence as the draft's own reading. _Benefit:_ the subtype call gets stronger — a compound that ran on other people's labor and devotion is better self-pres evidence than one that merely charged tuition — and the draft stops using a rhetorical move its own thesis condemns. _Confidence:_ high that the critical literature exists and reaches this conclusion; medium on any specific detail, since I read the book through reviews and catalogue copy, not the text. _Acceptance test:_ the Fellowship paragraph names at least one specific, attributed criticism and its source, and the word "cynics" does not carry the load.

**CRITIC-C3 — The strongest critic is neutralized by an unevidenced type assignment, and his quote is compressed away from its best-attested form.**
_Passage:_ "Philip Johnson called him the greatest architect of the nineteenth century, and meant it the way a Type 3 means most things, as a critique dressed for dinner." Repeated in the Rabbit Hole: "**Type 3.** Philip Johnson was the Three in that rivalry." _Problem:_ two things at once. (a) The draft assigns an Enneagram type to a real third party with zero supporting evidence, and uses that assignment to characterize his motive — ad hominem by typology, aimed at the one voice the article has designated as its steelman. (b) The best-attested form of the line is self-aware — _"The worst thing I ever said about Mr. Wright was that he was the greatest architect of the 19th century"_ — and Johnson later softened it (_"I thought it was a compliment"_). The bare form plus the motive attribution makes Johnson pettier than the record does. _Repair:_ cut "the way a Type 3 means most things" and the Rabbit Hole's Johnson-typing; restore the "worst thing I ever said" framing, which is funnier, better sourced, and makes the concession that follows land harder. Keep the Type 3 _counterargument_ about Wright — it is sharp — but argue it from Wright's refusal to adapt, not from Johnson's alleged type. _Benefit:_ removes an unsupported claim about a named person, upgrades a citation from a 2016 secondary to a traceable 1957 publication trail, and makes the steelman actually strong. _Confidence:_ high (packet T-05 documents both the wording and the Pacific Architect and Builder trail). _Acceptance test:_ no third party in the draft carries an Enneagram type without evidence. The Johnson quote appears in its self-aware form or with its provenance dated 1957.

**CRITIC-C4 — The closing line adjudicates against the visitors, and against 21 artists the draft never mentions.**
_Passage:_ "The crowd lined up at Fifth Avenue and 89th Street and climbed his spiral, looking _at_ the paintings, which was wrong, while a building he had spent sixteen years fighting for held them up." _Problem:_ at the emotional peak the draft rules that people looking at art in an art museum were "wrong." In December 1956, twenty-one artists — Willem de Kooning, Robert Motherwell, Adolph Gottlieb, Philip Guston, Franz Kline, Milton Avery, Jack Tworkov, Herbert Ferber, Seymour Lipton among them — published an open letter to the museum's director and trustees arguing the spiral's curved walls and natural light were unsuitable for showing their work; Wright wrote a reply. The exact constituency the building existed to serve said in print that it did not work, and the article closes by telling them they misunderstood. _Repair:_ keep the "looking at / living with" echo — it is the piece's best structural rhyme — but stop short of the verdict, or concede it in a half-sentence ("which twenty-one painters had told him in print was the whole problem"). _Benefit:_ the ending keeps its music and stops being the one place where the draft argues Wright's case for him. It also lands the thesis harder: a man who reframed his critics into people who did not understand him, one more time, at the very end. _Confidence:_ high (Smithsonian Archives of American Art holds the letter and the signatory list; the Guggenheim's own finding aid records Wright's answer). _Acceptance test:_ the final paragraph either drops "which was wrong" or attributes the objection. A reader who knows about the 1956 letter does not finish the article thinking it was unaware.

**CRITIC-C5 — Wright's wartime conduct toward his own apprentices is absent from a piece built on the compound.**
_Problem:_ the draft argues the compound existed to keep the good life within reach and never leaves the frame benign. The record includes a chapter with real consequences for other people: in December 1942 federal judge Patrick T. Stone accused Wright of persuading Taliesin apprentices to seek conscientious-objector status; Stone had sentenced apprentice **Marcus Weston to prison** for refusing induction; Hoover opened an FBI inquiry on 17 December; a 1943 FBI field report recorded that **26 Taliesin fellows** had petitioned the Dodgeville draft board as a group. Chief draftsman John H. Howe spent 1943–46 in a Civilian Public Service camp. **Weston himself denied that Wright influenced him** — the coercion is alleged, not established, and any repair must say so. _Repair:_ two or three sentences in the Fellowship material or near the conclusion, stated at exactly the record's confidence: a judge alleged it, 26 fellows filed together, one went to prison, Weston denied Wright's hand in it. _Benefit:_ it is the strongest available evidence that the compound was an ideological enclosure and not just a nest — which is the draft's own subtype claim, upgraded. Its absence is the piece's largest omitted unfavorable chapter. _Confidence:_ high on the facts (the Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation publishes this from the FBI files itself); high on the Weston denial, which must travel with them. _Acceptance test:_ the draft acknowledges at least one episode in which Wright's authority over apprentices had consequences for the apprentices, with the allegation/denial asymmetry preserved.

**CRITIC-C6 — Type mechanics phrased as incapacity, converting choices into compulsions.**
_Passages:_ "sitting in that dining room with what had happened there was a condition he could not **survive** holding"; "Sevens **do not** leave one room; they leave several at once"; "The shell held because it **had to**." _Problem:_ the draft's best work observes behavior and infers carefully (see CRITIC-H5, one paragraph earlier). These sentences assert that Wright _could not_ do otherwise, which is a claim about capacity no source can support and which quietly relocates his choices outside his agency. For a subject whose defining acts harmed identifiable people, that is the excuse-making failure mode. _Repair:_ swap the modality, not the content — "a condition he did not stay in long enough to feel," "Wright did not leave one room; he left several." Costs nothing. _Benefit:_ keeps every insight, drops every unsupported claim about what Wright was capable of, and removes the strongest handhold for a reader who wants to dismiss the piece as apologia. _Confidence:_ high. _Acceptance test:_ no sentence in the body claims Wright was incapable of an alternative. Type language describes what he did repeatedly, not what he could not do.

**CRITIC-C7 — Regulators appear only as obstacles, and the beat that says so loudest has no located provenance.**
_Passages:_ the sixty-ton column ("Racine's inspectors made Wright load a test column with twelve tons before approving it. He loaded it with sixty"); Moses's "I don't care how many rules you have to break. The Guggenheim must be built." _Problem:_ two issues. Substantively, a powerful official instructing that building codes be broken for a well-connected relative is presented as a triumphant beat, with no acknowledgment of what it is; combined with the column story, the piece has a consistent frame in which safety regulators exist to be overwhelmed. That frame is doing sympathetic work the draft has not argued for — and it sits directly beside CRITIC-B2, where the engineers turned out to be right. Evidentially, the packet located **no primary letter, date, or archive citation** for the Moses quotation (D-05), and the specific family relationship is unresolved. _Repair:_ cut or compress the Moses beat to fund CRITIC-B2's paragraph — it is the third dominion anecdote in one section and the weakest-sourced. If retained, attribute it as reported rather than quoted, and let the code it broke be named. _Benefit:_ removes the draft's shakiest quotation, ends the section on a reversal rather than a third repetition, and pays for the repair that section actually needs. _Confidence:_ high on the provenance gap (packet); medium on the framing, which is a judgment call. _Acceptance test:_ the Moses line is either gone, or carries a hedge equivalent to the one the Vreeland citation carries.

**CRITIC-C8 — The two-hour Fallingwater claim is hedged in the body and stated flat in the FAQ.**
_Passages:_ body — "The legend has been picked apart since. Wright had been turning the site over for months, and the sprint was performance as much as composition." FAQ #3 — "The procrastination-then-flash pattern is **the cleanest Seven work signature in modern biography**." _Problem:_ the qualification lives in the prose and the unqualified superlative lives in the structured data that feeds FAQPage markup and AI answers — i.e. the claim is strongest exactly where the caveat cannot follow it. The body concession also never names its source. Franklin Toker, who did the archival work, compares the story to Lincoln writing the Gettysburg Address on an envelope and found preliminary sketches; Fallingwater's own institution endorses the fast version without engaging him. _Repair:_ name Toker in the body concession (six words), and rewrite the FAQ answer to rest on the safe version — nine months with nothing shown, then a finished scheme in one morning — which holds under _either_ account and is nearly as vivid. _Benefit:_ the strongest surviving claim becomes one no historian disputes, and the syndicated version stops outrunning the evidence. _Confidence:_ high (packet D-01 marks the dispute unresolved and specifies which framing is safe under both accounts). _Acceptance test:_ the FAQ contains no unhedged "two hours" superlative. The body names the skeptic.

**CRITIC-C9 — The most quotable line in the article carries a citation nobody has confirmed.**
_Passage:_ the block pull quote — "Early in life I had to choose between honest arrogance and hypocritical humility…" — attributed to _The Future of Architecture_, 1953. _Problem:_ Wikiquote traces this no further back than a **1981 anthology**; a 1958 Hugh Downs interview is also claimed; the 1953 attribution is **unconfirmed** (packet D-04). This is the piece's set-piece quotation, given a precise book-and-year citation it may not have earned. In an article whose thesis is that Wright manufactured a better version of his own record, a manufactured-looking citation is the specific error that costs the most. _Repair:_ confirm against _The Future of Architecture_ (one lookup settles it), or downgrade the attribution to "widely attributed to Wright; earliest located printing 1981." _Benefit:_ protects the piece's most-quoted sentence from being the one that gets it fact-checked. _Confidence:_ high that the attribution is currently unverified; unknown whether it is wrong. _Acceptance test:_ every dated book citation in the draft is either verified against the book or hedged. The Vreeland citation already models the hedge.

**CRITIC-C10 — Harmed parties are summarized in Wright's currency.**
_Passages:_ "The children were the price, and one of them left a receipt."; "Catherine, Miriam, and Olgivanna were each necessary and none of them enough."; "Catherine refused a divorce for thirteen years." _Problem:_ the summarizing sentences evaluate real people by their function in Wright's economy — children as a cost line, three women graded on sufficiency, and Catherine cast as the obstacle to Wright's plans rather than as a woman raising six children alone while their father was internationally famous. The draft's _scenes_ are better than this (the John Lloyd Wright paragraph is exactly right), which makes the pattern look like a summarizing habit rather than a view. _Repair:_ three small rewrites. "The children were the price" → let John's book title carry it. "None of them enough" → name what each actually did rather than grading them. "Catherine refused a divorce for thirteen years" → note she was raising six children through those thirteen years. Roughly net-zero words. _Benefit:_ removes the piece's clearest inherited-from-the-subject framing, at no cost to the thesis — and the thesis survives better when the costs are visible as people. _Confidence:_ medium-high; part judgment, but "the price" / "not enough" are the subject's accounting categories and the draft adopts them without marking them. _Acceptance test:_ no sentence grades a person by their adequacy to Wright. Catherine appears at least once as an agent rather than an obstacle.
