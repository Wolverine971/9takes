## Identity and scope

**Who.** Frank Lloyd Wright (born Frank Lincoln Wright or Frank Lloyd Wright — see dispute D-08 — 8 June 1867, Richland Center, Wisconsin; died 9 April 1959, Phoenix, Arizona). American architect, interior designer, writer, and educator. Wikidata **Q5604**. Principal works cited by the draft: the Prairie Style houses (Unity Temple, Robie House), Taliesin (Spring Green, Wisconsin) and Taliesin West (Scottsdale, Arizona), the Imperial Hotel (Tokyo), the S.C. Johnson Administration Building and Research Tower (Racine, Wisconsin), Wingspread (Wind Point, Wisconsin), Fallingwater (Mill Run, Pennsylvania), the Usonian houses, and the Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum (New York). Author of _An Autobiography_ (1932, revised 1943), _The Future of Architecture_ (1953), and _A Testament_ (1957).

**Aliases and name forms used in sources.** "FLW"; _lieber Meister_ is Wright's term for Louis Sullivan, not for himself. Mamah Borthwick appears variously as Mamah Bouton Borthwick, Mamah Borthwick Cheney, and Mamah Cheney; she reverted to Borthwick after her 1911 divorce. Herbert Fisk Johnson Jr. appears as "H.F. Johnson, Jr." and "Hib" Johnson. Samuel Curtis Johnson Jr. is "Sam" Johnson.

Frank Lloyd Wright has been dead since 1959; the biographical record is stable and the live variable is institutional programming and preservation news, not new facts about the man.

**A structural caution for every evaluator.** The single largest evidentiary problem with Wright is that the most-quoted source about Wright is Wright. _An Autobiography_ is a work of deliberate self-composition, and a large share of the anecdotes in circulation — the Welsh prophecy, the Froebel gifts, the two interviews with Sullivan, the burial of Mamah, the reframing of the fallow years — reach us through it or through apprentices who absorbed his version. That does not make them false. It does mean that a claim sourced only to Wright is **attributed claim**, not **verified fact**, and evaluators should not let the draft's confident narration convert one into the other. The draft is partly aware of this and says so about the prophecy; it is not consistently aware of it elsewhere.

## First-person evidence

### E-01 — The brittle-shell exchange (Tier 1, load-bearing)

> **WALLACE:** "Arrogance can sometimes be a shell to protect the inner man too, can it not?"
> **WRIGHT:** "Well, it's a pretty brittle shell."

Source: Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation's published excerpts from _The Mike Wallace Interview_ (S-08). **Verified fact** that Wright said it; the Foundation is transcribing its own subject's on-camera words and the full programs are archived at the Harry Ransom Center.

_What it can support:_ that Wright, at ninety, on camera, accepted Wallace's framing of arrogance as a defensive shell and volunteered that his own was thin.
_What it cannot support:_ that Wright believed the arrogance was _entirely_ performance, that he was conceding his self-assessment was false, or that this was a rare unguarded moment rather than a well-turned line from a man who had been giving interviews for fifty years. The draft's reading ("here he was conceding on camera that the whole thing was load-bearing… He did not believe it") is **interpretation** layered on a verified quotation. It is a defensible interpretation; it is not what the quote says.

### E-02 — The arrogance definition (epigraph) (Tier 1)

> "Arrogance is something a man possesses on the surface to defend the fact that he hasn't got the thing that he pretends to have."

Same source and same program (S-08). **Verified fact.** Note that this is Wright defining arrogance in the abstract, in the same conversation in which he was defending his own. Whether he intended it self-referentially is **unresolved**. The draft uses it as the epigraph, which frames it as self-diagnosis; the transcript excerpt does not settle that.

### E-03 — The rebellion answer (Tier 1)

> **WALLACE:** "Do you think that you are any less rebellious, less of a radical in your art and life, than you were a quarter of a century ago?"
> **WRIGHT:** "That I'm more so. Only more quiet about it."

Same source (S-08). **Verified fact.** The draft quotes Wallace's question in paraphrase ("whether age had cooled his rebellion"), which is faithful.

### E-04 — "Honest arrogance" (Tier 4 provenance — flagged)

> "Early in life I had to choose between honest arrogance and hypocritical humility. I chose honest arrogance and have seen no occasion to change."

The wording is stable across sources but the **provenance is not established**. Wikiquote sources it only to _The World's Best Thoughts on Life & Living_ (1981, comp. Eugene Raudsepp) and a 1998 _Michigan Daily_ citation (S-21). Another circulating account places it in a 1958 NBC Chicago interview with Hugh Downs. Variants swap "the former" for "honest arrogance" and "no occasion" for "no occasions." The draft attributes it to **_The Future of Architecture_, 1953**; no source consulted here confirms that attribution. Status: **attributed claim with unverified sourcing.** See CLM-04.

### E-05 — The Mamah burial passage (Tier 1, from _An Autobiography_)

> "So I cut her garden down and with the flowers filled a strong, plain box of fresh, white pine to overflowing."

> "No monument yet marks the spot where she was buried…. Why mark the spot where desolation ended and began?"

> "I wanted to fill the grave myself." / "I filled the grave — in darkness — in the dark."

All from Wright's _An Autobiography_ (reprinted in _Frank Lloyd Wright Collected Writings 1930–32_, vol. 2, ed. Bruce Brooks Pfeiffer, Rizzoli 1992, p. 240) (S-13, S-19, S-22). **Verified fact** that Wright wrote them; **attributed claim** as to what actually happened on the day, since Wright is the only witness quoted.

_What it can support:_ Wright's stated refusal of a marker, in his own words, and the physical act of filling the grave.
_What it cannot support:_ the draft's inference that the refusal was specifically a refusal to have "an address," a place he could be expected to return to. That is **interpretation** — and the strongest single piece of thesis-work the draft does. It should be evaluated as an argument, not as a quotation.

### E-06 — "Live with the waterfall"

> "I want you to live with the waterfall, not just to look at it, but for it to become an integral part of your lives."

Best-attested source is a **1935 letter to the Kaufmann family**, reproduced in Fallingwater's own media-resources quotes sheet (S-29). The same line also circulates as a spoken reply to E.J. Kaufmann and appears in a 1 May 1955 talk to the Taliesin Fellowship. The draft renders it as speech ("he told Kaufmann") and truncates it to "not look _at_ it." Status: **verified fact** as to the words; **medium and occasion are disputed**. See CLM-13.

### E-07 — The Froebel gloss

> "The virtue of all this… lay in the awakening of the child-mind to rhythmic structures in Nature."

The phrase "awakening… to rhythmic structure(s) in Nature" recurs across Wright literature and traces to _An Autobiography_. Wikipedia quotes a nearby autobiography passage directly: "For several years, I sat at the little kindergarten table-top… and played… with the cube, the sphere and the triangle" (S-01). Status: **attributed claim** — the substance is Wright's, but the exact wording the draft quotes was not confirmed verbatim against a primary text in this pass. Singular/plural ("structure" vs "structures") varies by source.

### E-08 — Wright on Japanese prints (Tier 1, _contradicts the draft_)

> "If Japanese prints were to be deducted from my education, I don't know what direction the whole might have taken."

From _An Autobiography_, quoted in _Smithsonian_ (S-30). The same article records the standard summary that **Wright admitted to exactly three influences: the Froebel gifts, Louis Sullivan, and the Japanese woodblock print.** This is direct counterevidence to the draft's sentence "Wright never named another influence on his work." See CLM-08.

## Third-party testimony

### T-01 — Herbert "Hib" Johnson / Sam Johnson, the Wingspread leak (Tier 2)

S.C. Johnson's own company history (S-05) tells the story against itself:

> "As fourth-generation leader Sam Johnson told the story, at a formal dinner party when he was thirteen years old, a sudden thunderstorm sent a stream of water down on H.F.'s head as he presided over the table."

Wright was reached in **Phoenix, Arizona**, and replied: **"Well, Hib, why don't you move your chair?"**

_Strength:_ first-party corporate history repeating a family story that reflects badly on a building the company still owns and promotes. Named narrator, named participants, named location.
_Limits:_ it is a **family recollection recorded decades later**, not a contemporaneous record. The page gives **no year**. Sam Johnson was born in 1928, which points at roughly 1941–42, but no source consulted states a date. The claim that the reply "carried across the room" / "came through the receiver loud enough for the table to hear" appears in the draft's testimony ledger; the S.C. Johnson page as fetched does not state that detail. Treat the audibility detail as **unverified**.

### T-02 — Edgar Tafel, apprentice 1932–41 (Tier 2)

> "Unpredictable, cantankerous, a striking figure with white hair, cape and cane, Frank Lloyd Wright was an individualistic spirit who delighted in acting out his own myth."

Associated with Tafel's _Years with Frank Lloyd Wright: Apprentice to Genius_ (1979; Dover reprint) (S-31). **Limitation an evaluator should know:** this sentence circulates overwhelmingly as the **Dover edition's jacket/catalogue description** of the book. Whether Tafel wrote the sentence himself in the text was **not established** in this pass. It should be treated as **attributed claim (publisher-level)** rather than as verbatim apprentice testimony. The draft leans on the verb "delighted" as evidence of enjoyed performance; if the sentence is publisher copy, that leaning weakens.

Tafel is also the named source for the two-hour Fallingwater drawing (T-04).

### T-03 — Baron Kihachiro Okura, telegram after the Great Kantō earthquake (Tier 2, quoted in Tier 3)

> "Hotel stands undamaged as a monument of your genius hundreds of homeless provided by perfectly maintained service congratulations."

Text matches the draft exactly (S-03). Critically, the same source states: **"Wright passed the telegram to journalists, helping to perpetuate a legend that the hotel was unaffected,"** and documents that the hotel in fact sustained damage — the central section slumped, several floors bulged, four pieces of stonework fell, fans fell from the balcony, toppled electric ranges started a kitchen fire. The hotel fell into an insurance "light damage" category, and roughly 20% of Tokyo's reinforced-concrete buildings sustained _no_ damage, i.e. outperformed it.

This is the packet's cleanest piece of evidence for the draft's reframe thesis, and it is _externally_ corroborated rather than resting on Wright's own telling. Evaluators should note the draft handles it honestly ("He did not add the missing words 'considerable damage notwithstanding'").

### T-04 — Edgar Tafel vs. Franklin Toker on Fallingwater (Tier 2 vs Tier 3, in direct conflict)

Tafel, present in the studio, maintains Wright designed Fallingwater in the time it took Kaufmann to drive from Milwaukee to Taliesin. Franklin Toker (_Fallingwater Rising_, University of Pittsburgh art historian) is skeptical, compares the story to the myth of Lincoln writing the Gettysburg Address on an envelope, and finds evidence of preliminary sketches. Toker: few if any other buildings were drawn start to finish in a day; none in two hours — "an astonishing act for the most complex house of the 20th century" (S-15). Fallingwater's own institutional account (S-06) sides with the fast-drawing version without engaging the dispute: the drawings "flowed fast and furious from Wright's pencil just hours before Kaufmann's unexpected visit."

### T-05 — Philip Johnson (Tier 2/3)

> "The worst thing I ever said about Mr. Wright was that he was the greatest architect of the 19th century."

Traced to Johnson's 1957 talk "100 Years, Frank Lloyd Wright, and Us," delivered to the Washington State AIA chapter and published in _Pacific Architect and Builder_ (March 1957); a competing account places the first utterance before Harvard design students in 1954 (S-12). Johnson later softened it: "I thought it was a compliment but in a way if you think back, the houses that I'm speaking of are the recent ones."

**Note the wording difference.** The best-attested form is a self-aware report of an insult ("the worst thing I ever said…"). The draft uses the bare form, "Philip Johnson called him the greatest architect of the nineteenth century." That is a fair compression, but the draft's testimony ledger dates it "c. 1955–59" via Hugh Howard's _Architecture's Odd Couple_ (2016) when a firmer 1957 primary-publication trail exists.

### T-06 — Robert Moses (Tier 3, primary source not located)

> "I don't care how many rules you have to break. The Guggenheim must be built."

Reported as written communication from Moses to Wright, in the context of the entrance's violation of NYC code on proximity to the sidewalk (S-32). **Moses was related to Wright by marriage**; the fetched source says only "related to Wright by marriage" without specifying the link, and a separate source refers to them as "cousins by marriage," citing correspondence held at the Getty Research Institute (S-33). Keiran Murphy's Guggenheim page independently places Moses in the project's orbit from the start — a 9 July 1943 diary entry by Priscilla Henken records that Wright "has signed the contract for the Guggenheim museum and Robert Moses is showing him the sites" (S-34) — but contains neither the quotation nor the family relationship.

Status: **attributed claim.** The wording is stable across secondary sources; no primary letter, date, or archival citation was located in this pass.

### T-07 — John Lloyd Wright (Tier 2, the son's own account)

Wikipedia's article on John Lloyd Wright (1892–1972) states plainly that "an argument over John's salary resulted in his firing and the two again became estranged," that he "perfected the design for his Lincoln Logs, an idea he had conceived while in Japan," brought them to market in 1918 through the Red Square Toy Company, received a patent 31 Aug 1920, and published _My Father Who Is on Earth_ in 1946 (S-11b).

The **sixteen months** figure and **"paid his own way home"** come from the Smithsonian/Lemelson Center article the draft cites; that page returned HTTP 403 in this pass and its text was recovered **only through search-result summaries**, which report: after 16 months in Japan, John argued with his father over unpaid salary, was fired, and returned to Chicago on his own dime, selling wooden toys to Marshall Field (S-35). A stronger, first-person version also circulates, quoting John: _"After deducting twelve hundred dollars for salary due me, I cabled the remaining eight hundred to Dad. The next day I received a cable: 'you're fired!'"_ — the draft's second-pass notes explicitly **rejected** this version as unsupportable and used only the Lemelson framing. That was a defensible call, but note the consequence: the fired-by-cable detail is the _better-evidenced_ half of the story in first-person terms, and the draft omits it.

### T-08 — Miriam Noel timeline (Tier 3, via biographer Finis Farr)

Noel first wrote to Wright about the Taliesin murders; **Wright received the letter in December 1914**, acknowledged it, and Noel wrote a second letter suggesting they meet; per Finis Farr they met "a few days later." Keiran Murphy describes her as "a sculptress" who had worked in Paris. They married November 1923; she left him by May 1924 (S-14). A separate account has them "living together by 1916" (S-36).

## Public record, accomplishments, failures, and controversies

**Output.** The Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation counts **1,114 designs, 532 realized** (S-24). The draft uses this figure and attributes it. Britannica is reported by the entity-gap packet to give roughly **800 designed / 380 built**; that figure could **not be independently confirmed** in this pass (britannica.com returned HTTP 403). Wikipedia says "more than 1,000 structures" designed and notes 409 extant as of 2013 (S-01). The draft's hedge — "though tallies vary by who is counting" — is honest but one-sided, since it names only the higher figure.

**Recognition.** Cover of _Time_, 17 Jan 1938, with Fallingwater in the background (S-16). Eight Wright buildings inscribed as UNESCO World Heritage in 2019 (per the entity-gap packet; not independently re-verified here).

**Failures and hard facts, stated without softening.**

- **The buildings leaked.** Not as an anecdote but as documented, expensive, ongoing fact. The S.C. Johnson Administration Building's Pyrex glass-tube caulking peeled after completion and leaked "for several years"; the Research Tower had the same problem (S-04). Fallingwater required a three-year, ~$7m conservation project addressing "endemic leaking problems," completed 2026 (S-10, S-20).
- **Structural conservatism was not Wright's failing at Johnson Wax.** The dendriform column test cuts the other way: state inspectors doubted a 12-ton design load; the prototype held 60 short tons before showing strain (S-04). Both facts are true at once and the draft uses both.
- **The Imperial Hotel did not survive the earthquake undamaged**, and the legend that it did was **actively spread by Wright** (S-03).
- **Abandonment.** In 1909 Wright left a wife and six children; Catherine did not grant a divorce until 1922 (S-01). His son John was fired by him over unpaid wages (S-11b, S-35).
- **The Taliesin murders (15 Aug 1914)** are documented history, not color. Seven dead: Mamah Borthwick; her children John (11 or 12) and Martha (8); Emil Brodelle, **draftsman**; Thomas Brunker, **foreman**; David Lindblom, **gardener**; Ernest Weston (13 or 14), son of Wright's carpenter William "Billy" Weston (S-02, S-13). The entity-gap packet's instruction to handle this as history rather than true-crime color stands.
- **Legal and financial.** October 1926 Mann Act arrest at Tonka Bay, Minnesota; charges dropped (S-01). May 1928 Bank of Wisconsin foreclosure on Taliesin, later repurchased through "Frank Lloyd Wright, Inc." with the help of friends and former clients buying shares (S-23).
- **Poverty in the fallow years.** Wright and Olgivanna "eked out a spartan existence at Taliesin, even cutting up curtains to make handkerchiefs," and "reputedly neither of them had bought clothes in four years" (S-27). Note the hedge in the source: **"reputedly."** The draft attributes this to apprentice report; the source consulted here does not name the apprentices.

**Contested characterizations, attributed rather than asserted.**

- That Wright was a narcissist or a psychopath. Psychology Today, a ResearchGate paper, and a narcissism podcast all diagnose him at a distance. The entity-gap packet's ruling is explicit and this packet endorses it: **do not import clinical labels.** Enneagram type is a pattern claim; NPD is a diagnosis, and neither the draft nor its evaluators are positioned to make one about a man dead since 1959.
- The "greatest architect in the world" courtroom-testimony anecdote is repeated everywhere and sourced almost nowhere. **The draft does not use it.** Evaluators should not ask for it.
- "That's how you can tell it's a roof" circulates as a stock Wright line without firm primary sourcing. **The draft does not use it.** It uses the far better-attested Wingspread exchange instead.
- Wright's porkpie hat is documented as a deliberate signature — the Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation's own copy describes the look as "a cape or scarf; a cane that was used not for walking, but gesturing; and finished with an exquisitely made porkpie hat," and offers the simile "Like the eaves of a well-designed building, Wright's hat completed his personal design" (S-37). Meryle Secrest credits Elbert Hubbard as the style's inspiration. **No source states that Wright shaped the hat to mirror his cantilevered roofs.** See CLM-19.

## Enneagram hypothesis evidence

The draft's hypothesis is **Type 7, wing 8, self-preservation subtype.** Everything below is evidence about a hypothesis. Nothing in this section is settled, and evaluators should not treat the strength of the evidence as confirmation of the typology system itself.

### Evidence consistent with Type 7 (reframe, motion, aversion to entrapment)

1. **The Okura telegram, handed to the press.** Externally documented; not Wright's testimony about himself. A catastrophe was converted into promotional material inside one news cycle, with the inconvenient half suppressed (S-03). This is the strongest item because its mechanism — reframe under pressure, in public, in real time — is visible in the record rather than inferred.
2. **The 1932 autobiography written at the bottom.** Twelve years of near-total commission drought (1922–34), a foreclosure, an arrest, and a public scandal, answered with a 1932 book that narrates the period as preparation, and with a school founded the same October (S-27, S-26, S-01).
3. **The refusal of the marker.** Wright's own sentence — "Why mark the spot where desolation ended and began?" — is a refusal of a fixed location for grief, in his own words (E-05).
4. **The speed of the next chapter after Mamah.** Rebuilding began quickly; Noel's letter arrived in December 1914 and was answered (S-14).
5. **Procrastination-then-burst.** Even under Toker's skeptical reading, the Fallingwater design was not committed to paper across nine months and then was, in a matter of hours, on the day the client arrived (S-06, S-15).

### Evidence consistent with the 8 wing (dominion rather than charm)

- "Well, Hib, why don't you move your chair?" — a reframe delivered as a verdict, to a paying client, in front of his guests (S-05).
- The 60-ton column loading: told 12 tons was doubtful, Wright loaded five times the design load (S-04).
- The bootleg Harlan House in violation of an explicit contract (S-01).
- Moses's "I don't care how many rules you have to break" is evidence of the _effect_ Wright had on regulators, not of his own aggression — a distinction worth holding (T-06).

### Evidence consistent with the self-preservation instinct

- Two permanent compounds (Taliesin, Taliesin West) with gardens, theaters, dormitories.
- The Fellowship monetized proximity: ~23 apprentices in Oct 1932 paying roughly $650/year, put to work on construction, operations, and maintenance (S-26, S-24).

### Independent corroboration of the subtype call

Tim Vreeland, "Frank Lloyd Wright (1867–1959) — The Life and Work of a Self-preservation Seven," _Enneagram Monthly_, 8 Jul 2024. The visible preview establishes the title, subtitle, author, date, and that the piece is "the second in a series of four articles describing three extraordinary 20th century architects whose types form a 7-1-4 configuration on the Enneagram diagram" (S-09). **The argument is paywalled and was not read.** This corroborates a _label_, not a _case_. The draft says exactly that, which is the correct handling. Evaluators should not upgrade it.

### Evidence against Type 7, and the strongest alternatives

**Type 5 (the aggregator consensus, usually 5w4).** So Syncd and other free typology aggregators type Wright 5w4 without argument (entity-gap packet). Evidence a Five-reader would point to: a lifetime of solitary geometric work; the Froebel-derived abstraction; the capacity to disappear into a problem; a private symbolic vocabulary; long stretches of withdrawal from the profession. The draft's rebuttal — Fives conserve and withdraw, Wright spent and performed, founded a school selling access to himself, published at the exact moment the profession stopped calling — is a real argument and is supported by the record (S-26, S-01). But note the rebuttal's limitation: it argues from _output behavior_, and a Five under financial duress can also monetize expertise. The discriminating evidence is not the school; it is the _appetite_ — the theatricality, the cape and cane, the courting of press, the reframe reflex. Evaluators should test whether the draft's Five rebuttal actually rests on discriminating evidence or on volume.

**Type 4.** Wright dressed theatrically, cultivated a misunderstood-genius identity, and built a persona on uniqueness. The draft's rebuttal is that Fours sit in pain and Wright outran his. This is the weaker of the two rebuttals as written, because it rests substantially on the four-month Miriam Noel timeline, which is real (E/T-08) but thin as a type discriminator — grief timelines vary enormously and one remarriage interval is not a typology.

**Type 3 (the draft raises it only in the Rabbit Hole).** The obvious Three-reading is Wright's relentless image management, the _Time_ cover, the autobiography as career instrument. The draft's counter — that Threes adapt the product to the market and Wright refused to, which is why the drought lasted twelve years rather than two — is the sharpest counter-typing argument in the piece, and it is supported (S-27: the drought is real and long). It is buried in a collapsed `<details>` block.

**Type 8 as core rather than wing.** Not addressed anywhere in the draft. The dominion behaviors (contract violation, regulator confrontation, "move your chair," firing his son over money) could be read as an Eight core with a Seven wing. The discriminating question is whether the underlying motive is _appetite and escape_ (Seven) or _control and non-vulnerability_ (Eight). The brittle-shell admission arguably cuts toward Seven — an Eight is generally read as less willing to concede the defense on camera — but this is an argument, not a finding. Evaluators may reasonably flag the absence of an 8-core rebuttal as a gap, given that the draft's own 8-wing evidence is its most vivid material.

### Behavior the Seven hypothesis does not explain well

- **Sullivan.** Fifty years of unbroken reverence for one man, through a firing Wright caused, twelve years of silence, and Sullivan's squalid death in 1924. The draft is _aware_ of this and makes it the centerpiece ("the thing a Seven cannot reframe is the thing he could not afford to lose"), which is elegant — but as a matter of evidence, a single unreframed attachment is being explained by the very theory it strains. It is equally readable as ordinary loyalty and does not require a typology.
- **Sustained multi-decade single-idea work.** The Prairie Style ran roughly sixteen years; the Guggenheim occupied sixteen years from commission to opening. Both are hard to square with "cannot tolerate the in-between." The draft addresses this only by assigning it to the growth line to Five.
- **The Mamah refusal.** The draft's most moving reading is also the most theory-dependent. A refusal to mark a grave is also compatible with grief, with a Unitarian family's plainness, with rage, or with a man who did not want tourists. Wright's sentence is real; the _reason_ is unrecoverable.

## Disputes and unresolved questions

**D-01 — How long did Fallingwater actually take to design?** Tafel (present) says the drawings were made in the roughly two hours of Kaufmann's drive. Toker (historian, with archival research) says preliminary sketches existed and that a two-hour start-to-finish design of the most complex house of the century is not credible. Fallingwater's own institution endorses the fast version without engaging Toker. **Unresolved.** Conclusions about Wright's working temperament drawn from the two-hour figure must stay qualified; conclusions drawn from "nine months with nothing shown to the client, then a finished scheme in one morning" are safe under either account.

**D-02 — When was Mamah Borthwick's grave marked, and by whom?** Two incompatible accounts: (a) Olgivanna sought out the site and marked it shortly after Wright's April 1959 death; (b) the marking happened in **1985**, contemporaneous with the exhumation, cremation, and reinterment of Wright's remains at Taliesin West. Two of three sources consulted support (b), including a detailed account of the subsequent 2009 unauthorized-replacement episode. Olgivanna died 1 March 1985, which complicates "his widow set a headstone" under either date. **Unresolved, leaning strongly to 1985.** The draft's "forty-five years" is only correct under (a).

**D-03 — Are the 1 and 28 September 1957 dates filming dates or broadcast dates?** Available reporting describes them as **broadcast** dates for the two-part ABC interview. The draft calls them filming dates, sourcing the HRC catalogue record, which this pass could not load. **Unresolved.**

**D-04 — What is the primary source for "Early in life I had to choose between honest arrogance and hypocritical humility"?** Wikiquote goes no further back than a 1981 anthology. A 1958 Hugh Downs interview is also claimed. The draft's _The Future of Architecture_ (1953) attribution is **unconfirmed.**

**D-05 — What is the primary source and date for Robert Moses's "I don't care how many rules you have to break"?** Stable wording across secondary sources; no primary letter, date, or archive citation located. The precise family relationship ("cousin by marriage" vs. simply "related by marriage") is also **unresolved** at the level of specific genealogy.

**D-06 — Building counts.** Foundation: 1,114 designed / 532 realized. Britannica (per the entity-gap packet, unverifiable here because britannica.com blocked the fetch): ~800 / ~380. Wikipedia: "more than 1,000 structures," 409 extant as of 2013. **Unresolved and probably definitional** — what counts as a "design" versus a project versus a variation is not standardized. Any number must be attributed to its counter.

**D-07 — Ages and occupations of the 1914 victims.** Wikipedia and Keiran Murphy disagree on the ages of Ernest Weston (14 vs 13) and John Cheney (12 vs 11). Neither describes Brodelle, Brunker, or Lindblom as apprentices. **Unresolved on ages; settled against "three apprentices."**

**D-08 — "Frank Lincoln Wright."** The claim that he was born Frank Lincoln Wright and had the middle name changed to Lloyd originates with Brendan Gill's 1987 biography; Wikipedia notes there are **no references to "Frank Lincoln Wright" prior to Gill's book**, and the earliest official document reads "Frank Loyd Wright." The draft does not use this claim; evaluators should not ask for it.

**D-09 — Was Wright's first Chicago employer relevant to the Sullivan story?** Wright worked for Joseph Lyman Silsbee before joining Adler & Sullivan around November 1887 (some sources say 1888). Whether omitting Silsbee materially distorts the "walked into the firm" narration is a judgment call, not a fact question — but the omission is real.

**D-10 — Did Wright ever reconcile with Sullivan?** Wikipedia records the twelve-year silence and does not describe a reconciliation. The draft asserts continuous reverence through 1959 without claiming a reconciliation, which is safe, but the biographical literature (Secrest, Gill, Huxtable — none read in this pass) is where this would be settled.

**D-11 — The Imperial Hotel's formal opening.** Wikipedia gives June 1923 (partial opening 2 July 1922). A widely circulated variant holds that the dedication banquet fell on 1 September 1923, the day of the quake. **Unresolved.** The draft's "three months after" is consistent with the Wikipedia date and does not depend on the variant.

**D-12 — Was the Wingspread reply audible to the table?** The draft's testimony ledger says the reply "came through the receiver loud enough for the table to hear." The S.C. Johnson page as fetched does not say this. **Unverified.**

**What must therefore stay qualified.** Any statement that the Fallingwater design took two hours; the "forty-five years" figure and Olgivanna's 1959 agency at the grave; the 1957 dates as filming dates; the _Future of Architecture_ attribution; the Moses quotation's provenance; the Research Tower's construction span; the characterization of three named murder victims as apprentices; and every sentence in which the Enneagram type is treated as established rather than argued.

**No repository research to reuse.** There is no `docs/content-analysis/research/Frank-Lloyd-Wright.md`, no transcript file, and no prior grade file. This packet therefore did substantially more first-pass verification than a typical evidence packet, and correspondingly more of it is single-sourced.
