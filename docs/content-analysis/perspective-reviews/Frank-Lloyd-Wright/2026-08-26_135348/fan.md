## Bottom-line verdict

Yes — the writer understood him. The _lieber Meister_ section is the best thing I have read about Wright in a popular register in years, and I have read a lot of them. It takes two facts every Wright reader already owns (the lifelong reverence for Sullivan; the sixty-year revision of _form follows function_) and connects them into something I had never seen: Sullivan is the one relationship in the entire biography where Wright's reframe machinery stalls out. That is the delight test, passed cleanly.

Then, four sentences into that same section, the draft tells me "Wright never named another influence on his work," and I stop trusting the paragraph that just impressed me. Wright named three influences — Froebel, Sullivan, and the Japanese print — and he wrote a whole book about the third one in 1912. The draft credits Froebel itself, two sections earlier. This is not a research gap. It is a sentence that contradicts the article it lives in, planted in the passage the article is built on.

The second problem is quieter and, for me, the more interesting one. The draft explains the Taliesin Fellowship — the compound, the tuition, the apprentices cooking his meals — entirely as Wright's Self-Preservation Seven appetite monetizing proximity to itself. It names Olgivanna as co-founder and then writes her out of her own institution. Olgivanna spent roughly seven years as a pupil of G.I. Gurdjieff, and Edgar Tafel, the apprentice this draft quotes twice, wrote that her Gurdjieff training shaped the Fellowship's form and activities from the beginning. That is the answer to "cynics called it a cult," the draft raises the charge and declines to engage it, and the absence quietly inflates the type claim: the compound the article offers as its cleanest self-pres evidence was substantially designed by somebody else.

Neither blocker touches the emotional center. The Mamah section is the strongest sustained writing here, the Okura telegram is the single best-chosen piece of evidence in the piece, and the ending earns itself. This is a revise, not a rebuild — but the Sullivan sentence has to go before anyone who knows Wright reads it.

## What landed

**FAN-H1 — The Sullivan reframe-stall.** H2 "Why Wright always called Louis Sullivan 'lieber meister'" (lines 246–248).

> "The Sullivan story is the only place in the biography where the reframe stalls. Wright reframed his marriage, his career, his murdered lover, his bankruptcies, his children. He did not reframe Sullivan, who stayed _lieber Meister_ until 1959. The thing a Seven cannot reframe is the thing he could not afford to lose."

> "Wright spent sixty years rewriting it as _form and function are one_, the only intellectual debt he edited instead of disowning, and the edit reads like a son trying to finish his father's sentence."

**Why it must survive revision:** this is the article's reason to exist. Every fact in it is common property — the German title, the twelve years of silence, Sullivan dying broke, the credo revision — and none of us had put them in this order. It is the one passage on the page that a Wright reader cannot get from Wikipedia, Gill, or the Ken Burns film, and it is the passage I would send to another fan. Note that the packet flags this as the argument most vulnerable to the charge that it explains a single loyalty with a whole typology (packet, "Behavior the Seven hypothesis does not explain well"). I do not care, and I do not think a fan will. It is offered as a reading, not a proof, and it reads true.

**FAN-H2 — The Okura telegram with the missing clause.** H2 "The years Wright cut his curtains into handkerchiefs" (line 286).

> "Wright handed the telegram to the American press. He did not add the missing words 'considerable damage notwithstanding.' Catastrophe converted to marketing copy in one news cycle."

**Why it must survive revision:** most Wright fans still half-believe the Imperial Hotel came through the Kantō earthquake untouched, because Wright made sure we would. This passage does the rare thing of showing the legend being manufactured, in real time, by the subject's own hand — and it is the only major piece of evidence in the article that does not depend on Wright's testimony about Wright. The draft also gets the damage list right rather than flattering it. Do not soften "he did not add the missing words." That clause is the whole move.

**FAN-H3 — The unmarked grave as a refused address.** H2 "The grave Frank Lloyd Wright refused to mark" (lines 270–274).

> "Then Wright took the shovel and filled the grave himself, alone, until the August light was gone."

> "A marker is an address, and an address is a place you can be expected to return to and sit in. He would fill the grave with his own hands and then make certain there was nowhere to go back to."

**Why it must survive revision:** I knew the burial and I knew "Why mark the spot where desolation ended and began?" I had read it as grief too large for a stone. Reading it as a refusal of a _location_ — a place the world could require him to go and sit — reorganized the sentence for me permanently. It is also the only place in the article where the Seven thesis buys the reader empathy rather than a verdict. See FAN-R6: there is a checkable number inside this passage that is probably wrong, and I am flagging it specifically so the passage survives contact with a fact-checker.

**FAN-H4 — The Wallace pairing and the closing spiral.** H2 "What Wright admitted at ninety" (line 322) and the final paragraph (line 332).

> "Wright's answer: 'That I'm more so. Only more quiet about it.'"

> "The crowd lined up at Fifth Avenue and 89th Street and climbed his spiral, looking _at_ the paintings, which was wrong, while a building he had spent sixteen years fighting for held them up. He had finally built a room nobody could ever move their chair out of."

**Why it must survive revision:** the brittle-shell line is famous and I arrived expecting it. "More so. Only more quiet about it" I did not know, and putting the two admissions from one interview at opposite ends of the article is a genuinely good structural decision. The final paragraph then closes three loops at once — the "live _with_ / look _at_" distinction from Fallingwater, the chair from the cold open, and the spiral as a room you cannot stop moving through. That is composition, not decoration.

**FAN-H5 — Hib Johnson's checkbook as the rebuttal.** H2 "What Wright meant when he told a client to move his chair" (line 314).

> "And after the leak, after the phone call, after the wet dinner jacket, Johnson came back and commissioned the fourteen-story Research Tower... The client took the insult and kept writing checks for fifteen years."

**Why it must survive revision:** every Wright piece repeats "move your chair" and stops on the laugh. This one turns it over and finds the thing that actually complicates the arrogance — the man with the wet head kept hiring him. The draft says so with a commission history instead of an adjective, which is the right way to win that argument, and it means the article's defense of Wright is not in the article's own voice. (The construction dates are wrong; see FAN-R9. The argument is not.)

**FAN-H6 — _My Father Who Is on Earth_.** H2 "Why Wright walked out on six children for Mamah Borthwick" (line 264).

> "Wright never wrote about him at length. John wrote a whole book about Wright in 1946 and called it _My Father Who Is on Earth_."

**Why it must survive revision:** I knew John Lloyd Wright invented Lincoln Logs. I did not know the title of his memoir, and the blasphemy in it — the Lord's Prayer with the father relocated and diminished — is the single sharpest thing any of Wright's children said about him. Two sentences, no commentary, and the abandonment thread set up 1,500 words earlier finally closes. The restraint is correct; do not explain the title.

## What missed

**The Sullivan section undoes itself.** FAN-R1. The passage I most admire contains the flat falsehood that most damages it. Details in Red flags.

**The Fellowship arrives without its co-author.** FAN-R2. "Cynics called it a cult" is raised and dropped. The reason it was called a cult is available, well documented, and attested by the draft's own witness. Details in Red flags.

**The lean years are missing Wright's other business.** FAN-R3. The draft's account of 1922–34 is foreclosure, curtains cut into handkerchiefs, no new clothes in four years, and "one publicity windfall." An informed fan reads that and thinks: what about the prints? Wright was America's foremost dealer in Japanese woodblock prints, sold hundreds to the Metropolitan Museum, and at times earned more from prints than from architecture, with print sales repeatedly pulling him back from ruin. That is not trivia — it is a Seven fact. It is a second appetite, a parallel identity, and a hatch out of the trap, running underneath the exact years the draft describes as trapped. Its absence also weakens a specific sentence: "he sold the only product still moving, which was proximity to himself." He had another product.

**The buildings are furniture, not evidence.** FAN-R4. This is the miss that surprised me most. The persona title is "The Architect Who Designed Himself," the Froebel section promises that the blocks explain the work ("Frank built Fallingwater out of his"), and the last line reaches for the Guggenheim spiral as a psychological object. But between those points the buildings almost never testify. Unity Temple and the Robie House appear once, inside a list. Taliesin West is a name. The Usonians are a clause. Broadacre City — Wright's everyone-gets-an-acre utopia, the most Self-Preservation-Seven artifact he ever produced — exists only as a stress-arrow example inside a collapsed `<details>` block. The largest surviving body of evidence about how this man's mind worked is 532 buildings, and the article reads them almost not at all. Fallingwater is the exception and it is excellent, which proves the move is available.

**The Fallingwater dispute is handled better than the FAQ admits.** The body hedges honestly ("The legend has been picked apart since... the sprint was performance as much as composition"), which I respected. FAQ #3 then states the two-hour version flat, with no hedge, and calls it "the cleanest Seven work signature in modern biography." Fans know Toker's objection. Having the body concede it and the FAQ deny it is the article arguing with itself in the place most likely to be extracted and quoted.

## What I expected

- **The Japanese print, in both of its roles** — as one of the three influences Wright actually named, and as the trade that kept him solvent. Present in neither role; actively denied in the first. See FAN-R1, FAN-R3.
- **Olgivanna as a person with her own history**, given that she co-founded the institution the article uses as its central self-pres evidence and outlived Wright by 26 years running it. She appears four times, always as a name attached to Wright's timeline. See FAN-R2.
- **Silsbee.** "The twenty-year-old Wright walked into the Chicago firm of Adler & Sullivan" is the version Wright told. Joseph Lyman Silsbee employed him first, and Silsbee had designed Unity Chapel for the Lloyd Joneses — the same chapel whose cemetery takes Mamah in the next section but one. That is a connection the article is already positioned to make and does not. See FAN-R7.
- **Toker by name.** The draft alludes to him ("the legend has been picked apart since") without naming him or _Fallingwater Rising_. Naming the skeptic is cheap and it signals to a reading fan that the writer knows the literature.
- **Broadacre and the Usonians as the good life made national.** The Usonian house is where "keep the good life within reach" stops being a private compound and becomes a program for the whole country. The article had that thread and let it go.
- **What I did not expect and did not need:** encyclopedic building coverage, the courtroom "greatest architect in the world" story, or "that's how you can tell it's a roof." The draft skips all three stock anecdotes and is better for it. It uses the far better-sourced Wingspread exchange instead. That is a writer who knows which Wright stories are load-bearing and which are barnacles.

## What surprised me

**Welcome.** "More so. Only more quiet about it" (line 322). I have seen the brittle-shell exchange quoted in a dozen places and never once seen the second admission from the same broadcast. Pulling two moments out of one interview and spending them 4,000 words apart is the kind of thing that only happens when someone actually watched the thing.

**Welcome.** The Fellowship accounting — "at sixty-five, with roughly two commissions in eight years, he needed to keep the lights on." The economics of the Fellowship are usually either romanticized or scandalized; stating them as arithmetic is refreshing. (The commission count is unverified per packet CLM-25, and the paragraph is where FAN-R2 lands, but the instinct is right.)

**Jarring and unearned.** The conclusion (line 330): "he put the hat back on, the one he had shaped to mirror his own cantilevered roofs." That is presented as biography, in the article's penultimate image, and it is not in the record. The Wright Foundation offers "like the eaves of a well-designed building, Wright's hat completed his personal design" as _its own simile_; Secrest credits Elbert Hubbard for the style. The specific harm is that this is exactly the kind of line a fan repeats at a dinner party. I would have repeated it. See FAN-R5.

**Mildly jarring.** "Three apprentices" among the 1914 dead (line 268). Brodelle was a draftsman, Brunker the foreman, Lindblom the gardener. Anyone who has read about that day knows the roster, and getting three murdered men's jobs wrong in a passage the article otherwise handles with real care reads as a slip in exactly the wrong place. The critic and subject reviewers will own the dignity argument; I am flagging it as a canonical-accuracy failure.

## Red flags

**FAN-R1 — "Wright never named another influence on his work" is false, and it is in the best paragraph.** BLOCKER.

- _Passage:_ H2 "Why Wright always called Louis Sullivan 'lieber meister'," line 242: "Even after Sullivan died broke and alcoholic in a Chicago hotel room in 1924. **Wright never named another influence on his work.** He treated the mentorship the way medieval saints treated relics."
- _Reader effect:_ an informed fan hits a flat, checkable, false universal and re-reads everything upstream of it with suspicion. It is a trust break sited in the one section the article cannot afford to lose, and the article refutes itself eight lines earlier at line 234 ("Wright credited those blocks for the rest of his life"), so the reader does not even need outside knowledge to catch it.
- _Evidence:_ Wright is standardly described as acknowledging three influences — the Froebel gifts, Sullivan, and the Japanese woodblock print — and wrote in _An Autobiography_: "If Japanese prints were to be deducted from my education, I don't know what direction the whole might have taken" (packet E-08, CLM-08, via S-30 _Smithsonian_). He also published an entire book of aesthetic theory about the form, _The Japanese Print: An Interpretation_ (1912), and the Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation states the ukiyo-e tradition "offered him a model for insights he treasured deeply" (franklloydwright.org, "Frank Lloyd Wright and the Japanese Print"). Packet status: **contradicted**.
- _Minimum viable repair:_ replace the sentence with the true and stronger version — Wright named three influences, and Sullivan is the only one of the three that was a person. One clause, no net words, and the paragraph's argument gets _better_: a man can credit a set of blocks and a print tradition without owing them anything, which is precisely why owing a man was the debt he could not process.
- _Expected benefit:_ removes the article's most checkable falsehood, repairs an internal contradiction, and strengthens the thesis sentence it currently sits beside.
- _Confidence:_ high.
- _Acceptance test:_ search the reader-visible body for "never named another influence." Zero hits. The replacement text names the Froebel gifts and the Japanese print as the other two acknowledged influences, and the surrounding argument still reaches "the thing a Seven cannot reframe is the thing he could not afford to lose" without alteration.

**FAN-R2 — The Taliesin Fellowship is explained with its co-founder written out, and the cult charge is raised and abandoned.** BLOCKER.

- _Passage:_ H2 "The years Wright cut his curtains into handkerchiefs," line 294: "That same year he and Olgivanna founded the Taliesin Fellowship, where students paid Wright for the privilege of farming, cooking, drafting, and absorbing his presence. **Cynics called it a cult.** The accounting was unsentimental: at sixty-five, with roughly two commissions in eight years, he needed to keep the lights on, so he sold the only product still moving, which was proximity to himself. Build a nest, then fill it with people who pay you to be in it."
- _Reader effect:_ two failures at once. First, the article raises the most serious characterization of the Fellowship and answers it with an accounting non-sequitur — needing money explains why he _charged_, not why the place ran on communal labor, sacred dance, and personal devotion. A fan notices the dodge. Second, the paragraph names Olgivanna and then attributes her institution's entire design to Wright's type, which is an outsider tell: it is what the Fellowship looks like if you have read about Wright and not about Taliesin.
- _Evidence:_ Olgivanna Lloyd Wright worked with G.I. Gurdjieff for roughly seven years from 1917, beginning as a student of the Gurdjieff movements, which she later taught to her own students. Edgar Tafel — the apprentice this draft quotes at line 312 — wrote that her "experience with Gurdjieff did influence the form of the Fellowship and some of the activities envisioned from the beginning," and that "Mrs. Wright was the force that kept the Fellowship in working order, from the very start" (Wikipedia, "Olgivanna Lloyd Wright," citing Tafel; corroborated by gurdjieff.org's Olgivanna material and by Friedland & Zellman, _The Fellowship: The Untold Story of Frank Lloyd Wright and the Taliesin Fellowship_, 2006, which is the standard treatment). The packet does not cover this; it is a genuine gap in the research, not a disagreement with it.
- _Minimum viable repair:_ two sentences inside the existing paragraph. Name Olgivanna's seven years with Gurdjieff, note that Tafel credited her training with shaping the Fellowship's form from the start, and let that answer the cult line instead of the accounting. Then keep the self-pres reading but make it honest about what it explains: Wright supplied the appetite for a compound; Olgivanna supplied the operating system.
- _Expected benefit:_ converts a dodged accusation into the most interesting paragraph in the section, closes the largest fan-visible research hole in the piece, and makes the self-preservation claim more credible by scoping it.
- _Confidence:_ high on the facts; medium on how much the Fellowship's structure is Gurdjieff versus Wright, which is exactly why the repair should attribute rather than assert.
- _Acceptance test:_ the Fellowship paragraph names Gurdjieff, dates Olgivanna's involvement, and attributes the influence claim to Tafel by name. "Cynics called it a cult" is either answered in the same paragraph or cut. The self-pres claim elsewhere in the article does not silently absorb the Fellowship as unqualified evidence.

**FAN-R3 — The Japanese print business is absent from the section it most complicates.** CONCERN.

- _Passage:_ H2 "The years Wright cut his curtains into handkerchiefs," lines 284–294, specifically "He had one publicity windfall in the lean stretch" (286) and "he sold the only product still moving, which was proximity to himself" (294).
- _Reader effect:_ a fan who knows Wright's dealing years reads the poverty account as selectively staged. It is not false — the foreclosure and the handkerchiefs are real — but "one windfall" and "the only product still moving" are both stated as complete when a second income stream is missing from the frame.
- _Evidence:_ Wright was "an admirer, an early and significant collector, and an active dealer across his career — selling hundreds to the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York" (Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation, "Frank Lloyd Wright and the Japanese Print"). Trade coverage of the dealing career reports that for a period he earned more from selling art than from architecture, and that print sales rescued him from financial ruin on multiple occasions (_Antiques and The Arts Weekly_, "The Architect's Other Passion").
- _Minimum viable repair:_ one sentence in the lean-years section acknowledging the print trade as the other thing he was selling, and soften "the only product still moving" to "the product he had left that nobody else could supply." The Seven reading survives intact and gains a second appetite.
- _Expected benefit:_ removes a fan-visible omission, and the print trade is independently good Seven evidence — a parallel business run on connoisseurship and appetite while the main career was dead.
- _Confidence:_ high on the fact of the dealing career; medium on its precise financial weight during 1926–32 specifically, so the repair should not put a number on it.
- _Acceptance test:_ the lean-years section mentions the print dealing at least once, and no sentence in that section asserts that proximity/the Fellowship was his only remaining source of income.

**FAN-R4 — The buildings almost never function as evidence.** CONCERN.

- _Passage:_ structural, across the article. Concretely: line 254 (Unity Temple and the Robie House named once, in a list), line 234 ("Frank built Fallingwater out of his," asserted and never shown), the Rabbit Hole (Broadacre City appears only as a stress-arrow example), and line 332 (the Guggenheim spiral used as a closing joke about chairs rather than as evidence).
- _Reader effect:_ the article promises "The Architect Who Designed Himself" and then argues almost entirely from anecdote, quotation, and biography. A fan finishes it convinced about the man and unsure the writer has looked hard at the work — which for this subject is the harder and more valuable half.
- _Reasoning:_ the piece already knows how to do this. "Live _with_ the waterfall, not look _at_ it" (line 304) is a building used as psychological evidence, and it is the best-integrated paragraph in the second half. The move exists once and is not repeated. The Guggenheim is the strongest unused case: a museum with no rooms, no stopping points, and a continuous ramp is a Seven's building on its face, and the article gets to it only in the last sentence, as a gag.
- _Minimum viable repair:_ one added paragraph, paid for out of trims elsewhere, that reads one building as evidence — the Guggenheim spiral as a room you cannot stop moving through is the obvious candidate and it is already set up by the closing line. Alternatively, convert the existing Froebel assertion into a demonstration by naming one geometric signature the blocks actually produced.
- _Expected benefit:_ delivers on the persona title, gives the fan a second shareable insight, and puts the article's argument on the only body of evidence Wright cannot have edited.
- _Confidence:_ high that this is a real gap; medium on which building is the best vehicle.
- _Acceptance test:_ at least one building other than Fallingwater is read for what its form implies about the man, in the body, in more than a clause.

**FAN-R5 — The porkpie hat's origin is invented.** CONCERN.

- _Passage:_ conclusion, line 330: "Then he put the hat back on, the one he had shaped to mirror his own cantilevered roofs."
- _Reader effect:_ it is written as biographical fact, in a penultimate image, in an article whose entire subject is a man who improved on the record about himself. A fan will repeat it. When it does not check out, the article has done to Wright what Wright did to the Imperial Hotel — which is a bad irony to be caught in on this specific page.
- _Evidence:_ the Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation offers "Like the eaves of a well-designed building, Wright's hat completed his personal design" as its own simile, not as a claim about Wright's intent; Meryle Secrest credits Elbert Hubbard as the inspiration for the style. No source states Wright shaped the hat to echo his roofs (packet CLM-19, S-37).
- _Minimum viable repair:_ cut "the one he had shaped to mirror his own cantilevered roofs" to "the porkpie hat," or convert it to the Foundation's simile with attribution.
- _Expected benefit:_ removes an unsupported factual claim from the article's closing movement at a cost of eight words.
- _Confidence:_ high.
- _Acceptance test:_ the body contains no claim that Wright designed the hat to resemble his architecture, unless the sentence carries an attributed source.

**FAN-R6 — "Forty-five years" is probably wrong, inside the passage I most want protected.** CONCERN.

- _Passage:_ H2 "The grave Frank Lloyd Wright refused to mark," line 272: "It stayed bare for forty-five years, until after his death, when his widow Olgivanna set a headstone on it."
- _Reader effect:_ the whole section's power runs through the duration of that bare ground. A specific number that a reader can check, in the paragraph doing the most emotional work, is where a hostile reader will aim.
- _Evidence:_ two independent accounts place the marking in **1985**, contemporaneous with Wright's exhumation and reinterment at Taliesin West, with a stone reading "Mamah Borthwick CHENEY"; 1914 to 1985 is 71 years, not 45. A single competing account supports marking shortly after Wright's April 1959 death. Olgivanna herself died 1 March 1985, which complicates "his widow set a headstone" under either date (packet CLM-10, D-02; S-19, S-22, S-36). The packet's own verdict is "unresolved, leaning strongly to 1985."
- _Minimum viable repair:_ drop the number and the agent. "It stayed bare for decades, and was marked only long after his death." The thesis point — that Wright refused a marker and the ground stayed bare for a very long time — survives under both accounts and needs neither figure.
- _Expected benefit:_ removes the one checkable liability from the article's emotional center without touching a word of the writing that makes it work.
- _Confidence:_ medium-high (the packet could not resolve D-02 to a primary source; I did not spend source budget re-litigating it).
- _Acceptance test:_ the sentence contains no specific year count and no unqualified claim that Olgivanna personally set the stone. The Wright quotation and the "address" reading are untouched.

**FAN-R7 — Silsbee is elided from the Sullivan origin.** CONCERN.

- _Passage:_ H2 "Why Wright always called Louis Sullivan 'lieber meister'," line 240: "In 1887 the twenty-year-old Wright walked into the Chicago firm of Adler & Sullivan and talked Louis Sullivan into hiring him after two short interviews."
- _Reader effect:_ small on its own, but it is Wright's own version of his arrival, told straight, in an article whose thesis is that Wright's own versions are compositions. An architecture-literate reader notices, and the noticing is worse here than it would be elsewhere because the surrounding section is the piece's best.
- _Evidence:_ Wright arrived in Chicago in 1887 and worked first for Joseph Lyman Silsbee, joining Adler & Sullivan "around November 1887" (packet CLM-07, D-09; S-01). The "two short interviews" detail comes from Wright's own telling and is not independently confirmed. Silsbee had designed Unity Chapel for the Lloyd Jones family — the chapel whose cemetery receives Mamah two sections later.
- _Minimum viable repair:_ one clause — he came to Chicago, drew for Silsbee first, and talked his way into Adler & Sullivan by November. Optionally, since the article needs the Unity Chapel setting anyway, note that Silsbee was already the Lloyd Joneses' architect.
- _Expected benefit:_ removes an unforced repetition of Wright's self-mythology from the section arguing that Wright mythologized himself, and hands the article a free structural link to its own next-but-one section.
- _Confidence:_ high on the fact; medium on whether the Unity Chapel link is worth the words.
- _Acceptance test:_ the Sullivan section either names Silsbee or stops narrating the Adler & Sullivan hire as Wright's first act in Chicago.

**FAN-R8 — The Wasmuth compression and an overstated "credited."** CONCERN.

- _Passage:_ H2 "Why Wright walked out on six children for Mamah Borthwick," line 258: "In Berlin he assembled the _Wasmuth Portfolio_, a hundred-odd lithographs that Gropius and Mies van der Rohe later credited for shaping the Bauhaus."
- _Reader effect:_ two fan-visible problems. Wright readers know the Fiesole chapter — the drawings were substantially reworked in Italy in mid-1910 after Lloyd Wright and Taylor Woolley carried them out — so "in Berlin he assembled" reads as a writer working from a summary. And "credited" is a word that sounds verifiable while doing unverifiable work: the influence is asserted by historians, not documented as crediting by Gropius or Mies themselves.
- _Evidence:_ packet CLM-21; S-11, S-28. 100 plates, 66 buildings and projects 1893–1909, published by Ernst Wasmuth in Berlin 1910/1911; Fiesole rework mid-1910; direct crediting by Gropius and Mies not established.
- _Minimum viable repair:_ "he put together the _Wasmuth Portfolio_... a hundred plates that architectural historians credit with shaping the Bauhaus generation." Add "and Italy" or "finished in Fiesole" if the words are available; the Fiesole detail is also thematically useful, since Mamah was there.
- _Expected benefit:_ removes an outsider tell and downgrades one verb to what the record supports.
- _Confidence:_ high.
- _Acceptance test:_ the sentence no longer attributes the crediting to Gropius and Mies personally, and does not state that the portfolio was assembled solely in Berlin.

**FAN-R9 — Research Tower construction dates are wrong.** CONCERN.

- _Passage:_ H2 "What Wright meant when he told a client to move his chair," line 314: "he commissioned the fourteen-story Research Tower, built between 1944 and 1951."
- _Reader effect:_ the Johnson Wax buildings are canon; readers who know them know the tower went up in the late forties. A wrong date inside the article's best rebuttal gives a skeptical reader a reason to discount the rebuttal.
- _Evidence:_ designed/commissioned 1944, but built 1947–1950 — groundbreaking 6 Nov 1947, dedication 17 Nov 1950. Neither 1944 nor 1951 is a construction year. Storey count is genuinely contested (13, 14, 15 all circulate for a 153-foot structure with square floors alternating with circular mezzanines) (packet CLM-14; S-04, S-17).
- _Minimum viable repair:_ "commissioned the Research Tower in 1944 and dedicated in 1950." The dependent claim, "kept writing checks for fifteen years," still works from the 1936 Administration Building commission to the 1950 dedication.
- _Expected benefit:_ corrects a checkable date without weakening the rhetorical payoff it supports.
- _Confidence:_ high.
- _Acceptance test:_ the sentence gives 1944 as commission/design and 1947–1950 as construction, or gives the 1950 dedication; "fifteen years" is verified against whichever anchors are used.

## Specific improvements

1. Replace the false universal about Sullivan with the bounded three-influences formulation in FAN-R1; preserve the surrounding reframe-stall argument.
2. Attribute Olgivanna's role in shaping the Taliesin Fellowship before using the institution as evidence for Wright's type, as specified in FAN-R2.
3. Apply the factual, attribution, and date corrections in FAN-R3 through FAN-R9 without disturbing the ranked preserve list below.

## Follow-on questions

**FAN-Q1 — How much of the Taliesin Fellowship's daily form is Gurdjieff, and how much is Wright?**
_What would change:_ if the Gurdjieff influence is as structural as Tafel implies, the article's self-preservation claim needs scoping — the compound instinct would be Wright's, the institution's operating design substantially Olgivanna's, and the Fellowship becomes weaker self-pres evidence than the article currently treats it as. If the influence is decorative, FAN-R2 shrinks to a one-sentence context add.
_Best source to pursue:_ Roger Friedland and Harold Zellman, _The Fellowship: The Untold Story of Frank Lloyd Wright and the Taliesin Fellowship_ (2006) — the standard book-length treatment. Edgar Tafel's _Years with Frank Lloyd Wright_ (1979) for the first-person version, which would also settle whether the "cantankerous... cape and cane" sentence the draft quotes is Tafel's or Dover's jacket copy (packet S-31, currently unresolved).
