## Bottom-line verdict

Read from August 2027, this piece has a durable argument wearing a disposable coat.

The spine is twenty to thirty years old — a raindrop cue at five, a look across a dance studio at
thirteen, a Tupperware box in his twenties, a fence around a private life. None of that expires.
Strip every 2026 event out of the draft and the thesis still stands, still has its best sentences,
and still has its close. That is the single most important durability fact about this article, and it
is why the value rating is high rather than useful.

What fails is the surface. The two highest-traffic positions in the piece — the cold open and the
closer — carry six relative time markers with no absolute anchor behind them, so a reader arriving
twelve months from now is told a thirty-nine-year-old is thirty-eight, that a finished year is "this
year," and that "last November" was a November that had not happened yet when the events described
took place. Separately, the newest section in the draft answers an evergreen question ("What Is
Jonathan Bailey Doing Next?") with an answer that has a known expiry date inside the review window,
and hangs a thesis-level landing on a third-party news event that was nineteen days old at
publication.

Both blockers are cheap to fix. Neither requires new research, a restructure, or a single new source.
This is a `revise`, not a `hold_for_research` — and the revision is mostly punctuation-level date
insertion plus demoting one paragraph from argument to appendix.

One thing the piece does better than almost anything I could ask for: the relationship section
refuses to adjudicate a contradiction. That is the highest-churn fact about any living celebrity, and
by reporting the record instead of resolving it, that section will still be correct in 2027 no matter
what he says or does not say between now and then. Whoever wrote that made a deliberate durability
choice, and it worked.

## What landed

**FUTURE-H1 — The prompt book is dated by age, not by "now."**

> "### Thirteen: the friend in the doorway" / "### Thirty: the calls that went to his agent" /
> "### Thirty-seven: the backflip he didn't do"

and inside the third entry:

> "Twenty-four years after the leotard, the calculation still runs in real time and out loud."

Age-relative anchors never go stale. Thirteen is thirteen in 2027, in 2030, and in 2040. The entire
central section of this article is built on a clock that does not tick. This is the structural asset
that lets me rate durability high despite the blockers, and it must survive revision untouched — if a
future editor "modernises" these to dates or to "recently," they destroy the one part of the piece
that is genuinely time-proof.

**FUTURE-H2 — The 1p statistic is pre-hedged against its own correction.**

> "The figure is his own count rather than a verified statistic, and he repeats it everywhere he goes."

The packet records a published LGBT+ Consortium figure of 10p in every £100 (S-22, 27 Mar 2025) — an
order of magnitude away from Bailey's number, measuring a different denominator. If that figure gains
currency in the next twelve months, or if a fact-check runs at Bailey's number, this draft is already
insulated: it never asserted the statistic, it asserted that he says it. This is exactly how a fragile
number should be carried in an evergreen page. Preserve the hedge verbatim; do not let a later
copy-edit tighten it into a claim.

**FUTURE-H3 — The relationship section is the most future-proof passage in the draft.**

> "The honest answer to the search query is that it is not publicly established, and the record is
> openly contradictory."

Followed by both data points reported and neither resolved. Relationship status is the fact most
likely to change about any living public figure inside a twelve-month window. If Bailey names a
partner in March 2027, this paragraph does not become wrong — it becomes an accurate account of what
the record showed in 2026, and the analysis built on it (privacy as the thing that funds the speaking
out) is untouched. Most celebrity pages would need a rewrite; this one needs a sentence appended.
This is the single best durability decision in the article.

**FUTURE-H4 — A past-tense heading for a bounded event.**

> "## Why Jonathan Bailey Stopped Acting for a Year"

Past tense, bounded, resolvable. It reads correctly in 2026 and it reads correctly in 2029. Compare
this directly against H2 #7, which is the blocker below — the draft already demonstrates it knows how
to build a heading that survives, which makes the "Doing Next" construction a lapse rather than a
blind spot.

**FUTURE-H5 — The close cannot be dated at all.**

> "About nine courses. One finished. Expelled from the Open University for the rest, including one on
> Leonardo da Vinci and one on volcanoes and tsunamis."

No year, no status, no superlative, no news peg. The last thing the reader takes away is the most
durable sentence in the piece. The article ends better than it begins, temporally speaking, and that
is the right way round.

## What missed

The cold open is the piece's weakest section by durability, and it is the section every reader hits
first. Four separate time-sensitive constructions stack inside two paragraphs — an age, a superlative
status, an undated month, and a present-tense brand relationship — with not one absolute year among
them until the reader is deep into the body. The writing in that opening is strong enough that this
reads as an oversight rather than a habit; the same writer produced FUTURE-H1 three sections later.

The second miss is a promise problem. "What Is Jonathan Bailey Doing Next?" is an evergreen question
posed as a heading, and evergreen questions attract evergreen traffic. The section under it answers
with a status snapshot dated to a nineteen-day-old news cycle. The heading will keep earning arrivals
long after the answer stops being the answer, which is the specific way an SEO page decays into a
liability rather than merely aging.

## What I expected

For a page carrying `changefreq: 'monthly'` and a slug built for long-tail search, I expected the
draft to distinguish between two kinds of fact: the ones that anchor the argument (childhood, ballet,
coming out, Richard II reviews — all correctly dated, all durable) and the ones that decorate the
present (SMA, Armani, MARTINI, Bridgerton scheduling, the Barbican). The draft handles the first
category well and treats the second category as though it were the first.

I expected current-affairs material to sit in appendix position — reported, dated, and structurally
removable. Instead the newest item in the entire draft, the Grande withdrawal, occupies the final
paragraph before the Rabbit Hole and carries a thesis-level landing. That inverts the durability
ordering: the more fragile a fact is, the less argumentative weight it should bear.

I did not expect, and did not find, any resolution of the tension the piece itself creates — that its
central critical claim ("the toolkit is not in doubt, the arrival time is") has a scheduled test date
inside the review window. That is an opportunity, not just a risk, and it is covered in FUTURE-C9.

## What surprised me

Pleasantly: the internal age arithmetic is sound. "Thirty-two years later he returns" checks out
against the packet's verified 1995 Barbican debut at seven (S-17) and a summer 2027 opening. Ages 13,
30, and 37 in the prompt book all reconcile against the 25 April 1988 birth date and the events they
label. I went looking for arithmetic rot and found none.

Also pleasantly: my Armani research cut against my own hypothesis. I expected to find a
short-term campaign and a fragile present-tense claim. What the record shows is a brand-new masculine
pillar — the house's first in more than twenty years — launching in the US on 15 August 2026 and
rolling into Europe that September. A house does not build a twenty-year fragrance pillar and rotate
the face inside twelve months. "Armani is selling a bottle with his face on it" is far more likely to
survive the window than its present tense deserves. I have downgraded that concern accordingly rather
than reporting the risk I went in expecting.

Unpleasantly: the draft's own COHESION PASS ledger anticipated the double-ending problem and the
age-seam problem and logged both as "Left for the writer," but no ledger in the draft anticipates
temporal decay at all. Three ledgers track contrast-pairs, heading mix, and cross-draft similarity.
None tracks expiry. For a corpus of 489 evergreen pages, that is a missing instrument, not a missing
sentence.

## Red flags

### FUTURE-B1 — Six relative time markers with no absolute anchor, concentrated in the cold open and the closer

**Blocker.**

**Passages.** Closer, first line:

> "He is on screen this year regardless of the quitting headlines."

Same paragraph:

> "Anthony sat out the first half of _Bridgerton_'s fourth season in January and returned in February"
> "He has spent his quietest year on record doing the least photogenic work available to a movie star,
> which is hiring."

Cold open:

> "He is thirty-eight now."
> "Since June he has been The MARTINI Man"

Dating section:

> "Last November, after a viral press clip with his _Wicked_ co-star [Ariana Grande](...)"

**Reader effect / trust problem.** Every one of these is correct on 21 August 2026 and silently
re-points on a known date. "He is thirty-eight now" is false from 25 April 2027 — four months inside
the window. "This year," "in January," "in February," and "his quietest year on record" all re-point
to 2027 for a reader who arrives in 2027, and 2027 is a year in which Bailey is doing materially
different things. "Since June" re-points to June 2027. "Last November" re-points to November 2026,
which is not the November the sentence describes.

The compounding effect is what makes this a blocker rather than housekeeping. A reader who catches one
stale marker starts auditing the rest, and the markers are densest in the cold open — before the
article has earned any credit. The page stops reading as analysis and starts reading as an un-updated
scrape.

**Evidence.** Birth date 25 April 1988 (packet timeline, S-17). MARTINI announced 9 June 2026 (S-16).
The viral Grande clip is the November 2025 NYT Cooking appearance (S-20, S-21) — I checked this
specifically because "last November" from an August 2026 vantage could plausibly have meant the
November 2024 _Wicked_ junket; the packet confirms the 2025 referent, so the sentence is accurate
today and wrong-by-drift tomorrow. Bridgerton S4 part one 29 January 2026, part two 26 February 2026
(S-18, S-19).

**Minimum viable repair.** Replace each relative marker with an absolute one. "He is thirty-eight
now" → an age stated against a year, or cut the age (the sentence works without it). "this year" →
"in 2026". "in January and returned in February" → "in January 2026 and returned that February".
"Since June" → "Since June 2026". "Last November" → "In November 2025". "his quietest year on record"
→ "his quietest year on record, 2026". No new sourcing required; every date is already in the packet
timeline.

**Expected benefit.** The page survives to 2027 and beyond without an edit, and a future refresh has
explicit anchors to update rather than ambiguous ones to hunt.

**Confidence.** High. These are arithmetic facts about the calendar, not judgements.

**Acceptance test.** Grep the reader-visible body for `\b(this|last|next) (year|month|November|
January|February|June)\b`, `\bnow\b` adjacent to a number, and `\bSince (January|...|December)\b` not
followed by a four-digit year. Zero hits outside verbatim quotation. Then read the cold open and the
closer with the system clock set to 2027-08-21 and confirm no sentence is false.

### FUTURE-B2 — An evergreen "what's next" heading answered with a status snapshot that expires inside the window

**Blocker.**

**Passages.** Heading:

> "## What Is Jonathan Bailey Doing Next? Sondheim at the Barbican"

Section body:

> "In summer 2027 he goes back to the Barbican to play Georges in _Sunday in the Park with George_"
> "The show opens as planned. Bailey is still Georges."

**Reader effect / trust problem.** The heading is a permanent question; the answer has a date on it.
Summer 2027 falls inside the twelve-month review window — by 21 August 2027 the production has opened,
been postponed, or been pulled, and in every one of those branches "In summer 2027 he goes back"
(future tense) and "The show opens as planned" (present-tense forward status) are wrong tense at
minimum and wrong fact at worst. A reader who typed "what is jonathan bailey doing next" lands on a
heading that promises currency and gets an answer about a thing that already happened.

"The show opens as planned. Bailey is still Georges." is additionally a claim about the future stated
as settled fact with no as-of qualifier. It is accurately sourced — Variety's own headline is
"Producers Confirm Summer 2027 Show Will Go On 'As Planned' After Ariana Grande Exits" — but a
producer's forward assurance is not the same class of fact as a review that ran or a show that closed,
and the draft renders both in the same flat register. The packet also records that ticket sales for
this production had already slipped to autumn 2026 (S-30, Deadline, Jul 2026), which is a schedule
already in motion; the draft does not mention it.

**Evidence.** Barbican listing, summer 2027, dir. Marianne Elliott (S-12). Grande withdrawal 2 August
2026, production confirmed to proceed (S-10, S-27, S-31; corroborated by Variety and Just Jared in my
own search). Ticket-sale delay to autumn 2026 (S-30).

**Minimum viable repair.** Two changes. (1) Date-stamp the status rather than asserting it: "As of
August 2026 the show is scheduled to open in summer 2027, with Bailey as Georges." (2) Change the
heading so it does not promise perpetual currency — "Jonathan Bailey's Next Role: Sondheim at the
Barbican" keeps the search intent ("jonathan bailey next role" is the query family the HEADING MIX
ledger cites) while surviving the show's opening, because a role remains "his next role" in
retrospect far more gracefully than a question survives its own answer.

**Expected benefit.** The section ages into a record of what was planned rather than decaying into a
stale answer under a live question, and the as-of stamp makes the next refresh a one-line edit.

**Confidence.** High on the tense and status problem. Medium on the heading rewrite specifically — an
SEO reviewer may have a better construction, and I am diagnosing the requirement, not fixing the copy.

**Acceptance test.** Read the section with the clock at 2027-08-21 under three scenarios: show opened
on time, show postponed, show cancelled. Under all three, no sentence should be factually false; at
most one should read as "here is what was planned in 2026." If any sentence is false under any of the
three, the repair is not done.

### FUTURE-C1 — "She is ninety-five now" asserts a present-tense age from a nine-month-old source

**Concern.** Childhood section:

> "She is ninety-five now, and he has checked the story with her, because he does not quite believe it
> either."

The packet verifies the age verbatim (CLM-15) — but from the People SMA interview of **November 2025**,
nine months before this draft's publication date. "Now" is doing work the source cannot support: unless
her birthday falls in a narrow window, Bailey's grandmother is already ninety-six on 21 August 2026,
and she is ninety-six or ninety-seven by the review horizon. This is the one temporal claim in the
draft that may already be imprecise at publication rather than merely heading that way.

There is a second reason to fix it that has nothing to do with arithmetic. For a named living relative
in her mid-nineties, a present-tense "she is" can go wrong in a way that is painful rather than merely
inaccurate, and this page is designed to sit unattended for years.

**Minimum viable repair.** Bind the age to its source moment: "She was ninety-five when he told the
story in 2025." One word of tense and one date.

**Expected benefit.** The sentence becomes permanently true and stops carrying mortality risk.

**Confidence.** High on the sourcing gap; medium on whether she is already ninety-six (her birth date
is not in the packet, so the exact flip date is unknown — which is itself the argument for the repair).

**Acceptance test.** No un-dated present-tense age claim about any third party survives in the body.

### FUTURE-C2 — The Sexiest Man Alive superlative is present tense and gets superseded three months after publication

**Concern.** Cold open:

> "By at least one official measure he is also the most desirable man on the planet. People named him
> its 2025 Sexiest Man Alive"

The second sentence is correctly dated and permanently true. The first is present tense and depends on
Bailey holding a title that People re-awards every November. A successor is named around November 2026
— three months after publication — and by the review horizon the 2025 title is a full cycle stale. A
2027 reader reads "he is the most desirable man on the planet" against a page that itself supplies the
2025 date two sentences later, and the mismatch is visible without leaving the paragraph.

**Minimum viable repair.** Move the tense into the past or into the year: "In 2025, by at least one
official measure, he was the most desirable man on the planet." The rhetorical effect of the line
survives the tense change intact.

**Expected benefit.** The cold open's biggest status claim stops expiring on a schedule.

**Confidence.** High. The annual cycle is the whole premise of the award.

**Acceptance test.** The claim reads true with the clock at 2027-08-21 and at 2030-08-21.

### FUTURE-C3 — Four unsourced "most bookable" superlatives, none dated

**Concern.** Four instances:

> TL;DR: "a single accounting figure sent the world's most bookable actor into a year of hiring staff"
> Prompt book: "with _Bridgerton_ making him the most bookable romantic lead on television"
> Year-off section: "The most bookable man in the industry described his sabbatical as hiring."
> Closer: "the most bookable romantic lead on Netflix was off making _Jurassic World Rebirth_"

"Most bookable" is a market-position claim with no source behind it in the packet and no date attached
to any of the four uses. Market position is exactly the kind of fact that moves. It is also in mild
tension with the piece's own reporting — a man who has publicly stepped back from acting for a year is
an odd fit for a present-tense industry superlative, and by 2027 the claim is being made about someone
who has spent the intervening period staffing a nonprofit.

**Minimum viable repair.** Keep at most two of the four, and bind each to its moment ("in 2023,
_Bridgerton_ had made him..." / "while season four was shooting in 2024 and 2025..."). The other two
are repetition, and the closer's instance is already doing scheduling work that does not need the
superlative.

**Expected benefit.** Removes an undated market claim from four positions and reduces a repetition the
FRESH EYES review flagged in a different register.

**Confidence.** Medium-high. The claim is defensible as characterisation; the present tense and the
repetition are the actual defects.

**Acceptance test.** No present-tense industry-ranking superlative appears without a year attached.

### FUTURE-C4 — Brand-ambassador relationships stated in an open-ended present tense

**Concern.** Cold open and closer:

> "He is the global face of Giorgio Armani's _I Will_"
> "Since June he has been The MARTINI Man"
> "Armani is selling a bottle with his face on it and a promise for a name"

Ambassadorships end, and the draft states three of them in a tense with no endpoint. My research
substantially reduced this risk rather than confirming it: _I Will_ is the house's first new masculine
pillar in more than twenty years, launching in the US on 15 August 2026 with a European rollout that
September, and no brand retires the face of a twenty-year pillar inside twelve months. MARTINI is
confirmed multi-year in the packet (S-16). So both relationships very probably survive the window.

The residual defect is therefore narrower than I expected: it is the undated "Since June" (covered in
FUTURE-B1) and the open-ended tense, not an imminent factual break. Worth a light touch, not a rewrite.

**Minimum viable repair.** Date the start of each relationship once ("Since June 2026", "signed in
August 2026") and leave the present tense, which is then self-limiting for the reader.

**Expected benefit.** A 2027 reader can tell how old the relationship is and calibrate accordingly.

**Confidence.** Medium — deliberately downgraded from my starting hypothesis on the strength of the
launch-scale evidence.

**Acceptance test.** Every commercial relationship in the body has a start date within one sentence of
its first mention.

### FUTURE-C5 — Present-tense interiority extrapolated from a record that stops in November 2025

**Concern.** The packet is explicit about this and it is the deepest durability issue in the piece:

> "No 2026 first-person source exists. The most recent substantial Bailey interview located anywhere is
> November 2025." (Research limitation 5)
> "The draft's present-tense framing ('He is thirty-eight now,' 'his quietest year on record') rests on
> 2026 _commercial_ activity plus 2025 _interviews_." (Dispute 11)

At publication the draft is extrapolating his present state roughly nine months past its newest
first-person source. At the review horizon it is extrapolating twenty-one months. And the gap will not
stay quiet: he has a Barbican production to promote, an Armani European rollout, and a MARTINI
multi-year deal, so a substantial 2027 press cycle is close to certain. When it arrives it will speak
directly to the questions this piece leaves open — the year off, what the hiring felt like, whether the
fence held.

This does not make the analysis wrong. It means the piece's present tense is its most perishable
register, and the refresh trigger is not a calendar date but an event: the next long-form interview.

**Minimum viable repair.** No copy change is strictly required. Add the trigger to the refresh list
below so the next interview is treated as a scheduled update rather than a surprise, and resist adding
any further present-tense interior claims (the SECOND PASS notes already correctly refused a second
`inner-thought` in the sabbatical section on exactly this ground — that judgement was right and should
hold).

**Expected benefit.** The piece's interpretive claims stay proportional to a record that is aging.

**Confidence.** High — this is the packet's own finding, not mine.

**Acceptance test.** No sentence asserts what Bailey currently feels, wants, or believes without a
quotation carrying a date.

### FUTURE-C6 — "Complete understanding and support" is the single most supersedable citation in the draft

**Concern.** Barbican section:

> "Empire Street Productions said the decision came with their complete understanding."

This is accurately reported and correctly attributed to the producer. The durability problem is that
producer statements issued within twenty-four hours of a star's exit are the class of claim most
frequently revised by later reporting, and the packet already holds a pointer in the other direction:
The Hollywood Reporter ran "Ariana Grande's Decision to Withdraw… Came as Surprise to Key Production
Members" (S-13). The packet is scrupulous that this exists as a headline only, with the body blocked by
tollbit, and it is right that a headline cannot carry a correction. But twelve months is ample time for
that body — or a longer post-mortem once a replacement Dot is announced — to become readable and
contradict the line the draft carries.

**Minimum viable repair.** Attribute the framing rather than adopting it: "Empire Street Productions
said the decision came with their complete understanding, though the trade reporting was not
unanimous." One clause, no new source required, and the sentence survives whichever way the story
lands.

**Expected benefit.** The draft cannot be caught having taken a PR statement at face value.

**Confidence.** Medium-high. The direction of the risk is documented; the magnitude is not.

**Acceptance test.** No producer or publicist statement about a live dispute is rendered in the draft's
own voice.

### FUTURE-C7 — The confirmed-and-pending Dot recasting is never mentioned

**Concern.** The Barbican section reports Grande's exit and stops there. But the recasting is not
speculative — it was confirmed by the producers on the same day, and the packet records it twice:

> "Reporting confirms **Bailey remains cast as Georges**; Dot to be recast" (S-11, TheaterMania,
> 3 Aug 2026)
> "Venue, summer 2027, Elliott, Tom Scutt design, Bailey as Georges, **Dot to be cast**" (S-12,
> Barbican listing)

My own search confirms the producers stated the role will be recast with casting "announced in due
course," and that no replacement had been named as of publication. A named Dot is very likely inside
the twelve-month window — a summer 2027 opening effectively requires it. When that announcement lands,
this section reads as though Grande's absence is the standing state of the production, which it will
not be.

**Minimum viable repair.** One clause: "…with the role to be recast." It costs nothing, it is sourced
twice in the packet, and it converts a soon-to-be-stale omission into a correctly incomplete statement.

**Expected benefit.** The section stays accurate through the recasting announcement instead of being
invalidated by it.

**Confidence.** High. Two independent packet sources plus my own confirmation.

**Acceptance test.** The section states that Dot is to be recast, and reads correctly after a
replacement is announced.

### FUTURE-C8 — A thesis-level landing built on a nineteen-day-old event about a third party

**Concern.** Final paragraph before the Rabbit Hole:

> "The two of them spent the same year being looked at more than almost anyone alive, on the same press
> tour, for the same film. When the Barbican came round, one of them stepped back from visibility and
> the other kept the date. Bailey built his fence at thirteen, out of a leotard and a look, and it is
> the reason he can still stand in the light without it costing him the thing behind it."

This is the piece using a current event as evidence for an enduring pattern, in the position where
argumentative weight should be lowest. The Grande withdrawal was nineteen days old at publication — the
draft's own FRESH EYES note calls it "three weeks old" and, correctly, "the biggest live miss" to have
omitted. Including it was right. Making it carry the second-strongest thesis statement in the article
is the durability error.

Three ways it decays. If Grande returns to the production, the contrast inverts. If Bailey's own
schedule moves, the contrast inverts against him. And if her hiatus develops into a longer or more
serious story, a paragraph that uses a woman's withdrawal-under-scrutiny as the foil for a man's
resilience ages badly in tone as well as in fact — a risk that grows rather than shrinks with time,
because the reader in 2027 will know how her story went and this paragraph will not.

**Minimum viable repair.** Keep the reporting; demote the argument. Let the section land on the
Barbican bookend, which is thirty-two years deep and cannot expire (RSC at seven → Georges at
thirty-nine, Elliott, _Company_), and let the Grande contrast sit as reported fact ahead of it rather
than as the closing proof. The fence thesis has already been proven twice by then — at thirteen and in
the dating section — and does not need a third-party event to carry it.

**Expected benefit.** The article's argument stops depending on a news cycle it does not control, and
the section's ending survives any branch of the Grande story.

**Confidence.** High on the structural point. Medium on the tone risk, which is a forecast.

**Acceptance test.** Delete the Grande paragraph entirely; the section must still have a thesis-level
landing. If deleting it leaves a hole, the weight is still misplaced.

### FUTURE-C9 — The piece's central critical question has a scheduled resolution date it does not flag

**Concern.** Richard II section:

> "Wolf himself allowed that the depth might arrive later in the run. That is the whole question about
> Bailey in one clause: the toolkit is not in doubt, the arrival time is."

This is the best sentence in the draft and I want it kept exactly as written. But the piece then, two
sections later, books the event that answers it: a Sondheim lead at the Barbican in summer 2027, under
the director who gave him his Olivier, in a musical about a man whose absorption in his work costs him
the woman posing for it. That is the grief register the critics said had not arrived, on the largest
stage available to him, inside the review window.

The article sets up a falsifiable prediction and does not notice it has scheduled its own test. That is
a missed opportunity now and a refresh trigger later: the _Sunday in the Park with George_ reviews will
either confirm or overturn the central critical claim of this piece.

**Minimum viable repair.** One sentence of connective tissue in the Barbican section pointing back —
the arrival-time question gets its answer in summer 2027 — plus the refresh trigger logged below.
This is the same move the COHESION PASS already made successfully between the dating and Richard II
sections.

**Expected benefit.** Two strong sections compound instead of sitting adjacent, and the piece gains a
genuine forward stake rather than a status report.

**Confidence.** Medium-high. The connection is real; whether the writer wants it is an editorial call.

**Acceptance test.** A reader can state what would prove the article's critical claim wrong, and when.

## Specific improvements

Ordered by cost-to-benefit. The first four are mechanical and together retire both blockers.

1. **Insert absolute years at six sites** (FUTURE-B1). Cold open ×2, dating section ×1, closer ×3. All
   dates already in the packet timeline. ~15 minutes. Retires blocker 1.
2. **Date-stamp the Barbican status and adjust the heading** (FUTURE-B2). "As of August 2026…" plus a
   heading that survives its own answer. Retires blocker 2.
3. **Add "with the role to be recast"** (FUTURE-C7). One clause, sourced twice.
4. **Bind the grandmother's age to 2025** (FUTURE-C1). One tense change, one date.
5. **Move the SMA superlative into the past tense** (FUTURE-C2).
6. **Attribute the "complete understanding" framing** (FUTURE-C6). One clause.
7. **Demote the Grande contrast from thesis-carrier to reported fact** (FUTURE-C8), letting the
   Barbican bookend close the section.
8. **Thin "most bookable" from four uses to two, both dated** (FUTURE-C3).
9. **Add the arrival-time callback in the Barbican section** (FUTURE-C9) — optional, highest upside.

Note on word count: items 1–6 add roughly 25–35 words to a body the COHESION PASS records at 4,089,
already past blog-lint's 4,050 warn line. Item 7 removes more than that. Sequencing 7 before 1–6 keeps
the piece under the ceiling without touching the mic-drop endings the FRESH EYES review flagged.
