> "People may think there's some hidden business logic behind this, but it's mainly driven by curiosity." — Liang Wenfeng, on buying 10,000 Nvidia GPUs, _36Kr Waves_ interview, 2023 (Zihan Wang translation, ChinaTalk)

<p class="firstLetter">Yan Junjie got to the restaurant early. A young man in a T-shirt was already at the table, and before the food arrived he was asking Yan detailed technical questions, one after another. Yan, who runs the AI company MiniMax, assumed he was talking to somebody's assistant, a sharp one. After half an hour he asked when Mr. Liang would be arriving. "I am Liang Wenfeng," the man said. (Yan's account, relayed by investor Zhang Jinjian in LatePost, republished by Tencent News, January 2026)</p>

That dinner was in early 2023, before anyone outside Chinese finance knew the name. Liang Wenfeng had already built High-Flyer, one of China's largest quant hedge funds, and was months from founding DeepSeek. By July 2026 the Bloomberg Billionaires Index put his fortune near $36 billion and called him the richest person alive who has built an AI model. DeepSeek, which he still controls, had closed its first outside funding round at a valuation near $50 billion and begun preparing a 2027 stock listing (Bloomberg, July 14, 2026).

Then, on July 23, a 42-page transcript leaked, claiming to record Liang talking to prospective investors for nearly four hours in May. The lines that went viral were blunt: China is about two years behind the United States on roughly one-twentieth of the compute, and DeepSeek still runs almost entirely on Nvidia. Two days later Bloomberg reported that he had told the next set of backers, lined up to put in another 50 billion yuan, that they would not be signing in the coming days. The round stayed frozen for eleven days.

The same transcript holds the sentence people now quote back at him: "Restraint is a strategy." (Tencent News edit of the purported May 2026 recording) DeepSeek has never confirmed the document; an investor in the company told National Business Daily the meeting happened and the content was credible. Read whole, it describes a lab that prices its product to pay back its hardware and declines to charge a yuan more, run by a man who says money "is definitely not a problem" (Tencent News transcript, 2026).

A quant fund exists to find mispriced things and take the difference. Liang got rich doing exactly that. Then he built the most mispriced asset in his industry, a frontier model sold for a fraction of what buyers would pay, and walked away from the difference. The explanation starts with a stamp album in a Wuchuan primary school.

## What is Liang Wenfeng's personality type?

### Liang Wenfeng is an Enneagram Type 5

The type that fits Liang Wenfeng is the Enneagram Type 5, the Investigator, and the cleanest evidence is his answer when a reporter asked what excites him most about building DeepSeek: "Verifying whether our hypotheses are correct. If they are, that's immensely satisfying." (36Kr, 2023) Most founders answer that question with a market. He answered it with an experiment.

A Five feels safe in proportion to how much he has figured out, and guards whatever the figuring requires: time, attention, and in Liang's case, compute. Asked by 36Kr in 2023 why a hedge fund had stockpiled 10,000 GPUs, he recited the count like a lab notebook, from "a single card in the early days" to "10,000 cards," and then gave the explanation at the top of this page.

Luo Yonghao, the entrepreneur and talk-show host, met him in a hotel lobby and came away with the same reading from the outside. Liang, he said in June 2025, "carries enormous curiosity and very seriously chases a question to the end," with a quality "common in students but hard to see in adults." Liang asks "very directly, never circles," and has not been "domesticated" by the high-EQ social style that usually arrives with money (澎湃新闻 / SCMP, June 2025).

What makes him a distinctive Five is what he did with the wealth. Most people who get rich expand. He used the money to need less. "I only want to eat one piece, only the piece I'm best at," he told investors in the purported May 2026 transcript (Tencent News edit, 2026). No overtime, "because it's just not that hard." No sales staff for the API; "users come on their own." No finished consumer products: "we haven't gone to fill them in. This is also a culture of ours." Every appetite the industry runs on, he treats as a leak in the system.

That is half of the quant puzzle. A quant exploits mispricing because the money is the point. Liang built the fund to afford the experiment, and once the experiment was funded, taking more from the market turned into a cost: more products to maintain, more people to manage, more attention pulled off the one question he cares about. Few founders have said it as plainly as the same transcript has him say it: "Those who take more will be defeated by those who take less." (Tencent News edit, 2026) The other half is in the same document, and it does not flatter the thesis. It can wait. The first half starts with the stamp album.

## Liang Wenfeng's childhood in Wuchuan: two teachers, a stamp album, and one school on the form

Liang Wenfeng was born in 1985 in Mililing, a village attached to Wuchuan, a county-level city on the Guangdong coast that he calls "fifth-tier." Both parents taught primary school; his father was deputy director of teaching at Meilu Primary, his mother taught at a school in a neighboring township (吴川报, 2002; 极目新闻, 2025).

The one childhood story he has told in public is about other people's parents. "In the 1990s, there were many opportunities to make money in Guangdong," he said in 2024. "At that time, many parents came to my home; basically, they thought studying was useless." (36Kr Waves, July 2024) "Looking back now," he added, "they've all changed their views."

His sixth-grade teacher, Mr. Li, remembers a boy who built model planes and boats after school and collected stamps by a method the teacher still describes with precision: "using stamps to fund stamps." He sold what he received, bought back in, and "barely spent his father's money." (湛江云媒, January 2025) A child who wanted something and designed a loop so that nobody would have to give it to him.

By middle school the maths had run out. His homeroom teacher, Mr. Rong, told reporters that Liang "finished high-school maths in middle school and had already started university maths," and did it without strain: "quiet but no bookworm ... as if he could master every subject without spending much time." (羊城晚报, January 2025)

In 2002 he scored 806 on Guangdong's 900-point scale, the top result in Wuchuan. His final-year teacher, Yang Yahong, remembers the application form better than the score. Students listed several universities. Liang listed one, Zhejiang, and when she asked whether he was sure, answered with a single word: "Certain." (羊城晚报, July 2026)

A classmate, writing pseudonymously on Zhihu years later, recalled that he skipped most lectures because the professors were slower than the textbook, and cycled across several provinces alone, sleeping on the ground, "without spending much money."

Self-taught, self-funded, self-transported. He finished a master's in 2010 with a thesis on tracking moving targets with cheap cameras. Then he disappeared.

## The cheap flat in Chengdu where Liang Wenfeng failed before he got rich

The 2008 financial crisis hit during his graduate years, and he began running trading experiments while markets fell. After graduating, while classmates joined Tencent and Alibaba, he moved into what the 36Kr interviewer called "a cheap rental in Chengdu" and spent the next few years applying AI to one field after another and failing at each (36Kr Waves, 2023). A friend building "unreliable" flying devices in a Shenzhen urban village asked him to join; he declined, and the friend's company became DJI (36Kr, 2023, from Liang's own telling).

Finance was the field where the failures stopped. In 2013 he and a classmate, Xu Jin, registered a small investment firm in Hangzhou (Sina, 2025). High-Flyer followed in 2015, and by 2021 it managed more than 100 billion yuan.

It was a strange kind of fund. High-Flyer sold directly and set no quotas. "We don't have KPIs or so-called quotas," Liang said in 2023, years before that line went viral.

The fund also did something quant funds rarely do. In December 2021, after its worst drawdown on record, High-Flyer published an apology: "We feel deeply guilty." The letter blamed its own AI, which, "when market style switches violently," had "tended to take bigger risks to chase more return" (Tencent News, December 2021). The machine had done the one thing its owner refuses to do. It reached for more, and the firm said sorry for it in public. The lab it announced sixteen months later already had the hardware to run on.

"If purely for quant investing, even a small number of GPUs would suffice," he admitted in 2023. The real purpose was a question: whether there are "simpler ways to express" the entire financial market, and where the edges of such models lie. "It's like someone buying a piano for a home," he said. "First, they can afford it, and second, such a group of people are eager to play beautiful music on it."

Even the extravagance was frugal. "NVIDIA GPUs hold their value well," he noted, "and older cards still find buyers." (36Kr, 2023) The stamps had become chips.

In April 2023 High-Flyer announced it would build large models, quoting Truffaut: "desperately ambitious, desperately sincere." DeepSeek was registered that July with 3 billion yuan of the fund's own money. The hypothesis had a name. "AGI may be one of the next most challenging frontiers," he said that spring, "so for us, the question is not 'why' but 'how'." Asked for the business case anyway, he gave the least reassuring answer a founder can give: "It might be hard if we must find a commercial justification, because it's not cost-effective." (36Kr, 2023)

He staffed the lab the way he had studied. Core technical roles, he said in 2023, go mostly to recent graduates, and explained why: "Experienced people will tell you how something should be done without hesitation, while those without experience will explore repeatedly, think carefully, and find a solution that fits the current situation." (36Kr, 2023) Zihan Wang, who joined as one of those graduates, described what the money bought from the inside: compute and freedom to experiment, "a luxury that few fresh graduates would get at any company." (MIT Technology Review, January 2025)

## How DeepSeek accidentally became a catfish, and what the critics get right

Less than a year old, in May 2024, DeepSeek released V2 and priced it at one yuan per million input tokens, a fraction of what ByteDance, Alibaba, Baidu, and Tencent were charging. Within weeks all four cut prices (Forbes, January 2025). Chinese media called DeepSeek the industry's catfish, the fish dropped into the tank to make the others swim.

"We didn't mean to become a catfish," Liang told 36Kr that summer. "We just accidentally became a catfish." He had not expected "pricing to be so sensitive to everyone." The principle was arithmetic, "we don't subsidize nor make exorbitant profits," and the model had simply become cheaper to run.

Eight months later R1 shipped, and on January 27, 2025, Nvidia lost about $589 billion of market value, the largest one-day drop for any company in history (CNBC). Liang spent that afternoon in Wuchuan, playing football with his old classmates, according to his middle-school teacher (湛江发布, January 2025). On the day the market repriced his industry, he was on a pitch with people who had known him at fourteen, the one place the number did not follow.

A week earlier, on the day R1 went live, he had spoken at Premier Li Qiang's symposium and, per the Wall Street Journal, told the premier that chip export controls remained a bottleneck. Three weeks after the crash he was in the room again, this time for Xi Jinping's meeting with private entrepreneurs. The state broadcast shows him at the far end of the long table, the same end as Jack Ma, while Ren Zhengfei, Lei Jun, and four others took turns addressing the president (CGTN / CNBC, February 17, 2025). On camera, the most talked-about founder on earth that month said nothing.

Others had plenty to say about him, and the strongest skeptic deserves a fair hearing. At Davos, [Alexandr Wang](/personality-analysis/alexandr-wang) of Scale AI said on CNBC: "My understanding is that DeepSeek has 50,000 H100s. They can't talk about it obviously because it's against the export controls." Elon Musk replied with one word: "Obviously." TIME's Harry Booth later wrote that the famous $6 million training figure "ignored the roughly $1.6 billion" DeepSeek had spent assembling its cluster, and called the founder's "formidable self-mythologizing" a market-mover in its own right (TIME, August 2025).

Concede the core of it. The $5.6 million was one training run, stated as such in the V3 paper; High-Flyer had spent about a billion yuan on Fire-Flyer 2 alone. The purported 2026 transcript has him telling investors "we can buy some non-compliant cards." A man who presents himself as restrained was, on the evidence, one of the most aggressive accumulators of GPUs in China. Those facts hold, and no personality lens dissolves them. [Dario Amodei](/personality-analysis/dario-amodei), who cited DeepSeek in arguing for tighter export controls, still described its people as "smart, curious researchers who just want to make useful technology" (January 2025). Both can be true of the same lab.

What the lens adds is the shape of the appetite, and the shape is a split. V3, R1, and now V4 have all shipped with open weights under an MIT license, the thing most labs guard, and he has explained the giveaway as something other than business. "Open source is more of a cultural behavior than a commercial one, and contributing to it earns us respect." (36Kr, July 2024) He does not hide his wealth, his pricing, or his opinion of China's lag either. What he has never published is the number of cards in the building, the only asset that cannot be bought back once it is taken away. He publishes the map and hides the engine, and nobody has ever had to hand him either one.

The behavior people mock most, the price-slashing that wrecked rivals' margins and the candor that froze his own round, runs on the same engine, and he has described the feeling under it in his own words. For thirty years, he said, Chinese tech had been "lying at home waiting 18 months for better hardware and software to emerge," used to "Moore's Law falling out of the sky," free-riding on work it had never joined. "The real gap is the difference between originality and imitation." (36Kr, 2024) Cheap tokens and blunt assessments are what it looks like when a man who spent his twenties failing in a rented room decides the imitation stops with him. The premier heard it. The investors heard it. Nobody gets the flattering version.

## Four of the 118 points: what the leaked Liang Wenfeng transcript says about restraint

What the investors heard, in the Tencent News technology desk's edit of the purported May 20 recording, is organized as 118 numbered remarks. No audio has surfaced and DeepSeek has not confirmed the document; four of the points do the most work.

**#4 and #46. "Actually, we have no organization."** The company, he says, is "vision-driven, organized by a vision," and "this vision isn't even written down." Bottom-up, "each person does whatever they want to do themselves, no one manages them, no KPIs."

**#11. "Restraint is a strategy."** The fuller line: "Sometimes you can give up some things to exchange for more of other things." His example was the previous year's user war. He declined to spend against ByteDance because "there are still watermelons behind, and what's in front is maybe just sesame seeds."

**#37. "Our greatest core interest is maintaining the stability of the team."** Then, for emphasis, this "can even be considered our only core interest." Money, a point later, "is definitely not a problem, resources aren't a problem." Read that beside #42, where he says the gap with America "is mainly in resources, and the gap in people isn't very big," and the fear under the restraint shows. Cards can be rebought. A fortune can be remade. The people who hold the unwritten vision in their heads cannot be, and from July 23 the document that says so, in his own words, was public.

**#91 to #93. The price.** DeepSeek's API price is set so that a batch of servers pays for itself in ten months. He volunteered that this is "actually not profit-maximizing": demand is inelastic in that range, so doubling the price would barely dent consumption. Early on, worried about demand, they set the price high, "and people on the team weren't very happy. Later I brought the price back down, down to a quarter, and everyone was happy." When prices fall, he said, "many people in the company group cheered."

Mid-session, the transcript has him asking whether anyone wants a break. "Let's rest for five minutes. Let me write quickly." Someone types a message on screen: a ten-month payback is too much profit. He agrees. "Indeed there's still room for price cuts."

<p class="inner-thought">They flew here to hand me fifty billion yuan, and the message on the screen says charge less. Good. Those are the ones to keep.</p>

He had said so out loud: the round was "carefully selected" for backers "with the least hostility toward us," because "not everyone hopes we can succeed."

When the document leaked in July, one more line traveled with the ones about the gap: DeepSeek could not train the largest models "even if we spent all 50 billion." By August 5 the round was back on, at a valuation near $74 billion (Bloomberg, August 6). The truth cost eleven days and no money at all. He had already priced it.

A fifth point, the other half of the puzzle left open above. In 2023 the man explaining a 10,000-GPU purchase said it was "mainly driven by curiosity." In the purported 2026 transcript the same man describes his habit of mind this way: "When I want to do something, my habitual thinking is: what has the greatest return for me to do at this moment?" Three years and roughly $36 billion sit between those two sentences. Either curiosity is the motive and return-on-attention is the discipline that guards it, or the second sentence is what the first one turns into once the experiment starts paying. The test is simple. If the API price goes up after the IPO, the restraint was a phase.

## Why nobody can find Liang Wenfeng's personal life

Until then, the record to test him against is thin. No video interview with Liang Wenfeng exists. The clips on YouTube labeled as interviews are synthetic voices reading the two 36Kr transcripts, and the "leaked audio" of the investor meeting is an AI reconstruction. In thirteen years as a founder he has sat for two long interviews, both with the same reporter.

Searches for his wife, his children, or his marriage return content farms, some of which have confused him with a snooker player named Liang Wenbo. No reputable outlet has established whether he is married. A fellow villager says that when he comes home "he generally does not go out much" (极目新闻, 2025), except, apparently, for football.

What fills the days is on record. Colleagues quoted by Chinese financial media say he has no hobbies other than programming (YOUNG财经, 2025), and the 36Kr reporter who sat with him in 2024 described his workday as "reading papers, writing code, and participating in group discussions." The man who controls the company still writes code for it. Bloomberg Businessweek described him up close: "prone to faltering speech and prolonged silences," a man new hires "learn quickly not to mistake" for timid, because after the silence come "precise, hard-to-answer questions about model architecture, computing costs" (May 2025).

Zihan Wang, asked on a podcast whether DeepSeek would keep releasing its weights, gave the only explanation anyone close to the lab has offered in public for the restraint. "Based on my naive opinion, I think he doesn't want to make money," Wang said. "Because he has made enough." (Manifold, May 2025) As of August 2026 that is about $38 billion (Bloomberg, via Fortune) and roughly four-fifths of a company headed for the stock market. He has not commented on the figures.

## The watermelons behind Liang Wenfeng

The transcript contains one remark that reads as an accidental self-portrait. "One strange thing is, the thing you most want to get, you can't get. Whereas the thing you don't care much about, is actually fairly easy to get." He meant users. Chinese internet giants fought "bloody battles" for consumer attention in 2024, and the traffic went to the company that did not want it. "The users just couldn't be driven away."

The fortune arrived the same way. He built a fund to pay for chips, chips to test a hypothesis, and a lab that charges ten months of hardware and no more. The market, unable to find the price he declined to set, set it for him: $36 billion, richest AI-model founder alive.

The IPO will hand him a number that updates every morning, the kind of thing he has spent his life declining to want. What he does want, the watermelon behind all of it, he named once, in 2023, as a frontier where the question was "how" and never "why," and has otherwise only ever called a hypothesis to verify. And by the rule he gave his investors, the thing you most want is the one you can't get.
