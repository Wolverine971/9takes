> "Therefore I do penance, am wroth with myself, am sad, compare myself with others, starve myself."
>
> — Marcus Aurelius to his tutor Fronto c. 146 AD, Loeb Classical Library vol. 1, 1919

<p class="firstLetter">In 162 AD the emperor of Rome took four days off at Alsium, a seaside town up the coast from the capital. When he got back, he wrote to his old tutor and refused to say how he had spent them. Both letters survive in the Loeb Classical Library edition of the Fronto correspondence, 1920.</p>

"In what holiday-wise we have kept our holiday at Alsium I will not put on paper, that you may not be yourself troubled and scold me, my master."

Fronto scolded him anyway. His 162 AD reply, in the same Loeb Classical Library volume, drops the mock innocence fast.

"It were easier to reconcile you to a polecat than to pleasure. Tell me, Marcus, I beseech you, have you repaired to Alsium only to fast with the sea in sight? What, could you not wear yourself out at Lorium with hunger and thirst and doing business?"

The man being teased in that letter, one of the funniest documents to survive from the second century, is the one whose face now sells composure on a million book covers. His private notebook, **Meditations**, is the most durable self-help text in the Western canon, and the quality everyone buys it for is calm.

His own teacher could not talk him into a beach.

The easy version of this story would be hypocrisy, and it is not available. Marcus Aurelius taught nobody, built no school, published no doctrine, and addressed his notebook to a single reader. The discipline ran inward, with no audience to impress and no rule he could be caught breaking.

So the question is what it was aimed at.

## Who Marcus Aurelius Was, and How Rome Picked Him Twice

He was born in Rome on 26 April 121, into a wealthy senatorial family, and was chosen for the throne before he was old enough to want it. In January 138 the dying emperor Hadrian named Antoninus Pius his heir on one condition: that Pius adopt the sixteen-year-old Marcus, along with a boy named Lucius. The succession was arranged over his head by two men who would both be dead before he had to use it.

He got twenty-three years to prepare, educated chiefly in rhetoric under Marcus Cornelius Fronto, the most celebrated Latin orator of the age. Somewhere in there the training took a turn Fronto never fully forgave. Marcus found the Stoics, through the man he thanks for it in his notebook: "I am indebted to him for being acquainted with the discourses of Epictetus, which he communicated to me out of his own collection."

Antoninus Pius died on 7 March 161. Marcus was thirty-nine, and his first act as emperor was to refuse the job on the terms offered: he would not take office unless his adoptive brother Lucius Verus received equal powers. For the first time in its history, Rome had two emperors at once. Nothing obliged him to do it. Verus was his opposite by temperament, a man who liked chariot teams and long dinners, and Marcus split supreme power with him for eight years.

The reign that followed was one long emergency. Parthia invaded Armenia almost immediately. The army that won that war brought a pandemic home with it, and the Antonine Plague, which broke out in 165 or 166, is estimated to have killed five to ten million people. Germanic tribes crossed the Danube while the empire was still burying them. Verus died in 169. In 175 a general in Syria declared himself emperor on a false report that Marcus had died.

He died on 17 March 180, aged fifty-eight, on the northern frontier. Ancient sources disagree about whether it was at Vindobona, modern Vienna, or at Sirmium, and the dispute has never been settled.

Cassius Dio, writing close enough to the events to know people who were there, put the reign in one sentence: "he did not meet with the good fortune he deserved, for he was not strong in body and was involved in a multitude of troubles throughout practically his entire reign."

## Two Archives, One Emperor: Marcus Aurelius at 22 and at 55

That is the outside view. Almost no historical figure leaves two independent first-person records of himself thirty years apart. Marcus left exactly that, and the second one was never supposed to exist.

The first is the correspondence with Fronto, lost for over a thousand years and recovered in the nineteenth century from a reused manuscript. It catches him young, unguarded and chatty. At around nineteen he is apologising for not studying: "Aye, I can sleep, for I am a great sleeper; but it is so cold in my room that I can scarcely put my hand outside the bed-clothes." A day without a letter from his teacher is unbearable: "To me this day seems, and will seem, longer than a spring day, and the coming night more tedious than a night in winter." The register runs hotter than that in places, and what it meant is contested scholarly ground. What matters here is the temperament the discipline was applied to.

The second is the notebook, written in Greek, in his fifties, in army camps on the Danube.

## What is Marcus Aurelius's personality type?

### Marcus Aurelius is an Enneagram Type 1

He grades his own conduct against a standard he can state and cannot reach. Praise is acknowledged and changes nothing. Failure draws penance rather than excuse. And the correction goes down on paper, privately, over and over, for a decade.

That last part is what settles it. Plenty of people hold high standards. What marks a [Type 1](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-1) is the machinery underneath: the standard measures whether you are good at the root, and the marking continues in an empty room. With Marcus the room was always empty.

The Ariston letter, written at twenty-five, carries the argument on its own. Watch the sequence in it, printed at page 217 of the 1919 Loeb Classical Library edition. He reads a philosopher. The philosopher shows him "how far short" his character falls. He blushes. He gets angry, at himself. Then comes the list, and he softens none of it: penance, self-anger, sadness, comparison to others, self-starvation. A full behavioral cascade, described without a trace of self-pity, by a young man who assumed he was writing to one friend.

Now put it beside the notebook. In George Long's 1862 translation, Book 5 carries an instruction he apparently needed: "Be not disgusted, nor discouraged, nor dissatisfied" when he fails to act on principle, and, "when thou hast failed, return back again." Thirty years of practice had left disgust as the default setting; all it bought him was a faster recovery.

Book 10, in the same 1862 George Long text, states the goal without decoration: "No longer talk at all about the kind of man that a good man ought to be, but be such."

One pressure test, because this typing has an obvious rival. The anxiety in his letters looks like fear, and fear is Type 6 territory. The 143 Senate letter is the test case, and it fails the Six read cleanly. A Six under that pressure seeks reassurance and takes it when it arrives. Marcus takes the reassurance, thanks the sender warmly, and then continues prosecuting the case anyway. His worry is not that something bad will happen to him. It is that he may not deserve his own teacher.

## Why Marcus Aurelius Could Not Enjoy a Holiday

That case ran privately. Rome noticed the joylessness anyway, and found it funny.

The **Historia Augusta**, a late and famously unreliable set of imperial biographies, records a habit that is easy to picture: "It was customary with Marcus to read, listen to, and sign documents at the circus-games; because of this habit he was openly ridiculed, it is said, by the people."

That source earns all the suspicion it usually gets. What makes the detail stick is Fronto, who had no reason to invent it, complaining about the same behavior decades earlier. Around 146 he admits to gossiping about Marcus: "Time was I did this, when you went about in public gatherings with too serious a face, as when you used to read books either in the theatre or at a banquet." Two sources, a generation apart, watching him work through the show.

There is a harsher version. During his 175 revolt, the general Avidius Cassius reportedly called Marcus "a philosophical old woman" who "wishes to be called merciful and hence suffers to live men whose manner of life he cannot sanction." That line survives only through the Historia Augusta, which puts speeches in mouths, so read it as a slur someone found plausible. The same collection notes that his philosophy made him "seem harsh in his military discipline and indeed in his life in general," and that he "was bitterly assailed" for it.

Then there is the objection that outlasts all of those. In 177, Christians were tortured and killed at Lugdunum, modern Lyon, on his watch. How much he knew or ordered is contested: the only detailed account is Eusebius, writing more than a century later, and Tertullian, alive at the time, called Marcus a protector of Christians rather than a persecutor. What nobody disputes is that these deaths sit inside the reign of the philosopher on the book covers. It does not resolve, and it should not be smoothed over.

The smaller charges do resolve. Concede what the critics get right: the discipline made him worse company. Fronto, who loved him, spends an entire comic essay begging him to sleep properly and ends it in exasperated affection: "Now if you have declared war on play, relaxation, good living, and pleasure, yet do sleep as a freeman should."

What they missed is sitting in the open in Book 1.

Book 1 is a list of debts. Seventeen entries, working through his grandfather, his mother, his tutors, and finally the gods, assigning every decent quality he possesses to whoever gave it to him. "To the gods I am indebted for having good grandfathers, good parents, a good sister, good teachers, good associates, good kinsmen and friends, nearly everything good." He credits himself with nothing. Not one line.

That is the whole explanation of the beach. A man who believes every good thing in him is borrowed experiences rest as spending money he did not earn, while the lenders are dead and cannot be repaid and the plague is killing five million of the people he was handed. Working through the circus games reads to the crowd as contempt. From inside, it is a man who cannot locate the moment the debt would be considered settled.

That still made him hard to be around, and Fronto was right to push. Rome spent nineteen years reading the ledger as arrogance.

## Why Marcus Aurelius Kept Writing Reminders About Anger

Fronto was pushing at something real. The notebook returns to anger at a frequency no serene man would require.

Book 2 opens by bracing him for the day: "Begin the morning by saying to thyself, I shall meet with the busybody, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial." Book 5 has him arguing himself down from irritation at something as small as another person's body: "Art thou angry with him whose armpits stink?... show him his error, admonish him. For if he listens, thou wilt cure him, and there is no need of anger." Book 11 runs a numbered list of reminders for handling people who have wronged him.

Nobody writes a numbered protocol for a problem they do not have.

This is the least visible thing about Ones and the most important. Their core emotional fuel is anger, and it is usually the last thing they will name, because losing your temper is itself evidence in the case against you. So it gets renamed on the way out, into disappointment, or firmness, or a very polite sentence delivered a few degrees cooler. What survives the renaming gets stored.

Fronto saw the residue and said so, in the same confession about gossiping: "I would call you an austere and unreasonable, even at times, stung by anger, a disagreeable sort of person." That is the person closest to him, describing a young man with a temper he was already learning to keep off his face, and the notebook is what that management looks like written down.

### Marcus Aurelius's Wing: 1w2

The <a href="/enneagram-corner/enneagram-wings-complete-guide">wing</a> call is 1w2 rather than 1w9, and the evidence is his warmth under conditions where a 1w9 would go cold and withdraw.

The 1w9 idealist tends toward the detached corrector: standards aimed at systems, anger running quiet, people held at a distance. Marcus warms up whenever people are in front of him. The early Fronto letters run warm to a degree that has occupied scholars for two centuries. Book 1 is an inventory of specific people he owes. And when a general raised an army against him in 175 and was killed by his own officers, Marcus refused the win: the Historia Augusta records that he "did not rejoice at Cassius' death, and gave orders that his head should be buried," then pushed the Senate to pardon the family.

### Marcus Aurelius's Instinctual Subtype: self-preservation

The dominant <a href="/enneagram-corner/enneagram-instinctual-subtypes">instinct</a> reads as self-preservation, showing up as bodily austerity used as a moral instrument.

The Ariston letter ends its penances with "starve myself." Cassius Dio reports that "he took but very little food and that always at night" and that close study left him "extremely frail in body." The self-preservation One turns the standard on their own appetites first, and worry gets discharged through regimen. Marcus had a whole empire he could have corrected instead, and kept starting with the body he was in.

### Stress and Growth Arrows

Under stress the One moves toward Four, and the Ariston letter is a textbook run of it: "am sad, compare myself with others." Comparison, despondency and a sense of personal deficiency, arriving together. The 143 Senate letter shows the same movement in miniature, where reassurance cannot dislodge the mood.

The growth line runs to Seven, faint in Marcus but real. Book 3 has him noticing that bread splits at the surface when baked, that "figs, when they are quite ripe, gape open," that ripe olives are most beautiful near rottenness. A Seven finds delight everywhere by default. Marcus reasoned his way to it, then wrote it down like a discovery.

### Counterarguments: Why Marcus Aurelius Might Not Be Type 1

Type 5 is the rival worth taking seriously. The reading, the withdrawal, the private notebook, and the imagery of retreating into his own mind all point that way. He even builds the Five's ideal explicitly: "the mind which is free from passions is a citadel."

What weakens it is narrower than it first looks. Meditations does carry real cosmological argument, on flux, on the view from above, on the whole and the part. But the behavioral instructions, which are the bulk of the book, barely develop at all: the same short list, restated for a decade. A Five builds. This rehearses.

The second rival is Type 6, treated in the main analysis. His duty, his loyalty to Antoninus, and his visible anxiety all fit. The reassurance test separates them: he receives comfort, values it, and does not let it change the verdict.

The strongest objection is not a rival type at all. Ones are supposed to correct other people, and Marcus mostly declined to. He bore blame without returning it, and at around twenty-two he asked Fronto to soften his prosecution of a courtroom opponent, a letter whose plain sense is a plea for mercy rather than a correction. That narrows the type instead of breaking it: he held unlimited power to correct everyone in the empire and pointed the standard almost entirely at himself.

Type 1 is also the rarest read in the 9takes corpus, 26 of 426 published profiles ([corpus stats](/corpus-stats)). Ones look, from outside, like people who have their act together.

What would change our minds: evidence that the self-criticism was situational. The two archives close that door, thirty-three years apart, saying the same thing.

## How Marcus Aurelius Paid for a War He Could Not Win Quickly

The same standard decided what he did with money. By 169 the treasury was empty, the plague was still running, and the Danube frontier needed an army Rome could not afford. Raising provincial taxes was the obvious move.

He held a yard sale instead.

The inventory is specific, which is why it has stuck for eighteen centuries: goblets of gold and crystal, flagons made for kings, his wife's silk robes worked with gold, and jewels he had found in a locked cabinet of Hadrian's. When the war was funded, he told the buyers that any of them who wanted their money back could return the goods and have it.

Dio records the principle behind it in the plainest sentence Marcus ever aimed at the Senate: "As for us, we are so far from possessing anything of our own that even the house in which we live is yours."

The notebook shows him working the same problem from inside. Book 6 carries a warning that only makes sense if he thought corruption was a live risk: "Take care that thou art not made into a Caesar, that thou art not dyed with this dye; for such things happen." The name in that sentence had belonged to [Julius Caesar](/personality-analysis/julius-caesar) before it became a title and a job description. Marcus wore it and treated it as a stain risk.

The instruction he gives himself next tells you what he was afraid of. "Do everything as a disciple of Antoninus." At roughly fifty, running an empire, his standard was: behave the way your adoptive father would have. He was still marking his work against a dead man.

## Marcus Aurelius, Faustina, and the Children He Buried

Antoninus was not the only person he had buried.

He married Faustina the Younger in April 145 and stayed married to her for thirty years. Ancient sources record thirteen or fourteen children. Six were alive when their mother died: five daughters and Commodus.

Do the subtraction. He buried at least seven of his own children, most of them small, and four of them before he was ever emperor.

Book 1 carries a line that changes once you know that arithmetic. Thanking the gods for everything he has, he slips in this: "that my children have not been stupid nor deformed in body." That is a man counting what was left.

The **Historia Augusta** supplies the detail that is hardest to hold. In 169 his seven-year-old son Verus died after an operation on a tumour under his ear. "For no more than five days did he mourn him," the biographer writes, "and even during this period, when consulted on public affairs he gave some time to them." The source is unreliable and the number may be invented. The behavior it describes is the behavior Fronto had been complaining about for twenty years.

When Faustina died in 175 in a village below the Taurus mountains, he asked the Senate to make her a god, founded a colony where she died, and endowed a fund for orphaned girls. Whatever he felt went into institutions.

The notebook handles it alone, in Greek. He copies out Homer on the generations of men and turns the line on his own household: "Leaves, also, are thy children." And he copies out a line of Epictetus on the only safe way to love one: "When a man kisses his child," it runs, "he should whisper to himself, To-morrow perchance thou wilt die." He did not have to imagine that.

## Marcus Aurelius and Commodus: The Correction He Never Made

Then there is the decision that will not resolve, and it concerns the son who lived. In 177 he made his son Commodus co-emperor, breaking a run of adoptive successions that had produced four consecutive competent rulers. Commodus went on to a reign ancient writers treated as a disaster, and one modern audiences know mostly through **Gladiator**, which invented its own version of his father's death. The film is fiction; the question underneath it is not.

Dio, who is otherwise an admirer, puts it as the single flaw in the ledger: "Just one thing prevented him from being completely happy, namely, that after rearing and educating his son in the best possible way he was vastly disappointed in him."

Whether Marcus had a real alternative is genuinely disputed. A living adult son had never been passed over for an adopted heir, and doing it would likely have meant the civil war he had narrowly avoided in 175.

The shape of it is clearer than the verdict. The man who audited himself daily for four decades, who wrote a warning against being changed by the purple, who could not take four days at the beach without expecting a scolding, applied none of that scrutiny to the one decision that outlived him.

The Historia Augusta claims he saw it coming and wished his son would die young rather than become another Nero. That is the sort of retrospective prophecy the collection invents. What the same source says of his final days is quieter and harder to dismiss: on the seventh day of his illness he admitted only Commodus, then sent him out of the room at once, in fear that the boy would catch what was killing him.

## The Book He Never Meant Anyone to Read

He wrote the notebook in Greek, a second language, in a camp, at night, between administrative days that ran into the dark. Book 1 ends with a location note: among the Quadi, on the river Granua. Book 2 ends with another: this in Carnuntum. The most reproduced philosophy book in the world has a war for a return address.

Then it vanished for centuries. The first possible mention is Themistius in 364 AD, and scholars dispute whether he meant the text or the man's reputation. The trail goes cold until around 900, when a Byzantine bishop named Arethas of Caesarea turns out to own a manuscript and has it recopied. The Greek title it carries, _ta eis heauton_, means something close to _to himself_, and may have been added centuries later by a reader who could see what the pages obviously were.

Everything you can buy at an airport with his face on it descends from that accident.

The reading it gets now inverts the pages. He is the calm one, the meme with the marble bust and the serene caption. He is quoted so relentlessly that he gets quoted saying things he never wrote: PolitiFact had to fact-check one of the most viral of them in September 2019, a line about opinion and perspective that appears nowhere in the book.

The real ones are harder to put on a poster. They are corrections, issued to a man who kept needing them, by the only person qualified to issue them and the only person who would ever read them.

He wrote for an audience of one for roughly ten years. That reader had already decided, and never revised, and the verdict is right there in the first line of Book 8: it is plain to everyone, including you, how far you are from it.

He was fifty-four, and he had been at it since he was twenty-five, and he was still marking the same paper.
