## Identity and scope

**Verified fact.** Marcus Aurelius Antoninus, born Marcus Annius Verus in Rome 26 April 121, Roman
emperor from 7 March 161 to 17 March 180, the last of the so-called "Five Good Emperors." Wikidata
Q1430. Author of a private Greek notebook conventionally titled _Meditations_ (Τὰ εἰς ἑαυτόν, _ta eis
heauton_, "things unto himself"). Died aged 58.

**Names and aliases in the record.** Born Marcus Annius Verus; called Annius Verissimus by Hadrian;
Marcus Aurelius Caesar after the 138 adoption; Marcus Aurelius Antoninus Augustus as emperor. The
ancient sources frequently call him simply "Antoninus," which is a live confusion risk when reading
the _Historia Augusta_ (where "Antoninus" can mean Marcus, Antoninus Pius, or Lucius Verus depending
on context).

**Domain.** Roman imperial politics and administration; Stoic philosophy; Latin rhetoric.

**Why the article covers him** (from the repository's own routing record, not an external fact):
`docs/content-analysis/entity-gaps/Marcus-Aurelius.md` scores him **33/100 PASS** on the
Emerging-Entity-Gap model — his exact-name SERP is described there as "the single most fortified
biography SERP this command has audited." The packet proceeds on a different rationale: the
**typology long-tail** (`marcus aurelius personality type`, `marcus aurelius enneagram`), which that
audit found is held only by UGC voting sites, plus internal-link value in a Stoicism cluster with
`Ryan-Holiday`. Evaluators should note the draft was **not** commissioned to win biography intent.

**Knowledge cutoff of this packet.** Sources consulted 2026-08-27. Ancient evidence is stable; the
modern-reception claims (the Stoicism industry, the PolitiFact fact-check) are current to that date.
Nothing in this packet depends on events after 2026-08-27.

**Structural research limitation, stated up front.** The subject died in 180 AD. There are no
transcripts, no interviews, and no living witnesses. The pipeline's usual first-person evidence step
is satisfied here by two independent **textual** archives (the Fronto correspondence and _Meditations_),
which is a genuine substitution, not an equivalent one: both archives are self-selected, both survive
by accident, and neither was produced under questioning.

## First-person evidence

All Fronto quotations below were re-verified verbatim against the Wikisource transcription of the
Haines Loeb text on 2026-08-27. All _Meditations_ quotations were re-verified verbatim against the
Standard Ebooks edition of George Long's 1862 translation on the same date.

### The Fronto correspondence (Loeb, Haines)

**FP-1 — The Ariston letter. Loeb vol. 1, p. 217 (djvu 281). Marcus to Fronto, 145–147 AD.**
Verified verbatim, in full:

> "Ariston's books just now treat me well and at the same time make me feel ill. When they teach me a
> better way, then, I need not say, they treat me well; but when they shew me how far short my
> character comes of this better way, time and time again does your pupil blush and is angry with
> himself, for that, twenty-five years old as I am, no draught has my soul yet drunk of noble
> doctrines and purer principles. Therefore I do penance, am wroth with myself, am sad, compare
> myself with others, starve myself."

_What it can support:_ that at 25 Marcus described a specific self-punishing cascade in response to
falling short of a philosophical standard, unprompted, to a private correspondent, and reported his
own age in the same breath.
_What it cannot support:_ that any of the described behaviours actually occurred. "Starve myself"
(_fame necor_) is his own self-report inside a literary letter to a rhetoric tutor, in a genre that
prized vivid self-presentation. It is testimony about his self-description, not a behavioural record.
_Context the draft does not use:_ the letter's immediate occasion is that he has **not done his
homework** — "with plenty of time on my hands I have not given an atom of it to the task you gave me
to write." The penance passage is offered as the excuse. He then resolves to "make my peace with
Ariston's works and allow them to lie still awhile." An evaluator may read this as either
self-condemnation or a graceful literary apology for missing a deadline.

**FP-2 — Ariston's identity.** _Verified fact / minor scholarly qualification._ The reference is
generally identified as **Aristo of Chios**, the third-century BC Stoic; Pierre Hadot (_The Inner
Citadel_) concludes this. The competing candidate is Aristo of Ceos, a Peripatetic. Consensus, not
unanimity. The draft's "the Stoic philosopher Ariston" reflects the majority view.

**FP-3 — The Senate-anxiety letter. Loeb vol. 1, p. 109 (djvu 173). Marcus to Fronto, 143 AD.**
The draft's Type-1-vs-Type-6 discriminator rests entirely on this letter, so the full run-up matters.
Verified verbatim:

> "Hail, my deservedly dear Fronto. I see through that most subtle ruse of yours, which you indeed hit
> upon in pure kindness of heart. For not being able to win credit for your praise of me by reason of
> your signal partiality in my case you sought to make it credible by throwing in some abuse. […] so
> have you been almost blinded by love in your judgment of some of my work. […] At all events, your
> letter had the effect of making me feel how much you loved me. But as to my despondency,
> nevertheless, I am still nervous in mind and a little depressed, lest I shall have said something in
> the Senate to-day, such that I should not deserve to have you as my master."

_What it can support:_ that reassurance was received, named, credited to love — and did not dislodge
the self-assessment; and that the worry is explicitly about **desert** ("should not deserve to have you
as my master"), not about consequence or threat.
_What it cannot support:_ the draft's FAQ gloss "a letter of lavish praise." Marcus's own first
sentence says Fronto's letter deliberately mixed praise **with abuse** as a rhetorical device. The
substance is praise, but the letter was not pure praise, and Marcus's reply is partly a display of
seeing through the technique. See CLM-06.

**FP-4 — The Alsium holiday letter. Loeb vol. 2, p. 3 (djvu 19). Marcus (as Augustus) to Fronto, 162 AD.**
Verified verbatim: _"In what holiday-wise we have kept our holiday at Alsium I will not put on paper,
that you may not be yourself troubled and scold me, my master."_
_Context:_ the same short letter continues "On my return to Lorium I found my little lady slightly
feverish," and the text is lacunose.

**FP-5 — The manuscript-burning letter. Loeb vol. 1, p. 139 (djvu 203). Marcus to Fronto, 143 AD.**
Verified verbatim: _"For he knows my failing and was afraid that, if they came into my hands, I should
do as I usually do, and consign them to the flames."_
_Context the draft does not use:_ the very next sentences undercut the gesture — "But, as a matter of
fact, those particular hexameters were in next to no danger. For, to tell my master the truth, I dote
on them." The self-destructive habit is asserted and immediately qualified by the same writer.

**FP-6 — The longing letter. Loeb vol. 1, p. 77 (djvu 141).** Verified verbatim: _"To me this day
seems, and will seem, longer than a spring day, and the coming night more tedious than a night in
winter."_ Immediately followed by "so I long above all to embrace the writer of this last letter."

**FP-7 — The cold-room letter. Loeb vol. 1, p. 55 (djvu 119).** Verified verbatim: _"Aye, I can sleep,
for I am a great sleeper; but it is so cold in my room that I can scarcely put my hand outside the
bed-clothes."_

**FP-8 — The forced-sleep letter. Loeb vol. 1, p. 175 (djvu 239).** Verified verbatim: _"See, my
master, I am not sleepy, yet force myself to sleep that you may not be angry."_

**FP-9 — The Herodes-trial letter. Loeb vol. 1 (djvu 231–233), ? 140–143 AD.** This is the single
piece of evidence the draft's counterargument section leans on, and the verbatim text is genuinely
two-sided. Verified verbatim, in sequence:

> "The trial approaches in which men, it seems, will not only give a generous ear to your eloquence,
> but turn a grudging eye upon your angry animosity. […] even if you think me an ill-advised
> counsellor or a forward boy, or too partial to your opponent, I will not, for all that, shew any the
> more hesitation in pressing upon you what I think the best counsel. But why have I said counsel,
> whereas it is a favour I claim, urgently claim, from you and, if it is granted, promise to be bound
> to you in return? But you will say, What! if assailed, shall I not requite in like terms? Nay, you
> will win by this means greater glory for yourself if, even when assailed, you make no reply. […] I
> would rather fail in judgment by writing than fail in friendship by keeping silence."

_What it can support, in both directions:_ it is simultaneously a plea ("a favour I claim") **and** a
correction — it names Fronto's "angry animosity" as the fault, argues from Fronto's own glory, and the
writer explicitly labels his own act as "counsel" before converting it to a favour. Evaluators
weighing the draft's claim that its "plain sense is a plea for mercy rather than a correction" — and
the second-pass note that a previous framing ("correcting his own teacher's ethics") was a "clever
read" — should note the text supports a both/and reading and that Marcus is correcting an **anger**
problem. The opponent is identified as Herodes Atticus from surrounding letters and editorial notes,
not from this letter's own words.

**FP-10 — Fronto's concession.** Fronto's reply, same date range, verified verbatim in part:
_"Rightly have I devoted myself to you, rightly invested in you and your father all the gains of my
life… But I beseech you, away with your forward boys and rash counsellors!"_

## Third-party testimony

**TP-1 — Fronto on Marcus's public manner. Loeb vol. 1, p. 207 (djvu 271), 145–147 AD.** Verified
verbatim, with the surrounding frame the draft omits:

> "I have occasionally inveighed against you behind your back in somewhat strong terms before a very
> few of my most intimate friends. Time was I did this, when you went about in public gatherings with
> too serious a face, as when you used to read books either in the theatre or at a banquet—nor was I
> then refraining from theatres, nor as yet from banquets—on such occasions, then, I would call you an
> austere and unreasonable, even at times, stung by anger, a disagreeable sort of person. But if
> anyone else found fault with you in my hearing with similar detraction, I could not listen to him
> with any patience."

_Weight:_ Tier 1 — a named intimate, contemporaneous, in a private letter, describing behaviour he
personally witnessed. This is the strongest third-party characterisation available.
_Limits an evaluator should hold:_ (a) it is offered as one of three "trifles" **proving Fronto's love**
— the other two are dream-embraces and smiling at Marcus's portraits — so the register is affectionate
teasing inside a love-declaration, not a complaint; (b) it is explicitly **retrospective and closed**
("Time was I did this"); (c) Fronto immediately says he would tolerate the same criticism from nobody
else. The draft quotes the two halves accurately but in two different sections, without the
"Time was" past-tense framing in the second instance.

**TP-2 — Fronto on the Alsium holiday, _De Feriis Alsiensibus_, 162 AD.** All verified verbatim:

- Loeb vol. 2, p. 5 (djvu 21): _"What? Am I not aware that you went to Alsium with the intention of
  indulging yourself and there giving yourself up to recreation and mirth and complete leisure for
  four whole days?"_
- Loeb vol. 2, p. 7 (djvu 23): _"You ever indulge in pleasu? It were easier to reconcile you to a
  polecat than to pleasure. Tell me, Marcus, I beseech you, have you repaired to Alsium only to fast
  with the sea in sight? What, could you not wear yourself out at Lorium with hunger and thirst and
  doing business?"_
- Loeb vol. 2, p. 11 (djvu 27): _"Now if you have declared war on play, relaxation, good living, and
  pleasure, yet do sleep as a freeman should."_

_Important correction to the draft's internal record._ The draft's TESTIMONY LEDGER item 2 explains the
dropped fragment "You ever indulge in pleasu?" by saying "the Wikisource transcription of the Loeb text
prints it truncated (the De Feriis has textual gaps)." **That explanation is wrong.** "Pleasu" is not a
lacuna. It is Haines rendering Fronto's deliberate wordplay: Fronto announces "if the truth must be
told in docked words" and then clips a run of words on purpose — "watchin'—I mean watching—, in
labors—I mean labours—, in vexats—I mean vexations. You ever indulge in pleasu?" The De Feriis does
have real gaps elsewhere (marked with dots), but this is not one of them. The editorial decision to
drop the fragment may stand on other grounds; the stated reason is a misreading of the source.

_What TP-2 can support:_ that Fronto, in 162, wrote an extended comic essay whose premise is that
Marcus would not rest.
_What it cannot support:_ what Marcus actually did at Alsium. The whole _De Feriis_ is Fronto's
**imagined** reconstruction ("I have no doubt that you have set about enjoying the holiday… in this
fashion: after taking your usual siesta…"). Fronto is satirising an expectation, and Marcus's own
letter pointedly refuses to say what happened. Nobody knows how Marcus spent those four days.

**TP-3 — Cassius Dio, _Roman History_ bk. 72 (Cary, Loeb / LacusCurtius).** All verified verbatim:

- 72.36.2: "As a result of his close application and study he was extremely frail in body, though in
  the beginning he had been so vigorous that he used to fight in armour, and on the chase would strike
  down wild boars while on horseback."
- 72.36.3: "However, he did not meet with the good fortune **that** he deserved, for he was not strong
  in body and was involved in a multitude of troubles throughout practically his entire reign."
  (The draft and the TESTIMONY LEDGER both drop the word "that.")
- 72.36.4: "Just one thing prevented him from being completely happy, namely, that after rearing and
  educating his son in the best possible way he was vastly disappointed in him."
- 72.6: "he took but very little food and that always at night. It was never his practice to eat
  during the daytime, unless it were some of the drug called theriac."
- 72.33.2: "'As for us,' he said, in addressing the senate, 'we are so far from possessing anything of
  our own that even the house in which we live is yours.'"
- 72.32.3: "Therefore I am surprised to hear people even to-day censuring him on the ground that he was
  not an open-handed prince."
- **Fragment (Dio, after 72.36):** "On one occasion when, with wars impending, he found himself at a
  loss for funds, he neither devised any new tax nor brought himself to ask anyone for money, but
  instead exposed in the Forum all the heirlooms of the palace together with any ornaments that
  belonged to his wife, and urged any who so desired to buy them… he issued a proclamation to the
  effect that any one of the purchasers of the imperial property who wished might return the article
  purchased and receive its value."

_Weight and limits:_ Dio was a Roman senator writing within living memory, but books 72–73 survive only
in Byzantine epitome (Xiphilinus/Zonaras), so the wording is at one or two removes. Dio is also openly
an admirer ("I admire him all the more for this very reason"), which is worth holding when he is used
as neutral corroboration.

_Evidence upgrade available to evaluators:_ the draft sources the auction **solely** to _Historia
Augusta_ 17.4–5, which it correctly flags as unreliable. Dio corroborates the auction independently,
including the two distinctive details (no new tax; refunds offered afterwards). The episode is
therefore better attested than the draft's own sourcing implies.

_Nuance on the "house in which we live is yours" line:_ it is verified verbatim, but it is Dio 72.33.2,
attached to a **later and different occasion** — Marcus asking the senate for money from the public
treasury during the Scythian crisis (c. 178). The draft places it as "the principle behind" the c. 169
auction. That is a defensible interpretive bridge; it is not the same event, and in the Dio passage
Marcus **is** asking the senate for funds, whereas the auction fragment stresses he "neither devised
any new tax nor brought himself to ask anyone for money."

**TP-4 — _Historia Augusta_, _Life of Marcus Aurelius_ (Magie, Loeb / LacusCurtius).** All verified
verbatim:

- 15.1: "It was customary with Marcus to read, listen to, and sign documents at the circus-games;
  because of this habit he was openly ridiculed, it is said, by the people."
- 17.4–5: "…he held a public sale in the Forum of the Deified Trajan of the imperial furnishings, and
  sold goblets of gold and crystal and murra, even flagons made for kings, his wife's silken
  gold-embroidered robes, and, indeed, even certain jewels which he had found in considerable numbers
  in **a particularly holy cabinet** of Hadrian's. This sale lasted for two months…" and "he gave the
  buyers to understand that if any of them wished to return his purchases and recover his money, he
  could do so."
- 21.3–5: "Marcus lost his seven-year-old son, by name Verus Caesar, from an operation on a tumour
  under his ear. For no more than five days did he mourn him; and even during this period, when
  consulted on public affairs he gave some time to them. **And because the games of Jupiter Optimus
  Maximus were then in progress and he did not wish to have them interrupted by public mourning**, he
  merely ordered that statues should be decreed for his dead son…"
- 22.5–6: "But because Marcus, as a result of his system of philosophy, **seemed** harsh in his military
  discipline and indeed in his life in general, he was bitterly assailed; to all who spoke ill of him,
  however, he made reply either in speeches or in pamphlets."
- 25.2–3: "he was speedily slain and his head was brought to Antoninus. Even then, Marcus did not
  rejoice at Cassius' death, and gave orders that his head should be buried."
- 26.4–9: "in the village of Halala, in the foothills of Mount Taurus, he lost his wife Faustina, who
  succumbed to a sudden illness. He asked the senate to decree her divine honours and a temple… He
  established a new order of Faustinian girls in honour of his dead wife… he made the village where
  Faustina died a colony."
- 28.8: "On the seventh day he was weary and admitted only his son, and even him he at once sent away
  in fear that he would catch the disease."
- 28.10: "It is said that he foresaw that after his death Commodus would turn out as he actually did,
  and expressed the wish that his son might die, lest, as he himself said, he should become another
  Nero, Caligula, or Domitian."

_Weight:_ Tier 3 at best. The _Historia Augusta_ is a late-fourth-century compilation of disputed
authorship containing demonstrable forgeries. Every item above is evidence of **what was said**, never
of what happened. The draft states this in-text, which is the correct handling.
_Two omissions with interpretive weight:_ (a) HA 21.5 supplies its own explanation for the five-day
mourning — the games of Jupiter were in progress and Marcus did not want them interrupted by public
mourning. The draft quotes 21.3–4 and stops before that clause. (b) HA 22.5 says he "**seemed** harsh";
the draft renders it "seem harsh" to fit its sentence, which shifts a reported perception marginally
toward a description.

**TP-5 — Avidius Cassius on Marcus. Two different HA documents, two different dates.** This is the
packet's most consequential finding and is set out in full at CLM-01.

- _Avidius Cassius_ **1.7–8**, headed "**Extract from the letter of Verus**": Lucius Verus, writing to
  Marcus, reports what Cassius says — "Everything we do displeases him, he is amassing no inconsiderable
  wealth, and he laughs at our letters. **He calls you a philosophical old woman, me a half-witted
  spendthrift.** Consider what should be done." Verus died in early 169, and the letter refers to
  Antoninus Pius as "my grandfather, your father," which is one reason the letter is generally judged
  **spurious**. It cannot date from the 175 revolt.
- _Avidius Cassius_ **14.1–3**, a letter attributed to Cassius himself "written to his son-in-law after
  he had declared himself emperor" (so 175): "**Marcus is indeed the best of men, but one who wishes to
  be called merciful and hence suffers to live men whose manner of life he cannot sanction.** …Marcus
  Antoninus philosophizes and meditates on first principles, and on souls and virtue and justice, and
  takes no thought for the state."

**TP-6 — Marcus's clemency after the revolt.** _Historia Augusta_, _Avidius Cassius_ 12.6–7, Marcus to
the senate, verified verbatim: "you will grant pardon to the sons and son-in-law and wife of Avidius
Cassius. For that matter, why should I say pardon? They have done nothing. Let them live, therefore,
free from all anxiety, knowing that they live under Marcus." Also 13.6: "the descendants of Avidius
Cassius lived unmolested and were admitted to offices of honour." Same HA reliability caveat. Note the
sequel at 13.7: Commodus later "ordered them all to be burned alive."

**TP-7 — Modern scholarship.** Donald J. Robertson, _Marcus Aurelius: The Stoic Emperor_ (Yale UP, 2024) and the Yale UP essay of 20 Mar 2024, plus his 2017 blog survey of ancient criticisms and his
2017 piece on the Christian question. Anthony R. Birley, _Marcus Aurelius: A Biography_ (Routledge), is
the standard academic life. Pierre Hadot, _The Inner Citadel_, for the Ariston identification.
_Limit:_ Robertson is a sympathetic biographer and a participant in the modern Stoicism movement; his
"Did Marcus Aurelius Persecute the Christians?" piece is explicitly advocacy. Useful for locating
evidence, not for settling contested attribution on its own.

## Public record, accomplishments, failures, and controversies

### Accomplishments (verified fact unless noted)

- Refused sole rule in 161 and insisted Lucius Verus receive equal powers — "the first time that Rome
  was ruled by two emperors." Co-rule lasted until Verus's death in early 169, roughly eight years.
- Held the Danube frontier through simultaneous plague and multi-front invasion.
- Funded the Marcomannic war by auctioning imperial property rather than levying the provinces
  (corroborated by both Dio and HA).
- Extended clemency after an armed revolt: ordered Cassius's head buried, asked the senate to spare the
  family and to execute no senator during his reign.
- Deferred to the senate on finances in terms Dio found remarkable.
- Wrote the most-read work of practical philosophy in the Western canon without intending to publish a
  word of it. (The "most-read"/"most durable" superlatives are **interpretation**; no source in this
  packet quantifies them.)

### Failures and controversies

- **Commodus.** _Disputed claim._ Elevating his own son in 177 broke the run of adoptive successions.
  Wikipedia records that "Commodus's succession after Marcus has been a subject of debate among both
  contemporary and modern historians." Whether Marcus had a realistic alternative — no living adult son
  had ever been passed over, and passing him over risked civil war — is a live historiographical
  question, not a settled verdict. Public understanding is heavily contaminated by the _Gladiator_
  films, which invent the manner of Marcus's death.
- **Lugdunum, 177.** _Verified fact that it happened; Marcus's role disputed._ Roughly 48 Christians
  died at Lyon, including Bishop Pothinus and the slave Blandina. See the dispute section below, which
  contains material the draft does not carry.
- **Ancient criticism.** He was thought harsh in military discipline and in life generally (HA 22.5);
  censured as not open-handed (Dio 72.32.3); called insincere by some (HA 29.6, "he was insincere and
  not as guileless as he seemed"); openly ridiculed at the circus games (HA 15.1); called a
  "philosophical old woman" in the HA's spurious Verus letter and dismissed by the letter attributed to
  Cassius as one who "takes no thought for the state."
- **Faustina.** _Ancient rumour, correctly excluded from the draft._ Allegations of infidelity, the
  gladiator story, and her alleged suicide trace to Dio and the HA. HA 26.5 records that "she had
  suffered grievously from the reputation of lewdness"; HA 29.1 alleges Marcus advanced his wife's
  lovers. These are hostile late traditions. The draft omits them and says so in its FAQ, which is the
  defensible handling. Note that HA _Avidius Cassius_ 9–11 preserves letters purporting to show
  Faustina urging vengeance on Cassius's family, and 7.6 records the counter-rumour that Cassius
  revolted "at the wish of Faustina" — the tradition is contradictory in both directions.

### The Antonine Plague

_Disputed claim._ Wikipedia's infobox gives "5–10 million (estimated)"; its body text gives a much wider
scholarly range — "Estimates of the fatalities from the pandemic range from 2 to 33% of the Roman
Empire's population with deaths between 1.5 and 25 million people" — and notes that "most estimates
coalesce around a fatality rate of about 10% (7.5 million people)," with some historians arguing for "a
less than catastrophic outcome." There is as yet no genetic evidence identifying the disease. The
draft's "five to ten million" matches the infobox figure; the underlying range is far wider and
contested.

### Modern reception

_Verified fact._ PolitiFact, 26 September 2019, rated **False** the viral attribution to Marcus of
"Everything we hear is an opinion, not a fact. Everything we see is a perspective, not the truth." A
philosophy professor confirmed the line does not appear in _Meditations_ as stated, while noting that
an old translation contains "vaguely related material about 'opinion.'" The draft's "appears nowhere in
the book" is marginally stronger than the fact-check's own hedged wording.

## Enneagram hypothesis evidence

**Framing.** Enneagram type is a pattern hypothesis, not a diagnosis, and nothing below should be read
as a psychiatric claim. All of it is evidence about **behaviour and self-description in surviving
texts**, which for a subject dead 1,846 years is the only category available.

### Evidence consistent with Type 1

1. **FP-1 (Ariston letter, age 25).** A self-reported cascade — blushing, self-anger, penance, sadness,
   comparison, self-starvation — triggered by falling short of a standard, not by external consequence.
2. **FP-3 (143 Senate letter).** Reassurance received, credited, and explicitly not effective. The
   stated worry is about **desert**, not danger.
3. **Med. 8.1.** Thirty-odd years later, a private statement that he is "far from philosophy" and that
   this is plain to others and to himself. (Read CLM-03 before weighing this.)
4. **Med. 5.9.** An instruction not to be "disgusted… discouraged… dissatisfied" at his own failure —
   an instruction that presupposes those as defaults.
5. **Med. 2.1, 5.28, 11.18.** Sustained, structured anger-management material, including an explicitly
   enumerated nine-point protocol for people who have wronged him.
6. **TP-1 (Fronto).** A named intimate calls him austere, unreasonable, "at times, stung by anger," and
   disagreeable in public — the only direct outside characterisation of his temperament.
7. **TP-2 (Alsium).** A contemporary comic essay whose entire premise is that he would not rest.
8. **Med. Book 1.** Seventeen entries assigning his qualities to other people.
9. **Dio 72.6 and 72.36.2.** Bodily austerity: very little food, taken at night; frailty attributed to
   overwork.

### Evidence against, or unexplained by, Type 1

1. **He mostly declined to correct others.** FP-9's plain text is a request, framed as a favour, that
   his own teacher **stop** prosecuting hard. HA 22.6 says that when attacked he replied in speeches or
   pamphlets rather than punishing. He buried his usurper's head and pardoned the family. Med. 1.16
   praises Antoninus for bearing "with those who blamed him unjustly without blaming them in return" —
   the model he chose is a non-corrector. A type whose defining behaviour is correcting the world is
   thinly evidenced here; almost all the correction points inward.
2. **Med. 11.18, closing.** Marcus explicitly lists **self-reproach** among four "aberrations of the
   superior faculty" to guard against: "the fourth is when thou shalt reproach thyself for anything, for
   this is an evidence of the diviner part within thee being overpowered." A man who names the
   diagnostic behaviour as a fault to be wiped out complicates a reading built on that behaviour. It can
   be read either way — as awareness of the pattern, or as evidence that the pattern is not his settled
   position. Not used in the draft.
3. **The warm register.** FP-6 and TP-1's surrounding text (dream-embraces, smiling at portraits,
   longing) show an intense, physically affectionate epistolary relationship. Type 1 does not predict
   this. The scholarly discussion of that register's nature is live and unsettled; per the entity-gap
   handling rules, no romantic or sexual claim is made here in either direction.
4. **Med. 8.1's actual frame.** The passage's stated purpose is "the removal of the desire of empty
   fame," and it continues by urging him to "throw away the thought, How thou shalt seem to others."
   Read whole, the passage argues for **detachment from reputation**, not for a verdict against himself.
5. **The self-selection problem.** Both archives are documents in which self-examination is the genre.
   A rhetoric student writing to his tutor and a Stoic writing _hypomnemata_ are both performing a form
   whose conventions require exactly the self-scrutiny being treated as diagnostic. This is the single
   strongest structural objection available to a skeptic and it applies to every item in the "for" list.

### Closest alternative types

**Type 6.** Duty, loyalty to Antoninus (Med. 1.16, 6.30), visible anxiety (FP-3, FP-8). The draft's
discriminator is the reassurance test at FP-3. That test is real and the text supports it — but see
CLM-06: the letter Marcus is answering was not pure reassurance, and part of his reply is the observation
that Fronto's praise was a technique. An evaluator could argue that seeing through offered comfort is
itself compatible with a Six's scepticism toward reassurance.

**Type 5.** The reading, the withdrawal, Med. 4.3 ("retire into thyself"), Med. 8.48 ("a citadel").
The draft's narrowed rebuttal — that the **behavioural instructions** do not develop, whatever the
cosmology does — is the defensible form of the argument. The unquantified premise "which are the bulk
of the book" is not established by anything in this packet.

### Discriminating evidence

- **FP-3** is the cleanest discriminator on the record: reassurance is named, valued, and does not move
  the verdict, and the stated stake is desert rather than safety.
- **FP-1's "compare myself with others"** is the only first-person report of social comparison.
- **TP-1** is the only outside temperament description by someone who knew him.
- **Med. 11.18** cuts both ways and is the best single discriminator not currently on the page.

### Behaviour the hypothesis does not explain

The effusive, physically affectionate early letters; the near-total absence of outward correction
despite unlimited power to correct; and Med. 11.18's explicit warning against self-reproach.

### Contamination warning for evaluators

The entity-gap packet records that the existing hobbyist consensus is already **Enneagram 1w2** (MBTI
INFJ, Socionics EII) on sakinorva, Personality Database and Boo.world — "crowd-vote artifacts with no
visible argument behind them." The draft lands on 1w2. That agreement is not itself evidence in either
direction, but evaluators assessing whether the page reasons independently or converges on the prior
should know the prior exists.

**CLM-01 — The Avidius Cassius slur.**
_Claim:_ "During his 175 revolt, the general Avidius Cassius reportedly called Marcus 'a philosophical
old woman' who 'wishes to be called merciful and hence suffers to live men whose manner of life he
cannot sanction.'"
_Location:_ "Why Marcus Aurelius Could Not Enjoy a Holiday"; also TESTIMONY LEDGER item 5.
_Class:_ attributed quotation + dating.
_Status:_ **disputed claim — the two halves come from different documents, speakers and dates.**
_Supporting source:_ second clause verified verbatim at HA, _Avidius Cassius_ 14.3, a letter attributed
to Cassius written to his son-in-law after declaring himself emperor — correctly dated 175.
_Counterevidence:_ "philosophical old woman" is at HA, _Avidius Cassius_ **1.8**, inside an "Extract
from the letter of **Verus**" — Lucius Verus reporting Cassius's insults to Marcus ("He calls you a
philosophical old woman, me a half-witted spendthrift"). Verus died early 169, so
the line cannot belong to the 175 revolt; the letter also calls Antoninus Pius Verus's "grandfather,"
one reason it is generally judged spurious even by HA standards. The draft's "That line survives only
through the Historia Augusta, which puts speeches in mouths" is true but understates the chain: HA →
probably-forged Verus letter → Cassius's alleged words.
_Risk if wrong:_ this is the draft's designated critic-pressure anchor. A reader checking the citation
finds one quotation assembled from two sources thirteen-plus years apart, with the more colourful half
misdated and one speaker removed from where the draft places him.

**CLM-02 — Who gave Marcus the Epictetus discourses.**
_Claim:_ "Marcus found the Stoics, through the man he thanks for it in his notebook: 'I am indebted to
him for being acquainted with the discourses of Epictetus, which he communicated to me out of his own
collection.'"
_Location:_ "Who Marcus Aurelius Was, and How Rome Picked Him Twice."
_Class:_ quotation attribution.
_Status:_ **verified fact (the quotation) with an unresolved referent in the draft's prose.**
_Supporting source:_ Med. 1.7, verified verbatim — and 1.7 is the entry headed "**From Rusticus**."
_Counterevidence:_ the two preceding sentences of the draft name Fronto twice ("educated chiefly in
rhetoric under Marcus Cornelius Fronto… the training took a turn Fronto never fully forgave"), and "the
man" is never named. Fronto does have his own Book 1 entry — 1.11, "From Fronto I learned to observe
what envy, and duplicity, and hypocrisy are in a tyrant" — and it is not this one.
_Risk if wrong:_ a reader who takes "the man" to be Fronto acquires the reverse of the actual fact,
inside the sentence that establishes how Marcus reached Stoicism at all. The research file records the
correct attribution (M7, "From Rusticus"); the draft's prose does not carry the name.

**CLM-03 — Book 8's opening as a verdict.**
_Claim:_ "he opened Book 8 by telling himself he had fallen short of philosophy and that everyone could
see it" (TL;DR, FAQ 1, timeline, closing paragraph: "the verdict is right there in the first line of
Book 8").
_Location:_ five places; the closing paragraph is the load-bearing one.
_Class:_ quotation framing / interpretation.
_Status:_ **verified fact (the quoted words) / interpretation (the framing).**
_Supporting source:_ Med. 8.1 verified verbatim from "it is no longer in thy power…" onward.
_Counterevidence:_ the draft's quotation begins mid-sentence. Long's full opening is "**This reflection
also tends to the removal of the desire of empty fame**, that it is no longer in thy power…"; the section
continues "so that it is no longer easy for thee to get the **reputation** of a philosopher… throw away
the thought, How thou shalt seem to others, and be content if thou shalt live the rest…" On the page,
8.1's argument is a renunciation of caring how he appears, with the shortfall used instrumentally. Also
"both to many others and to thyself" is narrower than "everyone."
_Risk if wrong:_ the closing beat, the TL;DR, and FAQ 1 all treat 8.1 as a self-verdict. A
Stoicism-literate reader who opens Book 8 finds a passage about giving up the desire for fame.

**CLM-04 — The Ariston letter as the central Type 1 evidence.**
_Claim:_ the "penance… starve myself" passage, used as epigraph, FAQ 1, timeline, diagnosis and subtype
evidence.
_Location:_ five appearances.
_Class:_ first-person quotation.
_Status:_ **verified fact** (quotation), **interpretation** (its diagnostic weight).
_Supporting source:_ Loeb vol. 1, p. 217, verified verbatim including "twenty-five years old as I am."
Ariston identified as Aristo of Chios, Stoic, per Hadot and general consensus.
_Counterevidence:_ the letter's occasion is an apology for not writing an assigned exercise; the penance
passage is the excuse offered, and Marcus closes by resolving to set Ariston aside for a while and read
Cicero. The self-report is not corroborated by any outside witness.
_Risk if wrong:_ moderate. The quotation is solid; only its weight is arguable.

**CLM-05 — The Fronto testimony about his temper.**
_Claim:_ "I would call you an austere and unreasonable, even at times, stung by anger, a disagreeable
sort of person" as evidence of "a young man with a temper he was already learning to keep off his face."
_Location:_ "Why Marcus Aurelius Kept Writing Reminders About Anger."
_Class:_ third-party quotation.
_Status:_ **verified fact** (quotation), **interpretation** (the inference).
_Supporting source:_ Loeb vol. 1, p. 207, verified verbatim.
_Counterevidence:_ the sentence sits inside Fronto's declaration of love, as the second of three
"trifles" proving his affection, and is explicitly past-tense and closed ("**Time was I did this**"),
immediately followed by "But if anyone else found fault with you in my hearing… I could not listen to
him with any patience." The draft's second use drops the "Time was" frame.
_Risk if wrong:_ moderate. The words are real; the register is teasing intimacy rather than testimony
against him, which affects how much weight it carries.

**CLM-06 — The reassurance test (Type 1 vs Type 6).**
_Claim:_ FAQ 2 — he was "still nervous in mind and a little depressed"… "immediately after receiving a
letter of lavish praise from Fronto that he acknowledged and set aside"; body — "Marcus takes the
reassurance, thanks the sender warmly, and then continues prosecuting the case anyway."
_Location:_ FAQ 2; "What is Marcus Aurelius's personality type?"; the `inner-thought` beat.
_Class:_ causal/psychological claim; the draft's stated type discriminator.
_Status:_ **verified fact with a material qualification.**
_Supporting source:_ Loeb vol. 1, p. 109, verified verbatim, including "your letter had the effect of
making me feel how much you loved me. But as to my despondency, nevertheless, I am still nervous in

_Counterevidence:_ Marcus's own opening says the letter was **not** simply lavish praise — "not being
able to win credit for your praise of me by reason of your signal partiality in my case you sought to

Marcus's reply partly consists of announcing that he has seen through the device.
_Risk if wrong:_ this single letter carries the whole Type-6 rebuttal in both the FAQ and the diagnosis

**CLM-07 — Lugdunum 177 and the sourcing of it.**
_Claim:_ "How much he knew or ordered is contested: the only detailed account is Eusebius, writing more
than a century later, and Tertullian, alive at the time, called Marcus a protector of Christians rather
than a persecutor."
_Location:_ "Why Marcus Aurelius Could Not Enjoy a Holiday."
_Class:_ source characterisation on a contested controversy.
_Status:_ **disputed claim — the source description is imprecise in both directions.**
_Supporting source:_ Tertullian does present Marcus as a protector (_Apology_ 5), citing letters about
the German rain miracle. Eusebius did write c. 313–325.
_Counterevidence:_ the detailed account is **not** Eusebius's own. It is a **contemporary letter from
the churches of Lyon and Vienne to the churches of Asia Minor**, quoted at length by Eusebius, _EH_
5.1. More importantly, that contemporary letter reports an imperial rescript: at 5.1.44 the governor
holds prisoners "concerning whom he had written to Cæsar, and whose answer he was awaiting," and at
5.1.47, "**For Cæsar commanded that they should be put to death, but that any who might deny should be
set free.**" That is the single most damaging item in the record on this question and it appears
nowhere on the page. Against it: Robertson's survey notes no contemporary pagan or Christian author
outside this letter mentions the events, Irenaeus (in Lyon) is silent three years later, the letter
does not name Marcus personally as responsible, Marcus was weeks' march away, and the rescript
Tertullian relies on is itself disputed as possibly spurious.
_Risk if wrong:_ the draft explicitly promises the reader that this "does not resolve, and it should
not be smoothed over." A reader who checks Eusebius finds a contemporary document reporting that the
emperor ordered the executions — evidence the page's framing implies does not exist.

estimate hedge entirely and states five million as fact.
_Risk if wrong:_ low-moderate. The figure is defensible; its certainty is not.

**CLM-13 — "Four of them before he was ever emperor."**
_Claim:_ "He buried at least seven of his own children, most of them small, and four of them before he
was ever emperor."
_Location:_ "Marcus Aurelius, Faustina, and the Children He Buried."
_Class:_ derived arithmetic.
_Status:_ **unresolved question — the standard child list supports five, possibly six.**
_Supporting source:_ "at least seven" is safe on either the 13- or 14-child count.
_Counterevidence:_ deaths before March 161 on the conventional list: Domitia Faustina (d. 151), the two
149 twins, Tiberius Aelius Antoninus (d. before 156), and the unknown child (d. before 158) = five, with
Hadrianus (undated infant death) a possible sixth. No source located in this packet supports exactly
four.
_Risk if wrong:_ low factual, moderate credibility — it is a precise number in the draft's most
emotionally weighted passage, and it is checkable in one click.

**CLM-14 — "Thirty-three years, two languages, two readers, one report" / "at 22 and at 55."**
_Claim:_ the H2 "Two Archives, One Emperor: Marcus Aurelius at 22 and at 55" and the span assertion.
_Class:_ internal arithmetic.
_Status:_ **unresolved question (internal inconsistency).**
_Counterevidence:_ the draft's own timeline runs 143 (age 22) to c. 175 (age "~54") — 32 years, ages
22→54. The heading says 55, the body and the FORMULA FINGERPRINT LEDGER both say thirty-three years, and

not studying"). _Meditations_ is undatable internally beyond the c. 170–180 convention, so no precise
span is recoverable.
_Risk if wrong:_ low. No argument depends on the exact figure, but three different spans appear on one
page.

**CLM-15 — "Book 11 runs a numbered list of reminders for handling people who have wronged him."**

_Status:_ **verified fact, precisely.** Med. 11.18: "If any have offended against thee, consider

_Counterevidence:_ none as to the claim. Note for the diagnosis: the same section closes by naming

_Risk if wrong:_ none — this is the strongest textual support for the anger argument and it holds
exactly.

**CLM-16 — "Do everything as a disciple of Antoninus" as the instruction that comes "next."**

_Class:_ quotation adjacency.
_Status:_ **verified fact (both quotations) / imprecise (the adjacency).**

**CLM-17 — Book 1 credits him with nothing.**
_Claim:_ "Seventeen entries… He credits himself with nothing. Not one line."

_Status:_ **verified fact (seventeen entries) / interpretation (the absolute)**.

## Disputes and unresolved questions

1. **Marcus's responsibility for Lugdunum (177).** Unresolved and probably unresolvable. The
   contemporary Lyon letter preserved at Eusebius _EH_ 5.1.44–47 reports that the governor wrote to the
   emperor and that "Cæsar commanded that they should be put to death, but that any who might deny
   should be set free." Against that: the letter names no emperor personally as culpable; no other
   contemporary source mentions the episode; Irenaeus, in Lyon, is silent; Tertullian, writing within a
   generation, calls Marcus a protector; and the rescript Tertullian relies on is itself of disputed
   authenticity. Any conclusion here must stay qualified in both directions.
2. **Whether the "philosophical old woman" line can be dated or attributed at all.** It survives only in
   a letter the HA attributes to Lucius Verus that is widely judged spurious. Its date cannot be 175.
3. **Whether Commodus's elevation was misjudgment or constraint.** Live historiographical debate;
   Wikipedia records it as such. The draft's refusal to settle it is consistent with the evidence.
4. **Total number of children.** Standard references give 13 and 14 in different articles. Six alive at
   Faustina's death is derivable from the conventional list. The number who died before March 161 is not
   settled by any source located here; the list supports five or six, not four.
5. **Composition dates of _Meditations_.** Only two internal datelines exist. "Roughly ten years,"
   "thirty years later," and "thirty-three years" are all reconstructions built on the c. 170–180
   convention.
6. **The nature of the affectionate register in the early Fronto letters.** Contested scholarly ground.
   No romantic or sexual claim is made here in either direction, per the binding handling rule.
7. **Death location.** Vindobona vs. Sirmium; unresolved.
8. **Whether Themistius (364) refers to the text or to Marcus's reputation.** Wikipedia calls the
   mention "doubtful."
9. **Antonine Plague mortality.** Range spans an order of magnitude (1.5–25 million); no genetic
   evidence for the pathogen.
10. **Whether the Ariston of the letter is Aristo of Chios.** Majority view, not unanimous.
11. **Whether the two-archive evidence can bear diagnostic weight at all.** Both archives belong to
    genres that require self-examination. No independent behavioural record exists to corroborate any
    self-report. This limits every type conclusion on the page, whichever type is argued.

## Research limitations

1. **No transcripts, interviews or living witnesses.** Structural to a subject dead since 180 AD. Two
   textual archives were substituted; both are self-selected and genre-bound.
2. **Translations, not originals.** Every quotation was verified against an English translation (Haines
   1919/1920; Long 1862; Cary; Magie). The Latin and Greek were not consulted. Verification therefore
   establishes that the draft quotes its **stated editions** accurately; it does not adjudicate
   translation choices. Long's 1862 English is a Victorian rendering and Haines's Fronto is often
   strained.
3. **Loeb printed-page numbers were re-derived, not read from a physical Loeb.** The offsets (vol. 1:
   djvu − 64; vol. 2: djvu − 16) were confirmed against the printed page markers on two pages per
   volume. All research-file page citations checked out. A physical copy was not consulted.
4. **Britannica remained inaccessible.** The research file records HTTP 403 on 2026-08-27; this compiler
   did not retry it. Life-spine facts rest on Wikipedia plus the primary texts.
5. **Hadot was not read directly.** The Ariston-of-Chios identification is reported at second hand from
   a search summary. Tier 4 by the packet's own rules for a factual correction; it is used only to
   confirm a majority view the draft already reflects.
6. **The Lyon rescript was verified in Eusebius, not adjudicated.** Whether the reported imperial reply
   is historical, and whether the emperor in question acted knowingly, cannot be settled from available
   evidence. The packet records the text and the counterarguments; it does not resolve them.
7. **Dio survives only in epitome.** All Dio quotations are Byzantine excerptors' wording at one or two
   removes from Dio's own.
8. **No independent check of _Gladiator_'s content.** The draft's claim that the film "invented its own
   version of his father's death" was not verified against the film.
9. **Wikipedia used as a tertiary source for spine facts.** Acceptable for uncontroversial dates and for
   _documenting_ a divergence (the 13-vs-14 children split), never used here as the sole basis for a
   correction to a primary text.
10. **No sales, print-run, or circulation data located** for any superlative about _Meditations_'
    standing (CLM-18).
11. **Reception claims not exhaustively swept.** The modern-Stoicism-industry framing rests on the
    PolitiFact item and the entity-gap SERP audit; no systematic survey of misquotation prevalence was
    attempted.
