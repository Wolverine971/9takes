## Bottom-line verdict

Yes — the writer understood him well enough to show me something new, and the new thing is good enough
that I would send this page to two people I argue about Stoicism with. Opening on the Alsium holiday
letters instead of a marble-bust quotation is the correct instinct, and the Book 1 debt-ledger read is
a genuinely fresh synthesis of material I already knew. This is not a listicle written by someone who
skimmed a Wikipedia summary.

But it has two trust breaks that a fan finds fast, and one of them is in the sentence that explains how
Marcus became a Stoic at all. The page never once names **Junius Rusticus** — the man who actually
handed him Epictetus — and the surrounding prose hands the credit to Fronto by proximity. That is the
kind of error that makes an informed reader stop trusting the rest, because Rusticus is not obscure; he
is the seventh entry in Book 1 and the reason the notebook exists. The second break is a slur assembled
from two different _Historia Augusta_ documents, two speakers and thirteen years apart, and dated to the
wrong event.

Both are repairable without touching the argument. Fix those and this is one of the better popular
pieces on Marcus I have read this year, because it does the thing the genre almost never does: it takes
the Fronto correspondence seriously as evidence rather than as trivia.

## What landed

**FAN-H1 — The Alsium opening.** _"It were easier to reconcile you to a polecat than to pleasure. Tell
me, Marcus, I beseech you, have you repaired to Alsium only to fast with the sea in sight?"_ Every
popular Marcus piece opens on the same three or four _Meditations_ lines. This one opens on Fronto
being funny at him, and it is the single best decision on the page. **Must survive revision:** it
establishes in one paragraph that the writer has been inside the correspondence, not just the notebook,
which buys the credibility everything downstream spends.

**FAN-H2 — _"His own teacher could not talk him into a beach."_** One line, freestanding, and it carries
the entire thesis. **Must survive:** it is the sentence I would quote if I shared this.

**FAN-H3 — The Book 1 debt ledger as the explanation of the beach.** _"A man who believes every good
thing in him is borrowed experiences rest as spending money he did not earn, while the lenders are dead
and cannot be repaid."_ This is the delight test passing outright. I knew Book 1 was seventeen entries
of gratitude. I knew about the paperwork at the circus games. I had never put them together, and once
put together they do not come apart. **Must survive:** this is the article's actual contribution to the
subject and the reason it is worth publishing at all.

**FAN-H4 — _"Nobody writes a numbered protocol for a problem they do not have."_** Med. 11.18 really is
a nine-point enumerated list with a tenth appended, and the packet confirms the description is exact.
The inference is one sentence and it does not overreach. **Must survive:** it is the cleanest piece of
reasoning on the page.

**FAN-H5 — Refusing the hypocrisy frame in the intro.** _"The easy version of this story would be
hypocrisy, and it is not available. Marcus Aurelius taught nobody, built no school, published no
doctrine."_ Naming and closing the lazy read before anyone can reach for it. **Must survive:** it is
what separates this from the "Stoicism is toxic masculinity, actually" genre.

**FAN-H6 — _"The most reproduced philosophy book in the world has a war for a return address."_**
Built on the two internal datelines — among the Quadi at the Granua, this in Carnuntum — which every
fan has read a hundred times and nobody has ever done anything with. **Must survive:** best single
sentence in the piece.

**FAN-H7 — _"A Five builds. This rehearses."_** The Type 5 rebuttal in the Rabbit Hole is a literary
observation about the book rather than a typology move, and it is right: the behavioural instructions
genuinely do not develop. **Must survive:** it is the moment the Enneagram frame earns its keep.

## What missed

**The article's own thesis has an earlier and better witness that it never calls.** The page argues the
discipline was applied to a temperament, not born from the job. The strongest evidence for that is a boy
of twelve, and it is missing (FAN-I1).

**The "modern Stoicism industry" beat is fought with one hand.** The section names a fake quote and
PolitiFact, and then stops. It never names the industry, never names a real line, and so the reader is
told that Marcus is relentlessly quoted without ever being shown the quotation that built the whole
market (FAN-C6).

**Fronto is used for thirty years of argument and his payoff is left on the floor.** The braid structure
is called "Two Archives," and the second archive's verdict on the first archive's author is one line
about tyrants. The page does not report it (FAN-I3).

**The Lyon paragraph reads like it was assembled from a summary rather than the document** (FAN-C8),
which is a shame, because including Lyon at all was the right call.

## What I expected

- **Rusticus named.** Not present anywhere on the page. This is the expectation whose failure does the
  most damage (FAN-R2, FAN-I2).
- **The twelve-year-old in the rough Greek cloak, sleeping on the ground.** Absent (FAN-I1).
- **A modern translation.** The page runs entirely on George Long, 1862. The fan reading Marcus in 2026
  is reading Hays, and the register gap is not cosmetic (FAN-C4).
- **"The obstacle is the way," or at minimum the Daily Stoic economy, named.** Absent (FAN-C6).
- **The _Gladiator_ correction.** Present and correctly handled — _"which invented its own version of
  his father's death."_ Good.
- **The fake-quote problem.** Present, sourced to PolitiFact. Good.
- **Faustina's reputation handled or explicitly refused.** Refused, and the FAQ says so out loud. I
  endorse this; the ancient allegations are hostile late tradition and the page is right not to launder
  them into colour.
- **Commodus left unresolved.** Present and unresolved, with the civil-war constraint named. Good.
- **Lyon.** Present, which I did not expect from a personality-analysis page, but under-sourced.

## What surprised me

**Welcome.** That the counterargument section concedes the real objection — _"Ones are supposed to
correct other people, and Marcus mostly declined to"_ — and then narrows the type instead of pretending
the problem away. Most typing content would have hidden that.

**Welcome.** _"he told the buyers that any of them who wanted their money back could return the goods
and have it."_ I had forgotten the refund offer entirely. It is the detail that makes the auction a
character beat instead of a fiscal anecdote.

**Welcome.** The Epictetus kissing-your-child line placed immediately after the arithmetic of the buried
children, with _"He did not have to imagine that."_ Restrained and devastating.

**Jarring, mildly.** _"the most durable self-help text in the Western canon."_ I understand the move —
the article is about the airport-book afterlife — but calling _Meditations_ self-help in the third
paragraph spends credibility the piece has not yet earned, and it is never cashed. Preference, recorded
as such, not promoted.

**Jarring.** Lucius Verus is introduced as a co-emperor whose elevation Marcus insisted on against his
own interest, and then dies in a three-word clause. Not an omission I would penalise on encyclopedic
grounds, but the page spends a paragraph making him matter and one clause disposing of him.

## Red flags

### FAN-R1 — BLOCKER — The Avidius Cassius slur is one quotation welded from two documents, and misdated

- **Passage:** "Why Marcus Aurelius Could Not Enjoy a Holiday" — _"During his 175 revolt, the general
  Avidius Cassius reportedly called Marcus 'a philosophical old woman' who 'wishes to be called merciful
  and hence suffers to live men whose manner of life he cannot sanction.'"_ Also TL;DR bullet: _"a rival
  general called him a philosophical old woman."_
- **Trust problem:** this is the page's designated critic-pressure anchor. A reader who follows the
  citation finds two different HA documents, two different speakers and two different dates spliced by a
  relative pronoun. The article already tells the reader to be suspicious of the HA, which makes the
  splice worse, not better: it signals source-awareness while doing the thing source-awareness exists to
  prevent.
- **Evidence:** packet CLM-01 / TP-5. "Philosophical old woman" is HA _Avidius Cassius_ **1.8**, inside
  an "Extract from the letter of **Verus**" — Lucius Verus reporting Cassius's insults to Marcus ("He
  calls you a philosophical old woman, me a half-witted spendthrift"). Verus died early 169, so the line
  cannot belong to the 175 revolt. The second clause is HA _Avidius Cassius_ **14.3**, a letter
  attributed to Cassius after he declared himself emperor, correctly 175. The chain for the colourful
  half is HA → a Verus letter generally judged spurious → Cassius's alleged words.
- **Minimum viable repair:** split them. Attribute the "old woman" line to what it is — a jibe reported
  at second hand in a letter the HA puts in Lucius Verus's mouth, undatable and probably forged, so
  before 169 at the latest — and keep the "wishes to be called merciful" clause where it belongs, in the
  175 letter. Two sentences instead of one. Fix the TL;DR bullet to match.
- **Expected benefit:** the critic beat survives a citation check, and the second-hand chain is itself
  more interesting than the flat attribution — a slur that circulated among his own co-emperor's
  correspondence is a better fact than a rebel's battlefield taunt.
- **Confidence:** high. The packet verified both passages verbatim against LacusCurtius.
- **Acceptance test:** no sentence on the page attributes both clauses to one speaker on one occasion,
  and no sentence dates "philosophical old woman" to 175.

### FAN-R2 — BLOCKER — The Epictetus credit reads as Fronto; Rusticus is never named

- **Passage:** "Who Marcus Aurelius Was, and How Rome Picked Him Twice" — _"He got twenty-three years to
  prepare, educated chiefly in rhetoric under Marcus Cornelius Fronto… Somewhere in there the training
  took a turn Fronto never fully forgave. Marcus found the Stoics, through the man he thanks for it in
  his notebook: 'I am indebted to him for being acquainted with the discourses of Epictetus, which he
  communicated to me out of his own collection.'"_
- **Trust problem:** the sentence never states a falsehood, but Fronto is named twice in the two
  preceding sentences and "the man" is never named at all, so the referent chain delivers Fronto. The
  predictable takeaway is the exact reverse of the fact, and it sits inside the page's origin claim —
  how Marcus reached Stoicism, which is the precondition for everything the article argues. Nothing
  later on the page corrects the impression, because Rusticus never appears.
- **Evidence:** packet CLM-02 — Med. **1.7** is the entry headed "From Rusticus." Fronto's own entry is
  **1.11** and says something else entirely. Confirmed independently: HA _Marcus_ 3.2–3, "he received
  most instruction from Junius Rusticus, whom he ever revered and whose disciple he became"; Wikipedia,
  _Junius Rusticus_, quotes both Med. 1.7 clauses and records no dispute about the relationship.
- **Minimum viable repair:** name him. "Marcus found the Stoics through a different teacher, Junius
  Rusticus, who lent him his own copy of Epictetus" — then the quotation. One clause.
- **Expected benefit:** removes the misattribution, and unlocks the single best available connection on
  the page (FAN-I2).
- **Confidence:** high on the misattribution risk; certain on the underlying fact.
- **Acceptance test:** the name "Rusticus" appears on the page, adjacent to the Med. 1.7 quotation, and
  no reader tracing the referent can land on Fronto.

### FAN-C1 — CONCERN — "four of them before he was ever emperor" does not match the standard child list

- **Passage:** "Marcus Aurelius, Faustina, and the Children He Buried" — _"He buried at least seven of
  his own children, most of them small, and four of them before he was ever emperor."_
- **Trust problem:** a precise number in the page's most emotionally weighted paragraph, checkable in one
  click, in a passage whose power depends on the arithmetic being unarguable.
- **Evidence:** packet CLM-13. Deaths before March 161 on the conventional list are Domitia Faustina
  (d. 151), the two 149 twins, Tiberius Aelius Antoninus (d. before 156) and the unknown child (d. before 158) = **five**, with Hadrianus a possible sixth. The packet located no source supporting exactly four.
- **Minimum viable repair:** "at least five of them before he was ever emperor," or drop the pre-accession
  count and keep "at least seven," which is safe on either the 13- or 14-child total.
- **Expected benefit:** the paragraph's central arithmetic stops being falsifiable by a reader with the
  Wikipedia child table open.
- **Confidence:** high.
- **Acceptance test:** every number in the paragraph is derivable from the conventional child list without
  requiring the reader to assume a different one.

### FAN-C2 — CONCERN — "a locked cabinet of Hadrian's" is not what the source says

- **Passage:** "How Marcus Aurelius Paid for a War He Could Not Win Quickly" — _"jewels he had found in a
  locked cabinet of Hadrian's."_
- **Trust problem:** small, but it is an invented detail inside the page's best-sourced narrative unit,
  and this is a passage fans know almost by heart. Anyone who checks HA 17.4 finds a different adjective.
- **Evidence:** packet CLM-08. HA 17.4 reads "a particularly **holy** cabinet of Hadrian's." "Locked" is
  not in the source.
- **Minimum viable repair:** use "holy," or drop the adjective. "Holy" is the better detail anyway — it
  means he sold consecrated objects to pay for a war, which is a sharper fact than a lock.
- **Expected benefit:** removes an unsourced embellishment and upgrades the image.
- **Confidence:** high.
- **Acceptance test:** the adjective attached to Hadrian's cabinet appears in HA 17.4.

### FAN-C3 — CONCERN — Three different spans for the same argument on one page

- **Passages:** H2 _"Two Archives, One Emperor: Marcus Aurelius at 22 and at 55"_; body _"Thirty-three
  years, two languages, two readers, one report"_; TL;DR and FAQ 1 _"Thirty years later"_; timeline entry
  _"Notebook, age ~54"_; closing _"He was fifty-four."_
- **Trust problem:** the heading says 55, the timeline says ~54, the body says thirty-three years and the
  TL;DR says thirty. The braid structure is the page's signature form and its headline number wobbles
  four ways. A detail-reader reads that as carelessness and starts checking everything else.
- **Evidence:** packet CLM-14. 143 (age 22) to c. 175 (age ~54) is thirty-two years; _Meditations_ is not
  internally datable beyond the c. 170–180 convention, so no precise span is recoverable anyway.
- **Minimum viable repair:** pick one span and use it in all four places. "At 22 and at 54" with "thirty-two
  years" is defensible; "roughly three decades apart" is safer and costs nothing.
- **Expected benefit:** the signature section stops undermining itself arithmetically.
- **Confidence:** high.
- **Acceptance test:** heading, timeline, body, TL;DR and FAQ 1 all state the same span, and it matches the
  ages given.

### FAN-C4 — CONCERN — George Long's 1862 English works against the article's own thesis

- **Passage:** every _Meditations_ quotation. _"Begin the morning by saying to thyself, I shall meet with
  the busybody, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial."_
- **Trust problem:** the article's central claim is that these are raw private corrections a man wrote to
  himself in a tent. Long's "thou," "thy," "wroth" render them as Victorian scripture — exactly the
  marble-bust register the article is trying to dismantle. The modern reader who has actually read
  _Meditations_ has read Hays, whose 2.1 opens "When you wake up in the morning, tell yourself: the people
  I deal with today will be meddling, ungrateful, arrogant, dishonest, jealous and surly." That version
  sounds like a man talking to himself. Long sounds like a lectern.
- **Evidence:** packet S-03 and research limitation 2 — "Long's 1862 English is a Victorian rendering."
  The choice is presumably public-domain licensing, which is a real constraint, not an error.
- **Minimum viable repair:** keep Long for quotation (licensing), but add one sentence somewhere in "The
  Book He Never Meant Anyone to Read" acknowledging that the English most readers know is a modern one and
  that the Victorian diction is later dress, not his voice — he wrote plain camp Greek. Alternatively,
  gloss the two or three load-bearing quotations in modern English inline.
- **Expected benefit:** stops the translation from arguing against the thesis, and pre-empts the informed
  reader's "why does this sound like a hymnal" reaction.
- **Confidence:** medium-high. The constraint is real; the effect on the argument is real too.
- **Acceptance test:** a reader who knows Hays is told, once, why the quotations do not sound like the book
  they own.

### FAN-C5 — CONCERN — The affectionate register is used as evidence and then declined as a subject

- **Passages:** _"The register runs hotter than that in places, and what it meant is contested scholarly
  ground. What matters here is the temperament the discipline was applied to."_ And in the Rabbit Hole:
  _"The early Fronto letters run warm to a degree that has occupied scholars for two centuries"_ — offered
  as 1w2 wing evidence.
- **Trust problem:** the page banks the warmth as diagnostic evidence for the wing call while refusing to
  characterise it. To a fan who has read those letters, that reads as having it both ways, and the coyness
  draws more attention than a plain sentence would.
- **Evidence:** packet disputes item 6 — genuinely contested, with a binding handling rule against claims
  in either direction. So the _reticence_ is correct; the problem is that the page leans on the material
  twice while gesturing at it.
- **Minimum viable repair:** say plainly what the letters contain — longing, dream-embraces, a day without
  a letter unbearable — and then decline the interpretation explicitly, once, rather than twice hinting.
  The packet's FP-6 and TP-1 supply verbatim material that lets the page be concrete without adjudicating.
- **Expected benefit:** the reticence reads as discipline rather than squeamishness, and the wing evidence
  becomes checkable.
- **Confidence:** medium.
- **Acceptance test:** the reader can tell what is in the letters without leaving the page, and the page
  states once — not twice — that their nature is unsettled.

### FAN-C6 — CONCERN — The Stoicism-industry beat names a fake quote but never a real one

- **Passage:** "The Book He Never Meant Anyone to Read" — _"He is quoted so relentlessly that he gets
  quoted saying things he never wrote: PolitiFact had to fact-check one of the most viral of them in
  September 2019."_
- **Trust problem:** the paragraph asserts an industry and then evidences it only with a forgery. A reader
  who has never encountered the Daily Stoic economy has no idea what is being described; a reader who has
  notices that the page declined to name it or quote the line that built it. The claim "quoted relentlessly"
  is left standing on a fact-check about a quotation nobody said.
- **Evidence:** packet CLM-18 records that no circulation data supports the superlatives; the PolitiFact
  item is the only reception evidence carried. The obvious missing item is Med. **5.20**, the passage Hays
  renders "The impediment to action advances action. What stands in the way becomes the way" — the source
  text of the best-selling modern Stoic book and the phrase most people who have never opened _Meditations_
  can nonetheless recite.
- **Minimum viable repair:** one sentence naming the real line and what was built on it, before the fake-quote
  sentence. Naming the modern industry in prose requires no outbound link.
- **Expected benefit:** the reception beat acquires a concrete referent, and the contrast the paragraph is
  reaching for — real corrections versus poster philosophy — finally has both terms.
- **Confidence:** medium-high.
- **Acceptance test:** the paragraph contains at least one genuine, cited _Meditations_ line that a reader
  would recognise from outside the book. **Verify Long's exact 5.20 wording before quoting** — Long's
  Victorian rendering of that sentence differs materially from Hays's and must not be quoted from memory.

### FAN-C7 — CONCERN — "How Rome Picked Him Twice" delivers one pick

- **Passage:** H2 1 and the section under it. The section gives exactly one selection event — Hadrian's
  138 condition — followed by an accession in 161 that the article itself frames as Marcus _refusing_ the
  terms offered.
- **Trust problem:** a heading that promises a structure the section does not deliver. Minor on its own, but
  it is the first H2 and it sets the reader's expectation for how carefully the page is built.
- **Evidence:** read the section against its own heading; there is no second pick in the text.
- **Minimum viable repair:** supply the first pick, which exists and is better than the heading deserves.
  Hadrian had his eye on Marcus from childhood and renamed him for it — HA _Marcus_ 1.10, "After the death
  of his real father, however, Hadrian called him Annius **Verissimus**," and 4.1, "He was reared under the
  eye of Hadrian, who called him Verissimus." A boy nicknamed "most truthful" by the emperor, then written
  into the succession as a condition, is two picks, and the nickname is thematically free money for this
  particular article.
- **Expected benefit:** heading pays off; and the article gains its earliest datable evidence that the
  exacting quality was noticed in him before anyone handed him anything.
- **Confidence:** high.
- **Acceptance test:** the section contains two distinct moments at which Marcus is chosen, or the heading
  changes.

### FAN-C8 — CONCERN — The Lyon paragraph mis-describes its own source

- **Passage:** _"How much he knew or ordered is contested: the only detailed account is Eusebius, writing
  more than a century later, and Tertullian, alive at the time, called Marcus a protector of Christians
  rather than a persecutor."_
- **Trust problem:** including Lyon at all was the right call and I want it kept. But the sourcing sentence
  is the one thing on the page that reads as second-hand, and it is in the paragraph most likely to be
  scrutinised. Any fan who has argued this before knows the account is not Eusebius's.
- **Evidence:** packet CLM-07 / S-07. The detailed account is a **contemporary letter from the churches of
  Lyon and Vienne**, quoted at length by Eusebius (_EH_ 5.1) — so the testimony is contemporary and only its
  transmission is late. That same letter reports an imperial rescript: at 5.1.44 the governor holds
  prisoners "concerning whom he had written to Cæsar, and whose answer he was awaiting," and at 5.1.47,
  "For Cæsar commanded that they should be put to death, but that any who might deny should be set free."
  The page promises this "does not resolve, and it should not be smoothed over," then omits the single most
  damaging item in the record.
- **Minimum viable repair:** correct the description — a contemporary letter preserved by Eusebius, not
  Eusebius's own account — and add the rescript in one clause with the counterweights the packet lists
  (the letter names no emperor personally; Irenaeus in Lyon is silent; Tertullian calls him a protector;
  Marcus was weeks away). The paragraph gets longer by roughly two sentences.
- **Expected benefit:** the page's own promise of non-smoothing becomes true, and the paragraph stops
  reading like it was written from a summary.
- **Confidence:** high.
- **Acceptance test:** the sentence describing the source correctly identifies the contemporary letter, and
  the rescript appears with its counterarguments.

### FAN-C9 — CONCERN — Book 8's opening is quoted from mid-sentence and framed as a verdict

- **Passages:** five. TL;DR (_"he opened Book 8 by telling himself he had fallen short and everyone could
  see it"_), FAQ 1, the timeline entry, and the closing line (_"the verdict is right there in the first
  line of Book 8"_).
- **Trust problem:** the closing beat — the last thing the reader takes away — points at a passage which, on
  the page, is about giving up the desire for reputation. A Stoicism-literate reader who opens Book 8 to
  check the closing line finds a different argument than the one they were promised.
- **Evidence:** packet CLM-03. Long's full 8.1 opens "**This reflection also tends to the removal of the
  desire of empty fame**, that it is no longer in thy power…" and continues "throw away the thought, How
  thou shalt seem to others." Also, "both to many others and to thyself" is narrower than "everyone."
- **Minimum viable repair:** keep the quotation, but stop calling it the first line and stop calling it a
  verdict delivered to himself in isolation. Acknowledge the fame frame in a half-sentence — it does not
  damage the argument, because the shortfall is still asserted as fact inside it; he is arguing _from_ his
  own failure, which is arguably better evidence than arguing _about_ it. Change "everyone could see it" to
  "others could see it."
- **Expected benefit:** the page's most-repeated claim survives the one check a knowledgeable reader is most
  likely to run.
- **Confidence:** high.
- **Acceptance test:** no instance on the page describes 8.1 as a standalone self-verdict, and none says
  "everyone."

## Specific improvements

### FAN-I1 — Open the discipline at twelve, not at nineteen (highest priority)

- **Where:** "Who Marcus Aurelius Was, and How Rome Picked Him Twice," alongside the FAN-C7 repair.
- **What is missing:** the most famous anecdote about young Marcus in the entire tradition, and the earliest
  datable evidence for this article's exact thesis. HA _Marcus_ **2.6**, verified verbatim at LacusCurtius
  today: _"For when he was twelve years old he adopted the dress and, a little later, the hardiness of a
  philosopher, pursuing his studies clad in a rough Greek cloak and sleeping on the ground; at his mother's
  solicitation, however, he reluctantly consented to sleep on a couch strewn with skins."_
- **Reader effect it fixes:** the page currently begins its evidence at nineteen, which leaves the strongest
  skeptical reading open — that the austerity was the job, or Fronto's Stoic turn, or the plague. A boy of
  twelve on the floor of a rich senatorial house, six years before Fronto and twenty-three before the
  throne, closes that door. It also explains "Rome Picked Him Twice" and sets up the self-preservation
  subtype argument in the Rabbit Hole, which currently rests only on the Ariston letter and Dio.
- **Sourcing note:** HA, so Tier 3, and the page already knows how to handle that — it flags the HA in-text
  twice. Handle it identically. The mother's intervention is the detail that makes it a scene rather than a
  statistic.
- **Expected benefit:** the article's central claim gains a thirty-year head start and its best image.
- **Confidence:** high.
- **Acceptance test:** the page contains at least one dated behavioural datum predating the Fronto
  correspondence, flagged with the same HA caveat the page applies elsewhere.

### FAN-I2 — Name Rusticus, then connect him to Lyon

- **Where:** the FAN-R2 repair, with a callback available in the Lyon paragraph.
- **What is missing:** the man who made Marcus a Stoic — Junius Rusticus, Med. 1.7, "whom he ever revered
  and whose disciple he became" (HA _Marcus_ 3.2–3) — served as urban prefect of Rome between 162 and 168,
  and in that role presided over the trial that ended in Justin Martyr's execution, probably in 165
  (Wikipedia, _Junius Rusticus_, which records no dispute on either point).
- **Reader effect it creates:** this is the delight test. The article already covers both halves — how Marcus
  reached Stoicism, and Christians dying under his reign — and never notices that the same man sits at both
  ends. The teacher who handed him Epictetus condemned a Christian philosopher to death while Marcus was on
  the throne. That is a fact an informed fan knows in pieces and has never seen assembled, and it does real
  argumentative work: it makes the Christian question concrete and proximate instead of a distant
  administrative fog, without asserting anything about Marcus's own culpability.
- **Minimum viable form:** one clause naming Rusticus at the Epictetus quotation, and one sentence in the
  Lyon paragraph noting the connection with no causal claim attached.
- **Expected benefit:** repairs the blocker, supplies the page's best "I never connected those" moment, and
  strengthens the section a fan is most likely to arrive already arguing about.
- **Confidence:** high on both facts; the identification of the teacher with the prefect is treated as
  settled by the standard reference and is not flagged as contested.
- **Acceptance test:** a reader finishes the page able to name who gave Marcus his Epictetus, and the Lyon
  paragraph contains no causal claim about Marcus arising from the Rusticus connection.

### FAN-I3 — Report what the notebook says about Fronto

- **Where:** "Two Archives, One Emperor," at the close of the braid.
- **What is missing:** the second archive's verdict on the first. Med. **1.11**, in the same Long text the
  page already quotes: _"From Fronto I learned to observe what envy, and duplicity, and hypocrisy are in a
  tyrant…"_ That is the whole entry. Packet confirms it verbatim and notes the draft does not quote it.
- **Reader effect it creates:** the section's premise is two archives thirty years apart. Twenty-five years
  of the warmest correspondence in Latin literature — the polecat, the dream-embraces, the unbearable day
  without a letter — reduce, in the private ledger, to one line about spotting tyrants. That is the braid
  closing on itself, and it belongs to this article and no other, which is exactly what the section is for.
- **Expected benefit:** converts the section's structure into a payoff instead of an arrangement, and does it
  with a quotation already inside the page's chosen edition.
- **Confidence:** high.
- **Acceptance test:** the "Two Archives" section ends on something the second archive says about the first,
  not on a restatement of the thesis.

### FAN-I4 — The other accident, in bronze

- **Where:** "The Book He Never Meant Anyone to Read," beside _"Everything you can buy at an airport with his
  face on it descends from that accident."_
- **What is missing:** the page invokes his face twice — _"the face that now sells composure on a million book
  covers,"_ _"the meme with the marble bust"_ — and never notices that we have the face for the same reason we
  have the book. The equestrian statue in the Capitoline is the only surviving bronze equestrian statue of a
  Roman emperor; roughly twenty-two others are attested and all were melted down. It survived because it was
  mistaken for Constantine, the first Christian emperor.
- **Reader effect it creates:** two accidents, same shape — the notebook survives because a Byzantine bishop
  happened to own a crumbling copy, the face survives because Christians mistook him for one of their own. It
  is a one-sentence closing rhyme, and it converts the article's "survival by accident" argument from an
  assertion into a pattern.
- **Sourcing note:** the mistaken-identity explanation is the standard account and is widely reported, but it
  is an art-historical inference rather than a documented decision — phrase it as the usual explanation, not
  as established fact.
- **Expected benefit:** the closing section's central idea gets a second instance, and the "meme with the
  marble bust" line acquires a spine.
- **Confidence:** medium-high on the explanation as standard; high that it is worth including.
- **Acceptance test:** the claim is stated as the conventional explanation, not as documented fact.
