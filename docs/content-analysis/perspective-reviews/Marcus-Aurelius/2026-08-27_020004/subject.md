## Bottom-line verdict

This is a fair-minded article with a selection problem at its center, and the selection problem runs
in one direction.

The fairness instincts are unusually good. It refuses the hypocrisy frame that was sitting there for
the taking. It omits the Faustina sex rumors and says in the FAQ why. It leaves the death location
disputed. It explains the man's coldness without excusing it, then explicitly declines to let him off
("That still made him hard to be around, and Fronto was right to push"). It rejects a dramatic piece
of _Historia Augusta_ legend that would have made a better paragraph. Those are the marks of a writer
trying to be just to someone who cannot answer.

But the portrait is built by quoting Marcus accusing himself and stopping before he stops. I verified
the two _Meditations_ passages the article uses as its self-verdict evidence, and both continue, in
the very next clause, into self-acceptance:

- **8.1** — the article's closing beat, "the verdict is right there in the first line of Book 8" —
  is a passage whose stated purpose is "the removal of the desire of empty fame," and which turns to
  "throw away the thought, How thou shalt seem to others, **and be content** if thou shalt live the
  rest of thy life in such a way as thy nature wills."
- **5.9** — quoted as "an instruction he apparently needed" — continues "return back again, **and be
  content if the greater part of what thou doest is consistent with man's nature**."

Both are cut at the exact word where the man stops prosecuting and starts absolving. The result is a
final paragraph that tells the reader Marcus "had already decided, and never revised" by citing the
passage in which he revises. That lands hardest because it happens two paragraphs after the article
correctly indicts the Stoicism industry for clipping him onto posters. The page does a more careful
version of the thing it is condemning.

Two other trust breaks are attribution rather than framing: a rival's insult assembled from two
documents thirteen years apart and dated to the wrong event, and the most damaging personal claim on
the page — five days of mourning for a dead seven-year-old — quoted up to, and stopping immediately
before, the clause in which the source explains itself.

None of this makes the article unfair on balance. Marcus comes out recognizable, complex, credited
for real things, and held responsible for real things. All three blockers are repairable by adding
text that already exists in sources the article is already using, and two of the three repairs make
the piece _better_: a man who keeps writing down both the shortfall and the permission to let it go
is more interesting than a man with a fixed verdict.

## What landed

**SUBJECT-H1 — The refusal of the hypocrisy frame.**

> "The easy version of this story would be hypocrisy, and it is not available. Marcus Aurelius taught
> nobody, built no school, published no doctrine, and addressed his notebook to a single reader."

The cheapest possible article about a moralist is the one that catches him breaking his own rule.
This one closes that door in the third paragraph and commits to a harder question. From a fairness
standpoint that is the single most important structural decision on the page, and it is correct: the
notebook genuinely had no audience. (The sentence has a factual problem — see SUBJECT-C2 — but the
move must survive.)

**SUBJECT-H2 — The empathy turn that refuses to absolve.**

> "That is the whole explanation of the beach. A man who believes every good thing in him is borrowed
> experiences rest as spending money he did not earn... That still made him hard to be around, and
> Fronto was right to push."

This is the fairness contract executed properly. It explains the behavior from inside, using Book 1's
own accounting, and then concedes the cost to the people around him instead of converting the
explanation into a defense. Most sympathetic profiles stop one sentence early. This one does not.

**SUBJECT-H3 — Faustina protected, with the reasoning stated.**

> "Ancient allegations about Faustina's conduct come from Cassius Dio and the Historia Augusta and
> are best treated as ancient rumor rather than fact."

The salacious material was available, well-known, and would have driven traffic. The article excludes
it and tells the reader it is excluding it. That protects a third party who has even less ability to
answer than the subject does, and it raises rather than lowers the page's authority.

**SUBJECT-H4 — Holding the Commodus verdict open.**

> "Whether Marcus had a real alternative is genuinely disputed... The shape of it is clearer than the
> verdict."

The article's hardest charge against the subject is stated plainly ("applied none of that scrutiny to
the one decision that outlived him") while refusing to pretend the historiography is settled. That is
how you criticize someone without inventing certainty.

**SUBJECT-H5 — Rejecting a piece of legend that would have helped the story.**

> "The Historia Augusta claims he saw it coming and wished his son would die young rather than become
> another Nero. That is the sort of retrospective prophecy the collection invents."

Turning down a dramatic quote because the source is untrustworthy — when the same source is being
used elsewhere — is evidence of good faith that a subject would notice.

**SUBJECT-H6 — The sickroom detail as the last word on him as a father.**

> "on the seventh day of his illness he admitted only Commodus, then sent him out of the room at
> once, in fear that the boy would catch what was killing him."

Placed at the end of the section that is hardest on him as a parent. It restores the man without
retracting the criticism. Do not move it or cut it.

**SUBJECT-H7 — Defending him against fabricated quotes.**

> "He is quoted so relentlessly that he gets quoted saying things he never wrote: PolitiFact had to
> fact-check one of the most viral of them in September 2019."

The article takes his side against the industry that has flattened him. This is exactly the standing
correction the subject would want made — which is why SUBJECT-R1 is the finding that most needs
fixing, and why fixing it costs the piece nothing.

**SUBJECT-H8 — A stated falsification condition.**

> "What would change our minds: evidence that the self-criticism was situational."

Naming what would defeat the reading is the difference between an argument and an assignment. It
signals to the subject that the claim is offered as testable rather than pronounced.

## What missed

**The article never once quotes Marcus being gentle with himself, though his notebook supplies it
repeatedly and the article is quoting from the exact sections where it appears.** The promised effect
is a portrait drawn from the man's own private words. The delivered effect is a portrait drawn from
one half of them. This is not taste — see SUBJECT-R1 for the verified texts. A reader who buys the
book on the strength of this page will find a substantially warmer document than the page described.

**The diagnosis converts a school exercise into a personal symptom without ever naming that it is a
school exercise.** The article's evidence is that Marcus performed structured self-examination in
writing over years. That is what Stoic _hypomnemata_ are; the practice was prescribed, not invented
by him. The article never lets Marcus's own account of what he was doing — philosophy as therapy of
the soul — compete with the psychological reading. See SUBJECT-C9.

**"Two Archives, One Emperor" promises a demonstration and delivers an assertion.** "Thirty-three
years, two languages, two readers, one report" is the payoff line, but the two archives are never
actually held against each other to show what neither could show alone. Since this is the section the
whole fairness case rests on — that the self-criticism is dispositional, not situational — the
thinnest section is carrying the heaviest load.

## What I expected

Things a reader assessing whether this man was treated fairly would reasonably need, which the page
does not supply:

1. **His own reason for doing it.** The article says what the practice reveals about him and never
   what he said it was for. Marcus is explicit throughout the notebook that the exercises are aimed
   at living according to nature. That is available in the same books already being quoted.
2. **Med. 11.18's closing.** The article cites 11.18 for the nine-rule anger protocol — correctly and
   precisely — but omits that the same section ends by naming self-reproach itself among the
   "aberrations of the superior faculty" to be guarded against. The one place where the subject
   directly addresses the behavior the article makes his defining trait is not on the page.
3. **The occasion of the Ariston letter.** It is used five times as the cleanest evidence on the
   record. Its occasion is that he owed Fronto a writing exercise and had not done it.
4. **Any acknowledgment of his public philosophical acts.** He endowed four chairs of philosophy at
   Athens in 176, one for each major school including the Epicureans he did not agree with.
5. **What the type does not explain.** The Rabbit Hole handles this well for Types 5 and 6 and for the
   non-corrector objection. The main body never tells a reader who will not open the accordion that
   there is anything the reading fails to cover.

Not expected and correctly absent: encyclopedic biography, the Faustina rumors, a hypochondria claim,
and the Verus anxiety letter. The REVIEWER NOTES handling flags match what is on the page.

## What surprised me

**Welcome.** That the article says "Fronto was right to push." A page arguing that a man's harshest
critic was correct about him, inside the same paragraph that explains why he was that way, is rarer
than it should be. Also that the FAQ names its own exclusion of the Faustina material rather than
silently omitting it.

**Jarring.** That the closing inversion (SUBJECT-R1) sits three paragraphs after the PolitiFact
passage. The article establishes that Marcus is systematically misquoted, then ends on a clipped
quote. A reader who checks the citation — and this page invites checking, since it names book and
section — finds the article standing where it just said not to stand.

**Also jarring.** The TL;DR bullet: "he mourned his seven-year-old son for five days and took meetings
during them." It attributes ("The ancient life of him says") but drops the in-body flag that the
source is unreliable and the number possibly invented, and it drops the source's own explanation for
the brevity. The most-scanned element on the page carries the harshest form of the most sensitive
claim.

## Red flags

### Blockers

**SUBJECT-R1 — The closing beat inverts the passage it is built on, and the same cut is made twice.**

- **Location:** final section "The Book He Never Meant Anyone to Read," last paragraph — "That reader
  had already decided, and never revised, and the verdict is right there in the first line of Book 8."
  Also the timeline entry at c. 175 ("Book 8 opens:"), TL;DR bullet 2, FAQ 1, and the diagnosis
  section's use of Book 5.
- **Reader effect / trust problem:** the article's last word on the man is that his self-verdict never
  changed, cited to a passage in which it changes. A reader who opens Long's Book 8 finds an argument
  for releasing the desire to be seen as a philosopher, not a condemnation.
- **Evidence:** Long 8.1 in full begins "**This reflection also tends to the removal of the desire of
  empty fame**, that it is no longer in thy power to have lived the whole of thy life..." and turns at
  "If then thou hast truly seen where the matter lies, **throw away the thought, How thou shalt seem
  to others, and be content** if thou shalt live the rest of thy life in such a way as thy nature
  wills," closing on the principles that make a man "just, temperate, manly, free." Verified against
  the Standard Ebooks Long text; the frame and turn are also recorded at packet CLM-03. The same cut
  recurs at 5.9, which the article quotes as "when thou hast failed, return back again" — verified
  continuation: "**and be content if the greater part of what thou doest is consistent with man's
  nature**." Two quotations, both severed at the clause where self-accusation turns into permission.
  Separately, the timeline's "Book 8 opens:" begins mid-sentence, and "everyone could see it" is
  broader than Long's "both to many others and to thyself."
- **Minimum viable repair:** carry 8.1's frame and turn at one of its five appearances — the closing
  is the right one. The ending survives intact and gets sharper: he wrote down the shortfall _and_ the
  permission to stop caring who saw it, for ten years, and kept needing to write both. Restore 5.9's
  "be content if the greater part" clause, or drop the gloss "all it bought him was a faster
  recovery," which the full sentence does not support. Fix "Book 8 opens" to "Book 8 turns," and
  "everyone" to "others."
- **Expected benefit:** removes the page's one self-inflicted wound — a misquotation charge on a page
  whose thesis is that he is misquoted — and gives the closing a genuinely non-obvious final image.
- **Confidence:** high. Both continuations independently verified against the stated edition.
- **Acceptance test:** a reader who opens Long 8.1 and 5.9 immediately after finishing the article
  finds nothing in either section that contradicts how the article characterized it.

**SUBJECT-R2 — The rival's insult is assembled from two documents, two speakers, and two dates, and
placed at an event it cannot belong to.**

- **Location:** "Why Marcus Aurelius Could Not Enjoy a Holiday" — "During his 175 revolt, the general
  Avidius Cassius reportedly called Marcus 'a philosophical old woman' who 'wishes to be called
  merciful and hence suffers to live men whose manner of life he cannot sanction.'" Also TESTIMONY
  LEDGER item 5, which records it as one quote from one source.
- **Reader effect / trust problem:** this is the article's designated critic-pressure anchor and the
  most quotable hostile line on the page. It will be lifted and recirculated. As written, the speaker
  attribution, the date, and the document are all wrong for the first half.
- **Evidence:** packet CLM-01 / TP-5, verified verbatim at both loci. "Philosophical old woman" is at
  _Historia Augusta_, _Avidius Cassius_ 1.8, inside an "Extract from the letter of **Verus**" —
  Lucius Verus reporting to Marcus what Cassius says ("He calls you a philosophical old woman, me a
  half-witted spendthrift"). Verus died in early 169, so the line cannot date from the 175 revolt, and
  the letter is generally judged spurious even by HA standards because it calls Antoninus Pius Verus's
  grandfather. Only the second clause is from 175 (_Avidius Cassius_ 14.3, a letter attributed to
  Cassius after he declared himself emperor). The draft's hedge — "survives only through the Historia
  Augusta, which puts speeches in mouths" — covers reliability but not attribution or date, and the
  real chain is HA → probably-forged Verus letter → Cassius's alleged words.
- **Minimum viable repair:** split the two quotes. Keep the "merciful" line at 175 with its existing
  hedge. Either drop "philosophical old woman" or give it its own clause: a slur Cassius was reported
  to have used, relayed second-hand in a letter the _Historia Augusta_ attributes to Lucius Verus and
  scholars generally judge forged, so from before 169 if from anywhere.
- **Expected benefit:** the page's most repeatable hostile quote stops being checkable-and-wrong; the
  critic pressure survives, because the 175 line is the sharper of the two anyway.
- **Confidence:** high.
- **Acceptance test:** every hostile quotation on the page names its document, its speaker of record,
  and its date, and none is placed at an event it cannot belong to.

**SUBJECT-R3 — The five-days-of-mourning quotation stops immediately before the clause in which its
own source explains it.**

- **Location:** "Marcus Aurelius, Faustina, and the Children He Buried" — "'For no more than five days
  did he mourn him,' the biographer writes, 'and even during this period, when consulted on public
  affairs he gave some time to them.' The source is unreliable and the number may be invented. The
  behavior it describes is the behavior Fronto had been complaining about for twenty years." Also
  TL;DR bullet 6 and FAQ 5.
- **Reader effect / trust problem:** this is the most damaging human claim on the page — a father who
  gave his dead seven-year-old five days and took meetings during them. The article flags the source
  as possibly invented and then, in the next sentence, revalidates the claim by pattern-matching it to
  Fronto, while withholding the explanation the source itself supplies. A disclaimer that is
  neutralized in the following sentence is not a disclaimer.
- **Evidence:** HA 21.5, the immediately following clause, verified verbatim at packet CLM-10 /
  TP-4: "**And because the games of Jupiter Optimus Maximus were then in progress and he did not wish
  to have them interrupted by public mourning**, he merely ordered that statues should be decreed for
  his dead son." On the source's own account the brevity is a public-ceremonial decision about state
  religious games, not (or not only) a temperamental one.
- **Minimum viable repair:** carry the 21.5 clause. The point survives and is fairer: even on the
  biographer's own explanation, he took public business during the mourning days — the reader can
  weigh a man choosing not to interrupt the games against a man who could not stop working. Add the
  source hedge to the TL;DR bullet, or soften "took meetings during them," which is the article's
  compression rather than the source's language.
- **Expected benefit:** the section's emotional force is undiminished and the article stops making its
  harshest characterological inference on a truncated sentence.
- **Confidence:** high.
- **Acceptance test:** a reader who opens HA 21.3–5 finds no context in the passage that materially
  changes the inference the article draws from it.

### Concerns

**SUBJECT-C1 — The man Marcus actually credits for Stoicism is never named, in a sentence framed by
Fronto.**
"Marcus found the Stoics, through the man he thanks for it in his notebook: 'I am indebted to him for
being acquainted with the discourses of Epictetus...'" The quotation is Med. 1.7, the entry headed
"**From Rusticus**" (packet CLM-02, verified). The two preceding sentences name Fronto twice; "the
man" is never named. Fronto has his own Book 1 entry — 1.11, and it is about learning what hypocrisy
looks like in a tyrant. _Effect:_ the natural reading hands Fronto the credit and reverses the fact,
inside the sentence establishing how Marcus reached Stoicism at all — and it does so on a page whose
moral argument is that Marcus credited others by name with everything. _Repair:_ insert "Junius
Rusticus." Two words. _Confidence:_ high. _Test:_ no reader finishes the paragraph believing Fronto
introduced Marcus to Epictetus.

**SUBJECT-C2 — "built no school" is literally false and is load-bearing.**
Marcus established four chairs of philosophy at Athens in 176 — Platonic, Aristotelian, Stoic and
Epicurean — including for schools he did not belong to (Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, entry
"Marcus Aurelius"). _Effect:_ the sentence that rules hypocrisy out and licenses the entire thesis
contains a one-click-checkable error, and it erases the one public act by which he supported
philosophy for anyone but himself. _Repair:_ "built no school of his own" or "founded chairs for other
men's schools and none for a doctrine of his own" — the latter is a better sentence and reinforces the
point. _Confidence:_ high. _Test:_ the no-audience claim survives a reader who knows about the Athens
endowment.

**SUBJECT-C3 — A literary self-presentation is read as a behavioral record.**
The Ariston letter carries the epigraph, FAQ 1, the timeline, the diagnosis ("A full behavioral
cascade... by a young man who assumed he was writing to one friend") and the subtype call ("bodily
austerity used as a moral instrument. The Ariston letter ends its penances with 'starve myself'").
The packet's own reading: it "is testimony about his self-description, not a behavioural record," the
occasion is that he had not written the exercise Fronto assigned him, the penance passage is offered
as the excuse, and he closes by resolving to set Ariston aside and read Cicero. The reader is his
rhetoric tutor — the man grading his prose — in a genre that prized vivid self-presentation. _Effect:_
a twenty-five-year-old's stylish apology for missing a deadline becomes evidence of self-starvation.
_Repair:_ one clause naming the occasion at the diagnosis (not the epigraph, which should stay clean),
and let Dio 72.6 carry the austerity claim in the subtype paragraph, since it is independent
testimony. _Confidence:_ high. _Test:_ a reader who opens the letter is not surprised by what
surrounds the quoted lines.

**SUBJECT-C4 — Marcus's own verdict on self-reproach is missing.**
Med. 11.18 closes by listing self-reproach among four aberrations to guard against: "the fourth is
when thou shalt reproach thyself for anything, for this is an evidence of the diviner part within thee
being overpowered and yielding to the less honourable part" (packet, verified). The article quotes
11.18 for the nine-rule protocol and stops before this. _Effect:_ the subject explicitly named the
habit the article makes his signature, and called it a fault. _Repair:_ one sentence, best placed in
the Rabbit Hole's counterargument section. It narrows the reading rather than breaking it — he named
it as an error and kept doing it for another decade, which is a stronger version of the article's own
claim. _Confidence:_ high. _Test:_ the counterargument section engages the best textual objection
available, not only the type-vs-type ones.

**SUBJECT-C5 — Fronto's harshest line is teasing inside a love-letter, and past-tense-closed.**
"I would call you an austere and unreasonable, even at times, stung by anger, a disagreeable sort of
person" is the second of three "trifles" Fronto offers to prove he loves Marcus (the others are
dream-embraces and smiling at his portraits); it opens "**Time was I did this**" and is immediately
followed by Fronto saying he could not bear to hear the same criticism from anyone else (packet
CLM-05 / TP-1, verified). The article's second use drops the frame: "That is the person closest to
him, describing a young man with a temper he was already learning to keep off his face." _Effect:_ the
only outside description of his temperament is presented as testimony against him when it is offered
as an affectionate retraction. _Repair:_ keep "Time was" or one clause of frame. _Confidence:_ high.
_Test:_ the reader can tell the quotation comes from a declaration of affection.

**SUBJECT-C6 — "a letter of lavish praise" misstates the stimulus in the discriminator letter.**
FAQ 2. Marcus's own first sentence says Fronto mixed abuse in deliberately to make the praise credible,
and part of the reply is Marcus announcing he has seen through the technique (packet CLM-06, verified).
_Effect:_ the Type-1-over-Type-6 discriminator rests entirely on this letter, and the FAQ is the
element most likely to be scraped and quoted out of context. _Repair:_ "a letter of praise" or "praise
he could see the machinery behind." _Confidence:_ high. _Test:_ the FAQ's description matches the
letter's first paragraph.

**SUBJECT-C7 — "four of them before he was ever emperor" is not supported by the standard list.**
The conventional child list gives five deaths before March 161, possibly six (packet CLM-13). "At
least seven" total is safe. _Effect:_ a precise, checkable number inside the page's most emotionally
weighted passage. _Repair:_ "at least four" or "five of them." _Confidence:_ medium-high — the packet
notes no source supports exactly four. _Test:_ every number in the grief section is derivable from a
cited list.

**SUBJECT-C8 — The Lyon framing is more favorable to him than the record supports.**
"the only detailed account is Eusebius, writing more than a century later, and Tertullian, alive at
the time, called Marcus a protector." Per packet CLM-07: the detailed account is not Eusebius's own
but a **contemporary letter from the churches of Lyon and Vienne**, quoted by him at _EH_ 5.1, and
that letter reports an imperial rescript — 5.1.47, "For Cæsar commanded that they should be put to
death, but that any who might deny should be set free." Tertullian wrote c. 197, and the rescript he
relies on is itself disputed as spurious. _Effect:_ the article promises "It does not resolve, and it
should not be smoothed over," then omits the most damaging item and dates the surviving account a
century later than the document actually is. I flag this from the subject's side deliberately: a
sympathetic page that is caught soft-pedaling the worst charge makes every generous reading in it
suspect. The article's credibility is the subject's only protection. _Repair:_ name the contemporary
letter and the reported rescript, keep the existing counterweights (no other contemporary source,
Irenaeus silent, Marcus weeks away, the letter names no emperor personally), and keep the refusal to
settle. _Confidence:_ high. _Test:_ a reader who opens Eusebius 5.1 finds nothing more damaging than
the article prepared them for.

**SUBJECT-C9 — House courtroom vocabulary replaces his own, and the school-practice objection is never
faced.**
The FORMULA FINGERPRINT LEDGER claims "the page runs on Marcus's own words (penance, falling short,
marking, the debt list)." "Penance" and "falling short" are his in translation. "Marking," "audit,"
"verdict," "grades his own conduct," "the case against you," "continues prosecuting the case," "The
Emperor Who Graded Himself" are the article's imported metaphor system. _Effect:_ structured
self-examination arrives as symptom rather than as method, and the strongest objection to the whole
typing never appears on the page: written self-examination is a prescribed Stoic exercise, so
performing it is not by itself diagnostic of anything except being a Stoic. The packet names this as
"the single strongest structural objection available to a skeptic," applying to every item in the
evidence-for list. _Repair:_ one paragraph in the diagnosis section that raises it and answers it —
the practice was assigned, but the intensity was not; the penance cascade at twenty-five predates his
mature Stoic training; and the same exercise did not make Epictetus or Seneca write like this.
_Expected benefit:_ this is the highest-value addition available. It converts the page's weakest flank
into its most convincing paragraph, and it lets Marcus's own account of what he was doing appear on
the page at least once. _Confidence:_ high. _Test:_ a reader who knows what _hypomnemata_ are does not
close the page thinking the argument ignored the obvious objection.

## Follow-on questions

**SUBJECT-Q1 — Does any _Meditations_ passage support "the verdict never revised" once the consoling
clauses are restored?**
_What would change:_ if a scan of the full Long text turns up self-condemnation that does **not**
resolve into permission, the article's closing survives with a different citation and R1 becomes a
citation swap rather than a reframe. If every instance resolves, the thesis itself needs the softer
form — he kept writing down both halves — which is more defensible and more interesting.
_Best source:_ the Standard Ebooks Long text, read straight through for the accusation-then-release
pattern. This is the highest-value unfinished research on the page.

**SUBJECT-Q2 — Is the 143 Senate letter still a clean Type-1-over-Type-6 discriminator once the
"abuse" framing is restored?**
_What would change:_ if Marcus is partly performing detection of a rhetorical device rather than
simply failing to absorb comfort, the reassurance test weakens and the Rabbit Hole's Type 6 dismissal
needs a second discriminator. The packet flags that seeing through offered comfort is itself
compatible with Six skepticism.
_Best source:_ Haines Loeb vol. 1 p. 109, read in full alongside Fronto's preceding letter.

**SUBJECT-Q3 — Did Marcus ever teach or discourse on philosophy publicly?**
_What would change:_ the IEP entry finds no record of public teaching, which supports "taught nobody."
But the Athens chairs are established, and HA 22.6 says he answered critics "in speeches or in
pamphlets." If any of that amounts to public philosophical instruction, the no-audience premise needs
more than the wording fix at SUBJECT-C2.
_Best source:_ Cassius Dio bk. 72 on the Athens visit of 176 and on his conduct before departing for
the Marcomannic war; J. H. Oliver's "Marcus Aurelius and the Philosophical Schools at Athens" (JSTOR 294312) for the endowment's scope.

**SUBJECT-Q4 — Does the "starve myself" phrase carry the literal weight the subtype paragraph puts on
it?**
_What would change:_ if _fame necor_ is idiomatic in second-century epistolary Latin, the
self-preservation call loses its distinctive evidence and rests on Dio alone, which is thinner but
still viable.
_Best source:_ the Latin of Ad M. Caesarem 4.13 against Haines's rendering; the packet's own
limitation note records that no original-language check was performed anywhere in this pipeline.
