## Bottom-line verdict

I came in knowing roughly this much: Marcus Aurelius was a Roman emperor, he wrote _Meditations_, people
quote him about staying calm. I left able to state the thesis without help — the calm was a practice he
kept having to reapply, not a temperament he was issued — and able to say the type answer and why
(Enneagram Type 1: he graded his own conduct against a standard he could name and never reach, and kept
marking the paper in an empty room for thirty years). That is the whole test for my perspective, and the
article passes it.

It passes on the strength of a very good decision: it explains the Enneagram claim through what Marcus
actually did rather than through system vocabulary. I was never asked to learn a taxonomy in order to
follow the argument.

What holds it back is not the argument, it is the on-ramp and the shelving. The opening biography
section asks a newcomer to absorb 19 named people, places, wars and plagues in 397 words, and inside
that section one sentence quietly hands me the wrong answer to the most important question in the
piece — who turned him into a Stoic. Meanwhile the article's most trust-building material, its
counterarguments and its "what would change our minds" test, sits inside an accordion that tells me to
skip it. So the reader most likely to be persuaded is told to skip the part that would persuade them.

None of this rose to a blocker. Every one of the eight concerns has a small, bounded repair, and four of
them are one-sentence fixes. `revise`, not `hold_for_research` — nothing here needs new research, because
the evidence packet already contains every answer required.

## What landed

**UNFAM-H1 — The cold open.** "In 162 AD the emperor of Rome took four days off at Alsium, a seaside town
up the coast from the capital. When he got back, he wrote to his old tutor and refused to say how he had
spent them." Followed by Fronto's reply: "It were easier to reconcile you to a polecat than to pleasure…
have you repaired to Alsium only to fast with the sea in sight?" I knew nothing and needed nothing. A
man, a holiday, a teacher telling him off, and a joke that still works after eighteen centuries. **Must
survive:** this is the only passage on the page that costs a newcomer zero prior knowledge, and it sets
the entire thesis without naming a single concept.

**UNFAM-H2 — "His own teacher could not talk him into a beach."** One line, sitting alone. It converted
the anecdote into a claim about a person and made me want the rest. **Must survive:** it is the hinge
between the story and the argument, and nothing else on the page does that job.

**UNFAM-H3 — The plain-language definition of the type.** "What marks a [Type 1] is the machinery
underneath: the standard measures whether you are good at the root, and the marking continues in an empty
room. With Marcus the room was always empty." This is the moment I stopped worrying that I was going to
be taught a system. It defines the type operationally, in eleven words, and then immediately spends it on
the subject. **Must survive:** it is the single passage that makes the Enneagram claim accessible without
jargon, which is the whole test for my perspective.

**UNFAM-H4 — The debt-ledger explanation, and the refusal to let it exonerate him.** "A man who believes
every good thing in him is borrowed experiences rest as spending money he did not earn, while the lenders
are dead and cannot be repaid and the plague is killing five million of the people he was handed."
Then, crucially: "That still made him hard to be around, and Fronto was right to push." The first
sentence explained a behaviour I had found merely odd; the second stopped the explanation from becoming
an excuse. **Must survive:** together they are the most sophisticated thing on the page, and the second
sentence is what keeps the first honest. Do not cut the concession to save words.

**UNFAM-H5 — "He held a yard sale instead."** An anachronism, deliberately, and it works: I instantly
understood both the scale mismatch and the humility without knowing anything about Roman public finance.
The specific inventory that follows (crystal goblets, his wife's gold-worked silk robes, jewels from
Hadrian's locked cabinet) and the offer to refund buyers made it real. **Must survive:** best
self-contained narrative unit on the page for a reader with no background.

**UNFAM-H6 — "Nobody writes a numbered protocol for a problem they do not have."** Gave me the reasoning
rule, not just the conclusion, so I could check the next piece of evidence myself. **Must survive:** it
teaches the reader how the article reasons.

**UNFAM-H7 — "The most reproduced philosophy book in the world has a war for a return address."** I did
not know what the Quadi or Carnuntum were and did not need to; the sentence does the translating.
**Must survive:** it is the model for how the rest of the page should handle unfamiliar proper nouns.

**UNFAM-H8 — The sickroom detail.** "On the seventh day of his illness he admitted only Commodus, then
sent him out of the room at once, in fear that the boy would catch what was killing him." Nothing was
explained to me and nothing needed to be. **Must survive:** the only place the page let me feel something
about him rather than conclude something about him.

**UNFAM-H9 — The heading scan path.** Reading only the nine H2s gives a coherent story: who he was, two
archives, the type question, the holiday, the anger, the war funding, the buried children, Commodus, the
book. I tested this before reading the body. **Must survive:** a newcomer skimming still leaves with the
shape of the argument.

## What missed

**The Stoicism origin sentence lands backwards.** "Marcus found the Stoics, through the man he thanks for
it in his notebook: 'I am indebted to him for being acquainted with the discourses of Epictetus…'" The
man is never named. On first read I attached "the man" to Fronto — he is named twice in the two preceding
sentences and there is no other candidate on the page — which is the reverse of the fact, and it
contradicts the sentence directly before it ("the training took a turn Fronto never fully forgave"). I
registered that contradiction as a small wobble and read on without being able to resolve it. See
UNFAM-C1.

**The signature section's own arithmetic will not sit still.** "Two Archives, One Emperor: Marcus Aurelius
at 22 and at 55" is the article's centrepiece structure, and I could not pin down how long the span is. See
UNFAM-C2.

**One sentence closes a door in my face.** "The register runs hotter than that in places, and what it
meant is contested scholarly ground." I do not know what "hotter" means here, what is contested, or who
contests it. The article clearly knows something and has decided not to tell me. See UNFAM-C3.

**I was told to skip the honesty.** The counterarguments, the alternative types, the strongest objection
to the thesis, and the falsifiability test all live inside an accordion whose first line reads "Skip if
you're not deep into the system." See UNFAM-C4.

**Two men named Cassius.** See UNFAM-C5.

**The same quotation kept arriving.** See UNFAM-C6.

## What I expected

- **Who he is and why he matters, early.** Delivered, and well: "the one whose face now sells composure on
  a million book covers" plus "the most durable self-help text in the Western canon" gave me the stakes by
  paragraph four.
- **Why this article exists now.** Partly delivered, but late. The argument-with-the-quote-card — the
  Stoicism industry, the airport merchandise, the PolitiFact-debunked fake quote — is the reason a general
  reader should care, and it arrives in the final section. I would have oriented faster if the fake-quote
  fact appeared near the top. Recorded as a preference below, not a concern, because the ending works.
- **A one-clause gloss of what Stoicism actually holds.** Not delivered anywhere on the page. "The Stoics,"
  "the Stoic philosopher Ariston," and "far from philosophy" all assume I know what the school teaches. I
  do not need a primer; I need about eight words. The repair for UNFAM-C1 touches the exact sentence where
  this belongs, so the two should be fixed together.
- **What Commodus actually did.** "A reign ancient writers treated as a disaster" plus a _Gladiator_
  reference is thinner than the section's weight requires. I am asked to accept that this succession was
  the great failure of his life, on the strength of one adjective. One concrete detail would settle it.
- **A gloss for "1w2" and "self-preservation instinct."** Both appear in the TL;DR near the top, undefined,
  and are explained only inside the skip-labelled accordion. See UNFAM-C8.
- **Not expected, and correctly absent:** encyclopaedic biography. The article's selectivity is a feature.
  I never wanted a full reign chronology.

## What surprised me

**Welcome:**

- That _Meditations_ had no audience at all — "The book had no audience," written in a second language, in
  a camp, for one reader — and then nearly did not survive. The transmission trail (a doubtful mention in
  364, then silence until a Byzantine bishop turns out to own a crumbling copy around 900) reframed
  everything I thought I knew about the book. "Everything you can buy at an airport with his face on it
  descends from that accident" is the most memorable sentence on the page.
- The auction. I did not know an emperor could be short of money, let alone solve it by selling his wife's
  clothes in a forum for two months and then offering refunds.
- The arithmetic of the children. "Do the subtraction. He buried at least seven of his own children" —
  and then Book 1 thanking the gods "that my children have not been stupid nor deformed in body," with the
  line "That is a man counting what was left." I had no idea, and it changed the person for me.
- That Fronto was funny. I expected antiquity to be solemn.

**Jarring:**

- The "register runs hotter" sentence (UNFAM-C3). It is the only place where the article's tone shifts from
  telling me things to withholding them, and the shift is audible.
- The citation apparatus inside the cold open. Two references to the Loeb Classical Library edition
  land in the first 120 words, before I have any reason to care which edition anyone used.

## Red flags

**None.** No blocker in this review.

Stating the near-miss explicitly, because the reason it is not a blocker is itself the reason it matters:
**UNFAM-C1 reliably installs a false belief in exactly the reader who cannot detect it.** A reader who
already knows Marcus reached Stoicism through Junius Rusticus will read "the man" correctly and never
notice. A reader with no prior knowledge — my standpoint — attaches it to Fronto and walks away with the
reverse of the fact. My trust is not broken by it, because nothing in my experience of reading tells me I
have been misled; that is precisely what makes it worth fixing before publication rather than after. The
subject, fan and critic perspectives may reasonably escalate it. From here it is a top-priority concern,
not a trust break.

## Specific improvements

### UNFAM-C1 — Name Rusticus, gloss Epictetus, gloss the Stoics (highest priority)

- **Location:** "Who Marcus Aurelius Was, and How Rome Picked Him Twice," the sentence beginning "Marcus
  found the Stoics, through the man he thanks for it in his notebook."
- **Reader effect:** The referent is unnamed, and the two preceding sentences name Fronto twice. A newcomer
  attaches the credit to Fronto, which is the opposite of the fact, and which contradicts the immediately
  preceding clause about Fronto never forgiving the turn. Two further proper nouns in the quoted sentence
  ("the Stoics," "Epictetus") are never explained, at the exact hinge where the article needs me to
  understand what changed in him.
- **Evidence:** _Meditations_ 1.7 is the entry headed "From Rusticus," and the passage the draft quotes is
  Marcus thanking **Junius Rusticus** for lending him the Discourses. Confirmed twice, independently: the
  evidence packet's re-check of the George Long text (CLM-02, "1.7 is the entry headed _From Rusticus_"),
  and my own orientation search, where general-audience Stoicism publishers maintain dedicated explainers
  for both [Junius Rusticus](https://dailystoic.com/junius-rusticus/) and
  [Epictetus](https://dailystoic.com/epictetus/) — which establishes that naming and glossing these two is
  standard practice for exactly this audience, so the omission is a draft choice, not my ignorance.
  Fronto has his own separate Book 1 entry (1.11) and it is not this one.
- **Minimum viable repair:** Name Rusticus in the lead-in clause, and add a short appositive for each of
  the two unfamiliar names — who Epictetus was (a former slave whose lectures became the Stoic text Marcus
  carried) and what the Stoics held. Roughly 20–25 added words; the article is at its word ceiling, so pay
  for it from UNFAM-C6.
- **Expected benefit:** Removes a reliably-wrong inference at the causal hinge of the biography, and
  simultaneously closes the page's only unglossed philosophical-school reference.
- **Confidence:** High on the defect and the fact; high on the repair.
- **Acceptance test:** A reader with no prior knowledge, asked after reading "who introduced Marcus to
  Stoicism?", answers "Rusticus" and not "Fronto"; and asked "what was Stoicism?" can give a one-sentence
  answer from the page alone.

### UNFAM-C2 — Make the two-archive span one number

- **Location:** H2 "Two Archives, One Emperor: Marcus Aurelius at 22 and at 55"; the section's opening
  ("thirty years apart"); its closing ("Thirty-three years, two languages, two readers, one report"); the
  final timeline entry ("age ~54"); the article's last line ("He was fifty-four").
- **Reader effect:** The article's signature structure is defined by a span, and the span is stated four
  ways on one page. The heading advertises an age (55) that the article's own timeline and closing line
  both contradict (54). I tried to hold the number, failed, and stopped trusting the precision of the
  smaller figures around it.
- **Evidence:** My own read of the draft, then confirmed against the timeline the draft itself prints:
  143 (age 22) to c. 175 (age ~54) is 32 years, ages 22→54. The evidence packet reaches the same
  conclusion independently (CLM-14, "three different spans appear on one page") and notes that
  _Meditations_ is not internally datable beyond the c. 170–180 convention, so no exact span is recoverable
  anyway.
- **Minimum viable repair:** Pick one span and use it in all four places. Since the composition date is a
  convention rather than a fact, prefer the approximate form — change the heading to "at 22 and at 54,"
  and make both prose statements "more than thirty years apart." No new research required.
- **Expected benefit:** The centrepiece structure stops wobbling, and the page's many other precise
  figures inherit the credibility.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Acceptance test:** Grep the reader-visible body for the span; every statement of it agrees with the
  timeline's own endpoints, and the H2 age matches the closing line's age.

### UNFAM-C3 — Say what is contested, without settling it

- **Location:** "Two Archives, One Emperor," the sentence "The register runs hotter than that in places,
  and what it meant is contested scholarly ground."
- **Reader effect:** This is the one sentence on the page that withholds rather than explains. I cannot
  tell what is hotter, what the dispute is about, or why I am being told a dispute exists at all if I am
  not to be told its subject. It reads as insinuation, which is a worse outcome than plain statement —
  and it is conspicuous precisely because the article elsewhere names its disputes openly and well
  (Lyon, the death location, Commodus).
- **Evidence:** My unaided first read flagged it as the only passage I could not decode. The evidence
  packet confirms the dispute is real and, importantly, that a **binding handling rule** applies: "no
  romantic or sexual claim is made here in either direction" (packet, Evidence against §3 and Disputes §6).
  So the vagueness is compliance, not carelessness — but compliance can be achieved without opacity.
- **Minimum viable repair:** Name the _subject_ of the dispute in one clause while adjudicating nothing —
  that the early letters use intensely affectionate physical language, and that scholars disagree about how
  to read such language between a Roman student and his tutor. Then keep the existing pivot ("What matters
  here is the temperament the discipline was applied to") verbatim; it is a good exit.
- **Expected benefit:** Converts a closed door into a named, unresolved question, which is the register the
  rest of the article already uses successfully. Fully satisfies the no-claim handling rule.
- **Confidence:** High on the reader effect; high that the repair is compatible with the handling rule.
- **Acceptance test:** A reader asked "what were scholars arguing about?" can answer in one sentence, and
  cannot state which side the article takes.

### UNFAM-C4 — Do not shelve the counterarguments behind "skip this"

- **Location:** `<details class="enneagram-rabbit-hole">`, whose first line is "For the Enneagram nerds.
  Skip if you're not deep into the system. The rest of the analysis stands on its own." Inside it:
  "Counterarguments: Why Marcus Aurelius Might Not Be Type 1," the Type 5 case, the strongest objection
  ("Ones are supposed to correct other people, and Marcus mostly declined to"), and "What would change our
  minds."
- **Reader effect:** I followed the instruction and skipped it. In doing so I skipped the material that
  would most have increased my confidence in the article — not system trivia, but the page admitting what
  it cannot explain and naming the evidence that would overturn it. The main body pressure-tests exactly
  one alternative (Type 6) in a single paragraph, so a reader who obeys the label sees the thesis defended
  once and never seriously attacked.
- **Evidence:** Structural, verifiable in the file: the `### Counterarguments` and "What would change our
  minds" blocks are both inside the `<details>` element that carries the skip instruction.
- **Minimum viable repair:** Cheapest version — move the two sentences beginning "The strongest objection
  is not a rival type at all" through "pointed the standard almost entirely at himself," plus the "What
  would change our minds" line, out of the accordion and into the end of the diagnosis section. Leave
  wings, subtypes and arrows behind the accordion, where the "for the nerds" framing is accurate. If the
  word ceiling forbids the move, at minimum amend the accordion's summary line so it no longer instructs
  a general reader to skip the counterarguments.
- **Expected benefit:** The general reader encounters the article's intellectual honesty, which is
  currently its most under-delivered asset.
- **Confidence:** High on the effect; medium on the ideal placement given the word ceiling.
- **Acceptance test:** A reader who never opens the accordion can still state one serious objection to the
  Type 1 call and one piece of evidence that would overturn it.

### UNFAM-C5 — Disambiguate the two men named Cassius

- **Location:** "Cassius Dio, writing close enough to the events to know people who were there" (bio
  section); "the general Avidius Cassius" (holiday section); "he 'did not rejoice at Cassius' death'"
  (Rabbit Hole, wing paragraph); "Dio, who is otherwise an admirer" (Commodus section).
- **Reader effect:** The historian who admires him and the general who revolted against him share a name,
  are introduced roughly 1,500 words apart, and are thereafter referred to by the shared element — "Dio"
  for one, "Cassius" for the other. On my read the bare "Cassius' death" briefly parsed as the death of the
  historian whose verdict on the reign I had just been given.
- **Evidence:** Direct from the draft; four reader-visible mentions across four sections, two of them
  shortened to the colliding element.
- **Minimum viable repair:** At the second and later mentions of the general, use "Avidius Cassius" in
  full — including inside the Rabbit Hole quote lead-in — and add a three-word identifier at the
  holiday-section introduction ("no relation to the historian"), or reorder so the two are never bare.
- **Expected benefit:** Removes the only person-level confusion in a page that otherwise introduces its
  cast carefully.
- **Confidence:** Medium-high.
- **Acceptance test:** A reader asked "who called him a philosophical old woman?" does not answer "the
  historian."

### UNFAM-C6 — Give the Ariston quotation one home

- **Location:** Reader-visible appearances of the "penance… starve myself" passage: the epigraph; TL;DR
  bullet 2 ("made him blush and do penance"); the diagnosis section twice — "Failure draws penance rather
  than excuse" and the full re-listing "penance, self-anger, sadness, comparison to others,
  self-starvation"; and the Rabbit Hole subtype paragraph ("ends its penances with 'starve myself'"),
  with the stress-arrow paragraph then quoting a third fragment of the same sentence. Plus FAQ 1.
- **Reader effect:** By the third encounter I recognised it as the same sentence and began to suspect the
  evidence base was narrower than a 4,500-word article implies. The re-listing inside the diagnosis
  section is the costly one: it paraphrases in full a five-item list the reader met verbatim as the
  epigraph.
- **Evidence:** Counted mechanically against the reader-visible text (HTML comments and frontmatter
  stripped): five in-body appearances plus the FAQ. FAQ and TL;DR duplication is structural and fine; the
  in-body count is not. For contrast, I checked the Book 8 opener and found it quoted verbatim only once,
  with the TL;DR and closing both paraphrasing — that one is handled correctly and needs no change.
- **Minimum viable repair:** Keep the epigraph and the diagnosis-section analysis as the two homes. In the
  diagnosis section, cut the full re-listing and point back ("the list in the epigraph") — the surrounding
  sentence-by-sentence walkthrough already does the work. In the Rabbit Hole, keep "starve myself" for the
  subtype and cut the stress-arrow re-quote to a reference. Frees roughly 30–40 words, which pays for
  UNFAM-C1.
- **Expected benefit:** Restores the impression of a broad evidence base, and funds the highest-priority
  repair within the word ceiling.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Acceptance test:** The "penance / starve" sentence appears at most twice in reader-visible body prose
  outside the TL;DR and FAQ.

### UNFAM-C7 — Thin the on-ramp

- **Location:** "Who Marcus Aurelius Was, and How Rome Picked Him Twice," 397 words.
- **Reader effect:** I counted 19 named entities a newcomer must absorb in that section — Rome, Hadrian,
  Antoninus Pius, Marcus Cornelius Fronto, Lucius, Epictetus, the Stoics, Lucius Verus, Parthia, Armenia,
  the Antonine Plague, the Germanic tribes, the Danube, Cassius Dio, Vindobona, Sirmium, Vienna, Syria,
  Latin. That is a name every 21 words, in the first section after a cold open that had required none. It
  is the highest abandonment risk on the page, and it sits immediately after the article's best writing.
- **Evidence:** Mechanical count over the reader-visible section text.
- **Minimum viable repair:** Do not cut the reign — cut the geography. Vindobona/Sirmium/Vienna are three
  proper nouns spent on a dispute the article correctly refuses to settle and never uses again; the same
  fact survives as "ancient sources disagree about where." Parthia and Armenia can compress to "war in the
  east." That is four or five names removed without losing a fact, and it buys back words for UNFAM-C1.
- **Expected benefit:** Lowers the cost of the section every reader must cross to reach the argument.
- **Confidence:** Medium-high on the effect; high that the two named cuts are safe.
- **Acceptance test:** The section carries no proper noun that is not either reused later or load-bearing
  for a claim made later.

### UNFAM-C8 — Gloss "1w2" and "self-preservation" at first use, or defer them

- **Location:** TL;DR bullet 1, "Enneagram Type 1, most likely a 1w2 with a self-preservation instinct";
  same construction in FAQ 1. Explanation exists only inside the skip-labelled accordion.
- **Reader effect:** The TL;DR is positioned as the newcomer's shortcut and is the second thing on the
  page. It opens with two undefined pieces of notation, and the only place they are defined is the section
  I have been told to skip. My first impression of the Enneagram claim is therefore "system I do not
  speak," which the body then spends 4,000 words disproving.
- **Evidence:** Direct from the draft; "1w2" has no reader-visible gloss outside `<details>`.
- **Minimum viable repair:** Either add three or four words of apposition in the TL;DR ("a 1w2 — a Type 1
  with a helper's warmth next to it"), or drop the notation from the TL;DR entirely and let the bullet say
  "Enneagram Type 1," leaving wing and instinct to the Rabbit Hole where they are explained. The second
  option costs nothing and may be preferable at the word ceiling.
- **Expected benefit:** The newcomer's first contact with the type claim is legible.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Acceptance test:** Every piece of Enneagram notation in reader-visible text outside the accordion is
  either glossed on the spot or absent.

## Follow-on questions

**UNFAM-Q1 — Who is "the man" in _Meditations_ 1.7?**
_Resolved during this review._ Junius Rusticus. _What it changed:_ it converted my strongest first-read
comprehension wobble from "possibly my own ignorance" into a confirmed draft omission, and it supplied the
exact repair in UNFAM-C1. _Best source:_ the George Long text of Med. 1.7, which prints the section
heading; corroborated by [Donald Robertson on Marcus and
Rusticus](https://donaldrobertson.name/2014/04/26/marcus-aurelius-and-junius-rusticus/).

**UNFAM-Q2 — What precisely is the scholarly disagreement about the Fronto letters' register?**
_What an answer would change:_ whether UNFAM-C3's repair can name the dispute in a single clause without
implying a side. If the dispute turns out to be narrower or broader than I assume, the wording of the
one-clause fix changes. _Best source:_ the packet's own handling rule (Disputes §6) plus the scholarly
literature on the Fronto correspondence's affectionate passages; the draft's editor should confirm the
final wording against the entity-gap handling rules before publishing, since those rules are binding.

**UNFAM-Q3 — Is "four of them before he was ever emperor" correct?**
_What an answer would change:_ whether the most emotionally weighted passage on the page contains a
checkable slip. I flagged it on first read only as a suspiciously precise number inside an otherwise
carefully hedged passage ("at least seven," "thirteen or fourteen"); the packet independently reports
(CLM-13) that the conventional child list supports five or possibly six pre-161 deaths, not four, and that
no source it located supports exactly four. If that holds, the fix is to hedge the number or drop it.
_Best source:_ the conventional list of Marcus and Faustina's children with death dates; Robertson, _Marcus
Aurelius: The Stoic Emperor_ (Yale UP, 2024). Outside my lane to adjudicate — routing it to whoever owns
fact-checking.

**UNFAM-Q4 — Where do the FAQs render relative to the body?**
_What an answer would change:_ whether "The Historia Augusta reports…" in FAQ 4 reaches the reader before
the body's careful introduction of that source as "a late and famously unreliable set of imperial
biographies." If FAQs render above the body on this template, the source caveat needs to travel into the
FAQ answers. _Best source:_ the people-blog page template and its FAQ block placement.

**UNFAM-Q5 — What one concrete thing did Commodus do?**
_What an answer would change:_ whether the Commodus section's weight is earned for a reader who knows him
only from _Gladiator_. One specific detail would let me judge the succession decision instead of taking
"disaster" on authority. _Best source:_ Cassius Dio book 73 on Commodus's reign, already in the citation
set the packet works from.

## Limits of this review

  contains extensive HTML-comment blocks — a testimony ledger, heading/distribution/formula ledgers,
  reviewer notes, an embedded **FRESH EYES REVIEW**, and **SECOND PASS NOTES**. These are invisible in the
  rendered page but unavoidable when reading the file. Two consequences worth stating plainly. First, my
  "unaided" read was unaided as to research but not as to pipeline apparatus, which is a real deviation
  from an authentic first encounter. Second, and more importantly, the embedded fresh-eyes review contains
  a prior evaluator's findings. I did not let it set my agenda: every finding above came from my own read
  and was verified with my own counts **before** I opened the packet, and where the embedded review
  complained about something I found already fixed — the Book 8 quotation repetition — I checked and
  declined to carry the complaint forward. Where my findings converge with that block, treat it as
  independent convergence, not inheritance.
- **I am simulating a standpoint, not occupying one.** I have prior knowledge of Marcus Aurelius that I
  cannot fully suppress, which risks both over- and under-estimating what a newcomer needs. My mitigation
  was to convert judgement calls into measurements wherever possible (entity counts, quotation counts, span
  statements) rather than trusting my sense of what felt hard.
- **One search only**, per the protocol's unfamiliar-reader rule. Several questions in the follow-on
  section are consequently left open rather than resolved.
- **I cannot assess the rendered page.** Whether the `<details>` accordions default open or closed, where

  Type 1 conclusion itself, and the fairness of the portrait. Where I noticed something factual — the
