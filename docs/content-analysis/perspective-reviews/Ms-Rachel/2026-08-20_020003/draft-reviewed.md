> "I think it might be a little abnormal, the amount of empathy I have."
> — Rachel Accurso, The Washington Post, 2025

<p class="firstLetter">Ms. Rachel asks a toddler if she can say "mama," and then she stops.</p>

One second. Two. Three. She holds the camera's eye with her mouth slightly open and her hands still, doing nothing at all, while somewhere past the lens a child's brain assembles a word.

That silence is the product. Speech-language pathologists call it expectant pausing, and it is the reason 20 million subscribers hand their toddlers a woman in a bow headband instead of a cartoon. The songs are catchy. The puppets are fine. The pause is the technology.

Rachel Griffin Accurso learned it the hard way. Her son Thomas said his first word at two years and eight months old. It was "mama." She counts it in months, the way parents count when the waiting has gone on long enough to need a number.

She is 43, born in Biddeford, Maine, raised outside Sanford, trained as a music teacher at NYU and as an early childhood educator after that. In 2019 she pointed a camera at her living room. By 2026 her channel had 16.2 billion views, her show was one of the most-watched series on Netflix, and she had become one of the most argued-about people on the internet, which is a strange outcome for someone whose entire job is holding still.

Here is the part that does not fit. In May 2024 she offered 500 personalized videos at $100 each for a children's emergency fund. They sold out in about an hour. The people who love her told her to keep them short. She made them long, because she could not stand the idea of a child opening a short one.

She waits four seconds for every child on earth. She has never once given herself the four seconds back.

## Why Ms. Rachel Waits Four Seconds

Here is the entire method, timed.

**0.0 seconds.** She asks the question. Her pitch climbs about an octave above her speaking voice, her vowels stretch, her eyebrows go up. This is parentese, and it is not an affectation. Exaggerated pitch makes the individual sounds of a word easier for an immature auditory system to pull apart.

**0.5 seconds.** She stops. Most adults cannot do this. Conversational turn-taking in English runs on gaps of roughly a fifth of a second, and every instinct a fluent speaker has says fill it.

**1.5 seconds.** A neurotypical toddler is still working. A child with a language delay has barely begun. Comprehension, retrieval, and motor planning each take their own time, and none of them are fast at two years old.

**3.0 seconds.** Her face does not drift. She does not look away, glance at a mark, or soften into a smile that says never mind. The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association tells parents to pause after speaking, and the reason so few of them manage it is that three seconds of an adult's undivided attention, aimed at a child who is not producing anything, is genuinely uncomfortable to hold.

**4.0 seconds.** She responds as though the child answered. Whether or not the child answered.

Watch it once with the sound off and the sequence becomes obvious. Every light in the frame is on the kid. During the pause, the most-watched children's educator alive is doing nothing, wanting nothing, and taking up no space at all. She has turned the most characteristic posture of her personality into a production technique and sold it to Netflix.

## What is Ms. Rachel's personality type?

### Ms. Rachel is an Enneagram Type 2

The evidence is not the gentleness. Gentleness is her job.

The evidence is what she does when someone tells her the gentleness is fake.

In May 2025, the advocacy group StopAntisemitism asked Attorney General Pam Bondi to investigate whether Accurso was being funded by Hamas. It was a financial accusation with a paper trail attached, the kind of thing a lawyer answers with bank statements. Accurso called it absurd and patently false, and then, in interview after interview, she answered it with something else entirely.

"It's really painful," she told WBUR's _Here & Now_ in June 2025. "And I have to remind myself that people don't know my heart, and people try to tell you who you are, but you know who you are."

On Democracy Now! in August 2025, asked about the same accusation: "That's really painful, because obviously it's not true." She kept going: "It is difficult to receive criticism, but I know who I am."

Asked about money, she talks about her heart. Asked about a funding trail, she says she knows who she is. That reflex is the whole diagnosis. [Type 2s](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-2) locate their identity inside the realness of their care, which means an accusation of insincerity does not land as a factual dispute. It lands as an attack on the self, and it gets answered like one.

She said the quiet part out loud in a trade profile about studio equipment. Talking to The Hollywood Reporter in November 2025 about lighting and edit software, she volunteered: "I can't fake being genuine. I just, I love children so much."

Nobody had asked whether she was faking.

The other tell is the amount. "I think it might be a little abnormal, the amount of empathy I have," she told The Washington Post in 2025. She has noticed that something about her is unusual, and she has measured it as a quantity, as though the difference between her and everyone else is volume. Type 2s tend to describe the pattern in exactly that register. A surplus. Something extra they happen to carry, when it is the thing holding the whole structure up.

The wing, the subtype, and the strongest case against Type 2 are in the rabbit hole further down.

## Ms. Rachel's Maine Childhood and the Trolley She Tried to Stop

The surplus has a start date.

She grew up in Springvale, a village inside Sanford, in southern Maine, on a street with a lot of other kids on it. Local Maine reporting has her raised by a single mother. Past that the record thins out, and the sites claiming to know more about the Griffins contradict each other.

What she has said herself is better than any of it.

On Democracy Now! in August 2025, describing [Mister Rogers](/personality-analysis/mr-rogers): "I grew up loving Mr. Rogers, and I used to go up to the TV and try and block the trolley from leaving because I didn't want the show to end."

Sit with the mechanics of that. A small girl gets out of her chair and plants herself against the glass, because a man she has never met is about to stop paying attention to her and she has decided to physically prevent it.

She did theatre at Sanford High School, finished at Marshwood after her family moved, and went to the University of Southern Maine. Then she spent roughly twenty years of adult life on the other side of that transaction, and none of it was glamorous. A summer program for children with disabilities. Music lessons for refugee kids through a Boys and Girls Club in Maine. A public preschool classroom in New York City. Two master's degrees stacked on the bachelor's, music education at NYU and early childhood education after that, in a field where nobody gets rich.

The pattern predates the platform by two decades. She was choosing the children other systems had the least time for when there was no audience, no algorithm, and no money in it.

"Before I did this," she told the Post, "there were so many 'Oh, now I've found what I'm meant to do!' moments."

Plural. She kept finding it.

## How Rachel Accurso Built Songs for Littles Out of a Living Room

The founding story is usually told as an inspiring one, and the inspiring version leaves out the year of nothing.

Thomas was born in March 2018. When the speech delay was diagnosed, she went looking for material and found almost none of it. "A lot of things I teach are things I wish I had known for my son," she told HuffPost UK in early 2023. So she began doing the exercises herself, on camera, in the living room, editing in iMovie, and posting them.

She was talking to a child who was not talking back. Not for a week. For months, and then for more months, and then the word came.

"I had waited for that for so long," she said.

The channel launched in 2019 into essentially no audience, and then in 2020 every parent in the developed world was locked in a house with a toddler, and the thing she had built for one boy turned out to be built for all of them. By 2026: two videos past a billion views and a Netflix season that was the biggest kids launch in the platform's history.

Her husband, Aron Accurso, spent seventeen years on Broadway and was associate music director on _Aladdin_ before he left to put his hand inside an orange-haired puppet named Herbie. Herbie is the show's stand-in for the child watching. He is the one who answers Ms. Rachel, so that a toddler at home has somebody to answer alongside. A Broadway conductor gave up _Aladdin_ to play the kid who talks back, because for thirty-two months in that living room, nobody had.

Both Accursos came up through the BMI musical theatre workshop.

"I can be such a perfectionist," Aron told The Washington Post. "But we never would have gotten this off the ground if she hadn't been like, at some point, 'Who cares if it's not perfect? It's for the kids.'"

Everyone assumes the woman doing a forty-minute take of a nursery rhyme is the control freak in the marriage. Her husband, on the record, says she is the one who ships.

The other thing that arrived with the audience was a company. She signed with CAA in 2023, licensed the show to Netflix, put out a toy line and a Ms. Rachel doll, took a Penguin Random House imprint, opened an Amazon storefront. The woman who could not bring herself to record a short Cameo now runs an operation with agents, lawyers, and a merchandising calendar. Somebody in that building is very good at saying no. Nothing on the record establishes that it is her.

Then came the Cameo marathon of May 2024: 500 personalized videos at $100 apiece, the whole take going to a children's emergency fund covering Gaza, Sudan, Ukraine, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo.

"I wanted to make each video really long and special," she told the Post.

<p class="inner-thought">Three hundred down. The voice is going. This kid's parents paid the same as everyone else. Do the whole song.</p>

She lost her voice. The instrument the entire enterprise runs on, spent in a stretch, for $50,000, because a version where some children got less was not a version she could sit inside.

## Why Parents Find Ms. Rachel Unbearable

Millions of parents hear that instrument every morning.

There is an entire genre of adult complaint about this woman, and it is not really about politics.

It is about the voice. The stretched vowels, the enormous eyebrows, the relentless brightness. Parents joke about it in group chats the way people joke about a song stuck in their head, which is to say with real hostility. In June 2025 Olivia Munn told Parents and People that kids' shows drive her "crazy" and named Ms. Rachel as one she does not allow.

Here is what that voice actually is. It is a register a mother held, for thirty-two months, for one specific boy in one specific living room, while nothing came back. It sounds performed to an adult because adults have never had to sustain warmth into a silence that long. She can hold it for forty minutes on camera because she once held it for two and a half years off camera, and the alternative was letting the room go quiet on a child who could not fill it himself.

The thing that reads as saccharine performance is a person who has organized her nervous system around never being the one who stops first.

None of which makes the criticism wrong.

The most serious objection to Ms. Rachel comes from Jerrica Sannes, a child development specialist with a master's in early childhood curriculum and instruction who founded Raise Wildflowers. Sannes's argument is that Accurso deploys genuinely research-backed techniques to hold the attention of infants, and that the research on screens for that age group has never pointed anywhere except away from them. "I am not fear mongering," Sannes has said. "You should be scared."

She has a point that the type lens does not dissolve. A woman whose stated decision rule is "Is this helpful for kids?" has built a business that requires very young children to look at a screen, and the fact that her intentions are unimpeachable is exactly why the question is hard. Dr. Sanam Hafeez, the neuropsychologist who reviewed the content for PureWow in 2025, endorsed the method and then added the caveat anyway: "Keeping screen time balanced with other activities is important."

The most patient teacher on the internet is still a teacher who arrives through a rectangle.

## Ms. Rachel's Controversies: Jules, Pride, and Gaza

The fights that actually blew up were not about her method.

The first one had nothing to do with a war.

In February 2023 a TikTok creator who describes herself online as a traditional mother posted a video objecting to Jules Hoffman, a _Songs for Littles_ cast member who uses they/them pronouns. Within days there were thousands of comments and an organized push to make #cancelmsrachel trend. Hoffman answered it directly, telling Good Morning America that March: "I am hoping that by being me, I am able to show kids that there are different types of people in the world."

Accurso did not answer it at all. On February 27 she posted a video saying she was stepping away from TikTok, captioned: "Hurtful videos and comments, no matter how much attention they get, will not bring you want you want. Only love can do that." She did not name the campaign, name Jules, or use the word pronouns. She was back in about a week, talking about setting boundaries for herself and saying she was there "to serve children and their families every day."

Someone in her care was being attacked, and the woman whose whole identity is care went quiet and posted about love.

By the summer of 2024 she was losing two different constituencies in the same six weeks. In May 2024 she started posting about children in Gaza, and the account that had been a neutral utility in every American household stopped being neutral. On June 1 she posted a 25-second Pride video: "Happy Pride to all of our wonderful families and friends. This month and every month I celebrate you." Matt Walsh and a run of conservative accounts called for a boycott. She had seen that one coming and said so inside the video itself: "To those of you who are going to comment that they can't watch the show anymore because of this support, no worries and much love your way. God bless. I am not chasing fame or views, I'm standing strong in love."

What she has said about Gaza, in her words: "My deep care for children doesn't stop at any border." "I see all children as precious and equal." "I would risk everything, and I will risk my career over and over to stand up for them." She has also posted about Ariel and Kfir Bibas, the Israeli children taken hostage by Hamas, writing that her heart was with their family and the Jewish community. Her stated position is that no child is excluded.

Asked on WBUR in 2025 where any of it comes from, she did not give a political answer. "I do have a big platform, and I look at it as a responsibility," she said. "I also pray every day; 'How can I help children?' is just my prayer."

What has been said about her: the Hamas-funding referral of May 2025. In November 2025 she announced she was unsubscribing from The New York Times over what she called biased and dehumanizing coverage of Palestinians. In January 2026 she apologized for liking an antisemitic Instagram comment, saying the like happened while she was trying to delete it, and adding that she does not support language that targets or harms the Jewish community.

A personality framework cannot explain why anyone holds a political conviction, and this one will not try. What it can explain is what happened on the other side of the screen.

For five years millions of parents handed her their toddler's morning on an unspoken premise: that this woman's warmth was for their kid. She never made that promise. Watch any episode and the sentence underneath it is that a child is a child, which is the same sentence whether the child is in Ohio or Rafah. When she said it without a border, a large share of her audience experienced it as a betrayal, and what they had actually discovered is that the care they were consuming had never been addressed to them in particular. They had mistaken a disposition for a relationship.

What the fight costs her is easier to see. She is being called a liar about the one attribute she has organized her entire life around, and she keeps trying to settle it by insisting on her own sincerity, which is the least persuasive move available and the only one she seems able to reach for.

She is better at defending Mister Rogers than herself. She brings up his 1969 episode often, the one where he shared a wading pool with a Black police officer while American pools were still being segregated. "I know it didn't feel easy to do that," she told the Post. And then, about her own situation: "I think, in time, what I'm doing won't seem as controversial."

## The Apology Ms. Rachel Owed No One

Time is what she gives everyone else.

On January 9, 2025, she uploaded a video. Then she went quiet.

Weeks passed. Then more weeks. Parents on the internet, being parents on the internet, speculated. On March 21 she finally addressed it in an Instagram comment, and the first two words are the ones worth reading twice.

"I'm sorry. We've had some family things to attend to."

Her daughter Susannah would arrive on April 8, born via surrogate. Accurso said publicly that she had not been able to carry the pregnancy for medical reasons, and said nothing else about it. Her son was seven. She had a business, a Netflix launch, and two years of the worst public accusations of her life behind her. And what she led with, to an audience of toddlers who cannot read and parents who were not owed anything, was an apology for having taken a break.

It was the second time in three years she had gone dark under pressure and come back explaining herself to people who had not asked. After the Jules campaign she returned with a caption that read "Love > fear." This time she opened with "I'm sorry."

This is the part of the pattern that does not [photograph well under pressure](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-types-in-stress). The care is real, the exhaustion is real, and the rest is a permission problem: taking time back for herself registers as a debt she has to make good on.

## Ms. Rachel's Emmy Loss and the Folder She Took to Congress

The industry did eventually give her a night of her own.

In March 2026 she went to the Children's and Family Emmys with the first two nominations of her career, and she lost Outstanding Preschool Series to _Sesame Street_.

There is something almost too neat about that. The successor loses to the institution. She had spent years being called the Mister Rogers of her era by people who meant it as the highest compliment available, and the Academy handed the statue to the show that has been doing it since 1969. She had already told the Post what she thought of the comparison: "I revere him as a saint."

Three months later she was in a congressional office with a folder of letters and drawings from children in immigration detention, and a petition to close the Dilley processing center carrying 324,000 signatures.

Losing an award is the kind of thing a career answers with a campaign. She answered it with somebody else's children.

### Ms. Rachel's Wing: 2w1

The 2w1 reading rests on credentials and moral vocabulary. Accurso did not simply want to help toddlers, she went and got two master's degrees so the help would be correct, and the stated decision rule for the entire company is a correctness test: "Is this helpful for kids?" Her language runs to moral absolutes. "Not one is excluded" is the sentence of someone stating a rule they consider binding on themselves. The 1 <a href="/enneagram-corner/enneagram-wings-complete-guide">wing</a> also explains the specific shape of her advocacy, which is dutiful and repetitive rather than strategic, and which she frames as prayer: "How can I help children? is just my prayer."

The 2w3 case is real and should be conceded. The commercial machine described earlier is exactly what that wing builds, because 2w3s are the ones who scale care into an audience-facing production. The counterweight is that the scale arrived through the platform rather than being chased, and that she spent two decades doing the identical work in rooms with no witnesses in them. On balance: 2w1, with 2w3 machinery bolted on by circumstance and by a spouse who came from Broadway.

### Ms. Rachel's Instinctual Subtype: so/sx

Social first. The social Two orients toward a group rather than a household, and Accurso's arena is a category of person: children, everywhere, as a class. She organizes petitions, curates exhibitions, testifies to legislators, and describes her platform as a responsibility owed to a population. That is <a href="/enneagram-corner/enneagram-instinctual-subtypes">social instinct</a> in its most legible form.

Sexual second, in the specific sense of one-to-one intensity. Her actual method is dyadic. She talks to a single child through the lens, and her most disclosive statement about the advocacy is about one relationship: "Meeting Rahaf changed my life. I'm not the same as I was before I met her."

Self-preservation last, and not subtly. She recorded until her voice failed. She works with her husband, so the studio is also the house. She has taken two emergency breaks in three years and apologized for one of them.

### Stress and Growth Arrows

Under pressure, Twos move toward Eight, and the shift is visible in the record. The woman who spent five years never making an overt political statement began publicly refusing to collaborate with people who had not spoken up, cancelled her Times subscription in a post, replied directly on the magazines that quoted Olivia Munn, and walked into a congressional office with a folder. That is confrontation from a person whose entire brand is the absence of it.

Toward Four in growth, the tell is aesthetic. She curated an exhibition of children's artwork in a Manhattan gallery and wore a dress printed with those drawings to accept a Glamour award, then read a child's line from the podium: "I want to tell the world my sisters are not numbers. They are moons." Grief turned into an image rather than a task. Fours do that.

### Counterarguments: Why Ms. Rachel Might Not Be Type 2

The strongest alternative is Type 1. She is a credentialed educator with a reformer's vocabulary, she speaks in moral absolutes, and her advocacy has the flavor of duty. The evidence against it is Aron's, quoted earlier: he is the perfectionist, and she is the one willing to ship something unfinished because the kids need it now. A One defends the standard. Accurso defends her sincerity, every time, in the same words.

The honest anomaly is the business. Type 2s are characteristically terrible at boundaries and at charging money, and Accurso runs a hard-edged, well-defended commercial operation. That a Broadway veteran and a talent agency supply the boundary function she cannot supply herself is plausible and unsourced. It is the one behavior the typing does not cover, and it should stay on the table.

## Nobody Is Holding the Trolley for Ms. Rachel

A little girl in Springvale, Maine, once crossed a living room and put her body in front of a television, arms out, to stop a toy trolley from leaving.

She lost. The trolley left because the show was over, and shows are over. That is the part a small child cannot accept and the part every adult in the room already knows.

Then she grew up and built the thing that does not end. A queue that does not run out at bedtime, or on a bad week, or when the woman making it has lost her voice. No child watching her ever has to get out of the chair and stop anything.

Nobody has ever done that for her. There was no one on the far side of her screen holding it open, there is not one now, and it does not appear to have occurred to her to ask.

She is 43. She is still standing in front of the glass with her arms out. She is just on the other side of it.
