> "Waste no more time talking about what a good person is like. Just f\*cking be one."
> — Ryan Holiday, rendering _Meditations_ 10.16, 2026

<p class="firstLetter">In April 2026, a man who had spent more than a decade teaching strangers to go easy on other people filmed himself sticking his tongue out at Ivanka Trump.</p>

The video is a split screen. On one half she is describing what she took from Marcus Aurelius. On the other half Ryan Holiday smirks, rolls his eyes, and at one point gives a thumbs down while she is still talking. Mediaite transcribed the whole thing in May 2026, including the objection underneath the smirk: "She's totally right. That's exactly what Marcus Aurelius said... she's just not living or acting in accordance with it in any way."

She is right. That is the part that bothers him.

Two years before that video he posted his most-quoted line from Book 5 of _Meditations_ to the Daily Stoic account, in February 2024: "Be tolerant with others and strict with yourself." He wrote his own gloss underneath it: standards for yourself high enough that you have to stretch, standards for everyone else low enough that you are pleasantly surprised when they are met. The Daily Stoic, his own company, has put it flatly: projecting strict standards onto people who never agreed to follow them is a waste of time.

Then he spent a week projecting.

He broke his own rule, in public, on camera, and the internet told him so at length. The part worth understanding is why a man builds a thirteen-book career on a rule he cannot keep.

## What is Ryan Holiday's personality type?

### Ryan Holiday is an Enneagram Type 1

Start with the exercise, because it is the cleanest evidence in his life and almost nobody reads it correctly.

Holiday runs, swims, or bikes every day. Asked about it on the Diary of a CEO podcast in 2023, he ruled out every reason you would expect: "I'm not training for a marathon. I'm not trying to set any records. I'm trying to make a promise to myself and keep that promise. And it doesn't matter how badly the writing goes, it doesn't matter what news I just got from my agent, it doesn't matter how my business is doing."

Read what he threw away there. No distance. No time. No record. He deliberately stripped out every number that would let the swim be an achievement, and kept the only thing he actually wants from it, which is a ruling. Each morning he assembles a small piece of evidence that he is still the kind of person who does what he said he would do. Then he goes to work, and by evening the evidence has expired, and he has to produce it again.

That is a [Type One](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-1) engine. The Achiever wants the record. The One wants the verdict.

His mentor described this out loud, to his face, and seems not to have realized he was doing it. In a filmed conversation about _The Laws of Human Nature_, [Robert Greene](/personality-analysis/robert-greene) reached for Holiday as his example of how everyone walks around carrying a self-opinion. He improvised Holiday's, and the testimony that fell out in 2018 is the most useful thing anyone has said about him: "You, Ryan, have an idea of yourself... I'm very self-reliant, I'm very disciplined, I'm a good person, I'm conscientious... I'm a good father."

Greene has known him since he was nineteen. Given a free hand to describe how Holiday sees himself, the words he reached for were _conscientious_ and _a good person_, and nothing in the list is about being ambitious or brilliant or successful. The man who spent his twenties manufacturing publicity carries a self-image built entirely out of moral vocabulary.

And then there is the shape of the work itself. Between 2021 and 2025 Holiday published four books, one for each of the cardinal virtues: _Courage Is Calling_, _Discipline Is Destiny_, _Right Thing, Right Now_, _Wisdom Takes Work_. Thirteen books in thirteen years, and the culminating project was a report card with four categories, written by the student.

**A note on the obvious objection.** Holiday would find this page tedious, and his reasons are good ones. His entire position is that character is a practice rather than a label, that what you are is the thing you keep doing. He said so mid-tirade in the April 2026 video. "Stoicism is not a brand," he told the camera. "It's a practice. And it demands honesty, it demands self-awareness, it demands accountability." That is a real argument with a site like this one. We agree with him about the practice, and we are describing which practice he cannot stop performing. Something is asking him for that swim every morning, and it is not a publisher.

## Why Ryan Holiday dropped out of college at 19

His father was a police detective. His mother was a high school principal.

Two parents, two careers, one shared job description: holding other people to a standard and noticing when they fall short of it. Nobody has to lecture a child in that house about right and wrong, because the furniture is already teaching it.

He lasted less than two years at UC Riverside before quitting in his sophomore year, moving to Los Angeles, and talking his way into working for Robert Greene, author of _The 48 Laws of Power_. On its face this is the story of a kid escaping the rules.

Listen to how he described it in 2012, though, on Chase Jarvis's show, back when _Trust Me, I'm Lying_ was his only book and he had no reputation to protect. "I look at my education as having really started the day I dropped out of school, cuz now it was on me. It was my full-time job and no one was going to demand that I turn in papers... It wasn't, you know, a 9:00 a.m. class and then a noon class and then a 3 p.m. class. It was all the time, every day."

He is describing an upgrade in supervision. School demanded papers on a schedule, which meant there were hours when nothing was being demanded. He left, and the demand became continuous.

This is the move the pattern makes when it works well. A One who resents an external authority rarely walks away from authority itself. They relocate it inward, where it can run without a bell schedule and without anyone to negotiate with. He did not flee the household of two enforcers. He dissolved the last one standing between him and the job.

He also gave the whole thing away in the same interview, in a sentence nobody picked up: "I'm good at quitting actually." He is, and he has quit everything except the demand.

## Two Sides: the essay Ryan Holiday wrote before anyone was reading

On July 5, 2011, a 24-year-old marketing director at American Apparel published a short essay on his personal blog. There was no Daily Stoic. No bookstore. No ranch. _Trust Me, I'm Lying_ was a year from publication. He had no audience to manage and no brand to protect, so he wrote the truth, and then he left it up for fifteen years.

Read it as a document rather than a memory. Each line came first, and everything after it is the receipt.

> "I have always felt pulled in two conflicting directions. It has not been pleasant. It's made me feel miserable when I should feel happy, afraid when I should feel secure–a constant tension and uncertainty that takes an exhausting toll."

He is twenty-four and already exhausted. The phrase to hold onto is _when I should_. Even his description of his own misery is graded against a standard.

> "There is the part of me drawn to discipline. To work and do great things in this brief bit of time I have been given."

Eleven years later he published _Discipline Is Destiny_.

> "There is another part of me pulled towards recklessness. To simply live. To hate the things that most people seem to value."

This half rarely makes it into the public record. It is the half that dropped out, moved across the country, went to work for a clothing company famous for its sexualized billboards and for the man who wrote _I Hope They Serve Beer in Hell_, and spent six years getting very good at lying to journalists.

> "The few moves I've made in my life that could be considered daring or even reckless–dropping out of school, packing up and moving across the country–were almost immediately overwhelmed by the responsible side."

_Almost immediately overwhelmed._ He knows the outcome of the fight. He has watched it resolve the same way every time.

> "All this is to say that there is a tension in me that I have trouble grappling with. It is the feeling of living in a house divided against itself."

And then the line that reorganizes everything he has published since:

> "And yet I think they are both equal parts fear, the fear of simply _just being_."

Eight years after writing that sentence, he published a book called _Stillness Is the Key_.

The man who would go on to sell the case for stillness to millions of readers told us, in advance and without being asked, what stillness did to him.

## Did Ryan Holiday quit manipulating the media, or repoint it?

The half he called reckless left a résumé, and by his own accounting it is bad. At American Apparel he manufactured outrage on purpose, planted stories with small blogs knowing larger outlets would pick them up without checking, wrote comments under invented names, and engineered controversy over his own client's ad campaigns.

Then in 2012 he wrote a book explaining exactly how he had done it, mechanism by mechanism, and put the word _Confessions_ in the subtitle.

A man who is simply good at publicity and wants to keep being good at it does not publish the manual. He keeps the edge. Holiday burned the edge in print at twenty-five, and when Chase Jarvis asked him about that subtitle in 2012 the answer arrived tangled. The book was "here's what I do and here's how you can do it." He declined to call the rest of it an apology. Then he landed on the phrase he wanted: "an admission of some guilt in some sense."

_An admission of some guilt._ He reached for that unprompted, while promoting a business book.

Which makes the swing from media manipulator to Stoicism's most successful evangelist stop looking like a swing. He had been keeping the ledger the entire time, and _Trust Me, I'm Lying_ was the ledger going public. What that does not settle is the harder question: did he shut the machine down, or aim it at cargo he approves of?

Look at what happened next. _The Obstacle Is the Way_ came out in 2014 and did not spread the way philosophy books spread. It spread through locker rooms. ESPN traced the route in a May 2020 feature: Mike Lombardi, three decades a Patriots executive, handed the book to Seahawks general manager John Schneider at a pro day and told him it distilled the Patriot Way. Schneider passed it to Pete Carroll and the staff. "Soon, word gets out," ESPN wrote. "The two best teams in the league are reading the same book. Soon everyone else in the NFL is too." By August 2016 he was addressing the defending national champions at Alabama's football complex, with Nick Saban asking the first question; a year later a Steelers part-owner carried a copy into Ryan Shazier's hospital room.

A book about accepting what you cannot control became an operating manual for the most win-obsessed institutions in America. ESPN walked its readers through the American Apparel years in the same feature, because the two halves belong together.

That is the case for the prosecution and the case for the defense, and they are the same sentence. He learned exactly how attention gets manufactured, decided the machine was corrosive, and kept running it, at scale, for freight he believes in. Whether that counts as restitution or as laundering is not something the record settles, and he is still living inside the question.

## Why the Naval Academy canceled Ryan Holiday

The machine costs him, though, and the clearest bill came due in Annapolis. For four years running, Holiday had gone there to lecture midshipmen on the Stoic virtues. April 14, 2025 was to be the fifth, in front of the entire sophomore class, on the theme of wisdom.

His material centered on James Stockdale, the Naval Academy graduate whose Stoicism kept him alive through seven years as a prisoner of war, and whom the Navy had sent to Stanford in 1960 to read Marx and Lenin in the original. Stockdale put the logic to his parents plainly: "You really can't do well competing against something you don't understand as well as something you can." Wisdom requires exposure to the ideas you find repellent.

Roughly an hour before he was due on stage, the Academy called and asked him to drop the slide referencing the 381 books that had been pulled from the shelves of its Nimitz Library.

<span class="key-stat-number">381</span>
<span class="key-stat-label">books removed from the Nimitz Library. He was asked to not mention them for one hour.</span>
</div>

He declined. The lecture was canceled, and so was a planned talk to the Navy football team, whose players had been reading his books for years, a sequence he laid out afterward in a _New York Times_ op-ed. "The idea that there are topics that are off limits or that they can't handle is absurd on its face," he told WTOP afterward.

Jonathan Friedman, who directs PEN America's U.S. Free Expression programs, put the institutional version bluntly. "The Naval Academy is becoming known not only for banning books," he said, "but also for cancelling someone for planning to discuss the banning of books. This is a tragic new low."

Hold this episode next to the video that came a year later, because the same mechanism produced both. Something was wrong, in public, and Holiday could not agree to let it stand unremarked. In Annapolis that cost him the stage, and it read as courage, because a hard thing was done at real expense. The wiring does not distinguish between the two occasions.

## What Ryan Holiday does every morning before he writes

The rest of the time he holds, and the holding is daily work. He gets in the water. Nobody times him.

<p class="inner-thought">Nothing today has gone wrong yet. That is the problem with the morning. The record is clean, which means it is only available to be spoiled, and the swim is the one entry I can post before the day starts making its own case.</p>

The rest of the practice runs the same way. He writes his books on four-by-six note cards, thousands of them, the same system Robert Greene taught him at nineteen. He journals daily, and the questions he puts to the page are interrogations: "Why am I angry? What caused this? What can I do about it?... Why am I sad?"

Stephanie Ruhle asked him at the 92nd Street Y in December 2025 what he does with the pages. "Nothing," he said. "The page, doing them, is what I do." Does he keep them, then? "Yeah, I keep them because I struggle to throw anything away."

He has built a business on the premise of letting go and cannot throw away a notebook. The pages are the record, and the record has to exist even if no one ever consults it. Somewhere in Bastrop County, Texas, on forty acres with cattle and donkeys and goats, there is a growing archive of evidence in a case that will never come to trial.

The bookstore runs on the same rule. He and his wife Samantha put their life savings into a 140-year-old building on Main Street in Bastrop in 2021, and where a typical independent carries thousands of titles, The Painted Porch stocks what its own site calls "a small collection of only our absolute favorite books." The policy is posted: "If it's on our shelves, it's because we read it and think that you should too. Period." A retailer in a business with almost no margin, refusing to sell anything he has not personally vetted.

### Ryan Holiday's Wing: 1w9

The Nine <a href="/enneagram-corner/enneagram-wings-complete-guide">wing</a> shows up in the geography. He left Los Angeles for forty acres outside Austin, filled them with livestock, and put his bookshop in a Texas town of roughly ten thousand people. His public affect is cool and unhurried, and his anger, when it surfaces, arrives chilled rather than hot.

A 1w2 corrects you warmly, to your face, framed as help. Holiday's habitual mode is the essay, the newsletter, the book. He addresses the standard rather than the person, which is the Nine wing routing the correction away from direct confrontation and toward the system.

Which is exactly why the April 2026 video is diagnostically loud. The 1w9 pattern is to deliberate privately and publish the conclusion. Holiday published the deliberation, on a split screen, with the eye-rolls left in.

### Ryan Holiday's Instinctual Subtype: Social (so/1)

The social One is the one who teaches correct conduct to the group. Watch what Holiday actually reaches for when he is not being asked about himself. Talking to Ruhle, he went straight to public models: "We are a world in desperate need of heroes, like models of people who did the right thing when it wasn't in their interest to do so... These are the kind of people we put statues up of."

Statues. A self-preservation One would be describing his own habits and his own household. The <a href="/enneagram-corner/enneagram-instinctual-subtypes">social subtype</a> thinks in monuments, curricula, and what the culture is teaching its young. A newsletter, then a second one for fathers, then a bookstore, then a lecture circuit that ran through the Naval Academy for five straight years. That is a man organizing a syllabus for everyone.

### Stress and Growth Arrows

Under pressure the One <a href="/enneagram-corner/enneagram-types-in-stress">moves to Four</a>: aggrieved, withdrawn, convinced of being misunderstood. The clearest read on the April episode came from Doug Bates, a Stoicism writer watching it in real time, who noticed that Holiday "seems to want everyone to know how upset he is about all of this." That is the Four note precisely. The injury became the subject.

The Seven direction shows up in his account of where he has landed. On Diary of a CEO he described waking up "with this sense of living in the bonus that it's all extra." The parenting question in the section below, the one that deflates his own urgency before he can aim it at a child, is the same movement.

### Counterarguments: Why Ryan Holiday Might Not Be Type 1

The Three case is legitimate and should be stated at full strength. He runs three media properties, a bookstore, and a touring business, all built on his own name, and he spent six years as a professional manufacturer of image, which is the Three's native fluency. He publishes at a pace that reads as pure achievement drive.

Two things hold the line, both established above. The one practice nobody watches has had every metric stripped out of it, and a Three protects the scoreboard. Greene's improvised list contains no achievement word at all.

The Five case is thinner but real: the note cards, the reading, the ranch, the archive of journals he cannot discard. Fives withdraw from the audience, though, and Holiday has spent well over a decade building three of them.

What would change our mind: sustained evidence that he abandons a standard when it stops paying. So far the record runs the other way, and Annapolis cost him a stage.

## What kind of father does Ryan Holiday try to be?

Go back to the last item on Greene's list: _I'm a good father_. He put it last, which is where people tend to put the thing they are least willing to be wrong about.

He married Samantha in 2015 and they have two sons. He also runs a second daily newsletter written for fathers, published _The Daily Dad_ in 2023, and hosts a parenting podcast his wife has appeared on. He took the one relationship where the standard cannot be met by effort and built a publishing operation around it.

Then he does something the pattern does not predict. The question he says he puts to himself constantly as a parent, from the 2023 Diary of a CEO conversation, runs backwards: "Is this thing that I'm about to argue with them or force them to do or feel insecure about, does it actually matter? And the answer is almost always no. It doesn't matter. It's made up."

A man raised by a detective and a principal, who describes his own childhood as a stretch when "I felt a lot of pressure and I was demanded or asked a lot of myself," has decided that the main work of fathering is to not do that. The standard he cannot stop applying to himself is the one thing he is deliberately declining to hand down.

## The week Ryan Holiday could not be tolerant

The restraint he manages at home is the restraint that failed him in public. Back to the split screen, because this is where the sympathetic reading has to earn itself against the hostile one, and the hostile one is well argued.

Doug Bates, who writes the Stoicism newsletter _Ataraxia or Bust_, published the sharpest version in May 2026: "Holiday has turned Stoicism into a brand more than anyone else," and "Holiday's real gripe with Ivanka appears to be that her admiration of Marcus Aurelius infringes upon that brand." The deeper problem Bates named is that the tradition Holiday represents is built on the discipline of leaving other people's choices alone.

Concede the accurate parts, because most of them are accurate. He does run a brand. He projected a strict standard onto a person who never signed up for it, the specific error his own company has written newsletters warning against, and then he compounded it for days.

Now the part the hostile reading misses. Territory does not explain the tongue.

Brand defense is a calculation, and by his own published account Holiday is unusually good at calculating. If this were about market position he had a hundred cleaner moves available, he knows every one of them, and he used none. He posted a video of his own face failing to stay composed. That is not a strategy. That is a leak.

Here is what leaked. For this pattern, an obvious wrong sitting in public with nobody naming it does not read as neutral. It reads as participation. Silence gets filed as endorsement, and endorsement gets filed as being the sort of person who lets things slide, which is the exact charge he has been defending himself against every morning in the pool since roughly 2011. Ivanka Trump quoting Marcus Aurelius put a wrong in front of him with a live camera on it. The composure he sells is real, and it is not native. It is a structure he built and maintains at cost, and that week the structure gave.

This does not make him right. He broke his own rule, and the rule was the good one. He has also passed this exact test before, on the record.

In March 2015 the _New York Times_ ran an op-ed on Stoicism by Massimo Pigliucci, a philosophy professor at CUNY City College, and it climbed the site's most-emailed list. Holiday, who had by then made the subject his own, wrote down what he actually felt: "I also had an embarrassing human reaction: jealousy. Why did Professor Massimo Pigliucci get this opportunity and I didn't? Why are things so unfair?" Then he emailed the professor and published a long interview with him on his own site, because the article "almost certainly introduced tens of thousands of people to a topic I care about."

Same trigger both times. Someone else, in public, holding his philosophy. In 2015 he aimed the correction at himself and handed the other man a microphone. Eleven years later he stuck out his tongue.

Which means the anger arrived first and the philosophy came second. Thirteen books of counsel about equanimity are not a report from a calm man. They are the working notes of someone who needed the equanimity badly enough to reverse-engineer it, write it down, and sell it to a list he built himself, so that he would have to keep practicing it in front of witnesses.

In August 2026 he took _Wisdom Takes Work_ on the road, to Minneapolis, Chicago, and Detroit, with a pop-up bookstore in the lobby. Ten months after publication he is still touring a title that reads like a status report on himself.

## The Court of One

Nobody times the swim. Nobody reads the journals, including him.

He is thirty-nine years old, with thirteen books behind him, a bookshop on a small-town main street, two sons, and a standing invitation from most stages in the country. Every morning he still gets into the water and covers a distance no one will ever ask about, to obtain a ruling that expires by dinner.

At twenty-four, before any of it existed, he wrote down what he was actually afraid of, and it was not failure. It was simply being.

He was right. He has been outrunning it in public ever since, one virtue at a time.
