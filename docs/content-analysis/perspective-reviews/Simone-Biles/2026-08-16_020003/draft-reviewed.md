> "My knees are fine. My brain hurts." — Simone Biles, on what she told her family from the back room in Tokyo, _Call Her Daddy_, 2024

<p class="firstLetter">Aimee Boorman coached Simone Biles for twelve years, and she used to tease her about the ground. Did she look at the beam on the way down? At the floor? At the bars? Boorman recalled the answer on the <em>Beyond Medals and Perfection</em> podcast in October 2025. "No, that's scary. I don't want to look at it. That's scary."</p>

She never looked. She just always knew where it was.

Boorman calls her an air savant. Asked once what she perceives during a triple-twisting double back, Biles described not a picture but a sound, quoted back by her coach on that same 2025 <em>Beyond Medals and Perfection</em> episode. "It's more of a whoosh that I hear in my ears."

So here is a woman with 11 Olympic medals, 30 World medals and five named skills, who has spent twenty years above a hard floor on the authority of a signal she cannot see or explain to anybody. She is 29, she has not said whether she is going to Los Angeles in 2028, and the reason she gives for not knowing has nothing to do with her body. On July 27, 2021, in a Tokyo stadium with no fans in it, the signal cut out.

The most decorated gymnast in history is known worldwide for the day she stopped, and that is the part almost everyone reads wrong.

## What is Simone Biles's personality type?

### Simone Biles is an Enneagram Type 6

Search her name with the word "personality" and the internet hands you three answers. So Syncd says 3w2. Personality Database crowd-votes ESFJ. One Enneagram site says Six. Nobody shows their work. Here is the work.

In 2015 Biles won her third consecutive World all-around title, which no woman had done before. Of the twelve years she coached her, Boorman still names that competition as her proudest, and the reason has nothing to do with the score. Here is how she told it on <em>Beyond Medals and Perfection</em> in 2025. "At the end of it she came up to me and said, 'did I do it?'"

There were judges, a scoreboard, a medal, and a stadium of people who had watched. She walked up to the one adult whose read she trusted and asked her to confirm the result.

Eight years later, preparing for Paris, she was doing it again. Her therapist had already worked out what caused the collapse in Tokyo. Biles kept reopening it, and told _Call Her Daddy_ in 2024 how those sessions went: "I'm like, hey, look, it's Olympic year. Did we figure out why that happened? And she's like, Simone, we figured out why this has happened. I was like, are we sure? Are we sure? cuz it can't happen again."

Two sentences, eight years apart, running the same errand. A [Type 6](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6) does not doubt the evidence. She doubts whether it covers everything, because what she is really asking is whether she is safe now, and no scoreboard has ever answered that.

The Type 3 reading is the popular one and it is not stupid: the medals, the branding, the endorsement portfolio, her own line about needing to find out who she is without gymnastics. It breaks on those two occasions, when the result was already secured and she went looking for a person to certify it anyway. An Achiever trusts the scoreboard, because the scoreboard is the point. Biles trusts Boorman, and Cecile, and her therapist on Thursdays. She checks with them.

**Confidence: high.** The wing, the subtype, the arrows and the argument against all of it are collapsed further down the page.

## The room Simone Biles kept leaving at night

She was three years old when the state took her.

Her biological mother, Shannon, was struggling with drug and alcohol addiction, and calls were made. Simone, her sister Adria and their older siblings Ashley and Tevin went into foster care in Ohio. The home separated children by sex, girls in one room, boys in another.

Biles remembers almost nothing from that period. She remembers one thing.

"From my knowledge that sometimes during the night or during the day they would take kids out and replace them into a different home and that's usually how siblings were separated. So I was just so terrified that if I woke up my brother wouldn't be there."

She was three. She had already worked out the mechanism: people get moved while you sleep, and you find out in the morning. So she stopped waiting for morning. "Always sneak in and go sleep with him."

Sit with what that child did. She could not stop the system from moving anybody, lock a door, or ask a grown-up a question that produced a useful answer. What she could do was get out of bed, walk down a hallway, and put her own body in the room, where she would know the second anything changed. Verification was the only power on offer.

At six she was adopted, with Adria, by her grandfather Ronald Biles and his wife Nellie, who raised them in Spring, Texas. Ashley and Tevin were adopted separately, in Ohio. Nellie has described the beginning without sanding it down: "I knew I had my own barriers because these were not my biological children. You do everything that's nurturing, that's mothering, but emotionally, you still have to be there 100%."

The bond arrived. Biles calls them mom and dad without qualification, and when a gym full of kids once stopped practice to gawk at the news, her confusion was total. "Well, yeah, aren't you? Where'd you come from?"

Her clearest statement about any of it is actuarial. "I have no idea where I would be without being adopted. Unfortunately, I might end up the same statistic that other foster kids unfortunately end up... I fear for what my life would have looked like."

Three Olympics later, describing her own childhood, she reaches for the word statistic. The girl who checked the room at night grew into a woman who still checks.

## Why Simone Biles asked her coach "Did I do it?"

She found gymnastics because it rained: a daycare field trip got weathered out, the backup was a gym, and six-year-old Simone started copying the girls flipping across the floor. She has never once told that story in the singular. "We got into gymnastics. I say we as me and my sister."

Boorman spent twelve years refusing to squeeze what she had. When a teenage Biles started phoning in her training in 2013, Boorman let it play out. "I had to allow her and give her a safe space to fail in." Biles competed badly, disgusted herself, and came back having coached herself. Failure had to be survivable before she would risk anything, which is also why Boorman was the one she walked up to in 2015 and asked her whether she had done it.

Then watch what she does with her nerves. Boorman describes it as routine: "Before she competed, she was cheering for other people. She was watching the competition for enjoyment purposes." Minutes from her own routine she would be yelling encouragement at a gymnast from another country. Boorman's read on why: "She wasn't sitting there in her mind worrying about if she was going to fail somehow or if she was going to let somebody down."

Let somebody down. Years before Tokyo, she was already draining her dread into her rivals.

Rio 2016 gave her four golds and a bronze on beam that America told her was a disgrace. Here is what the summit of her sport felt like at nineteen:

<div class="pull-quote">"I was scared for the rest of my life. Like, how can I beat this? How can I top this? I won the Olympics at 19."</div>

She read it as the opening of a decline she now had to defend. Then she went quiet about the cost. "If you had something to describe me as, strong went before that word. So I felt like I could never show a sign of weakness." There was exactly one person she cried in front of, and it was Adria. "A lot of the time if it was my sister cuz we're very very very close. If it wasn't her, silent cries."

Five years later, in Tokyo, she lost the ability to keep any of it out of sight.

## Simone Biles's Tokyo vault, one beat at a time

An Olympic team final is a procedure: event order, warm-up length, touches on the apparatus, who can replace whom, all fixed before anyone walks in. Here it is, with her own account of what was happening inside it.

<div class="timeline">

**Back of the arena.** She has been getting lost in the air in training, what gymnasts call the twisties, and pushing through it. She tells her teammates: "I'm fighting demons. I'm fighting demons right now, but I'm going to do it for you guys." She has carried a formless dread since before the plane landed. "I knew something was going to go wrong. I couldn't pinpoint what it was or when." She tells no one else, on the grounds that she is the veteran and the rest are at their first Games. "They're looking up to me and I'm guiding them."

**The one-touch warm-up.** You sit forty-five minutes, then get exactly one practice vault. She goes over the table, does a one and a half, lands wrong. "What the... like what was that?" Her teammates crowd her. "Are you okay? Are you okay?" There are no more touches.

**Chalking up.** She does not know what she is about to do. She runs the options where she stands: a double pike she has not warmed up in four days, the two and a half on the scoresheet, a double back she rules out because "my coach will kill me. My team will never forgive."

**The vault.** She salutes and runs. Mid-air the twist count leaves her. She opens early, comes down on her feet, and grins like somebody who has gotten away with something.

**The landing.** Not relief. Not pain.

<p class="inner-thought">America hates me. The world is going to hate me. And I can only see what they're saying on Twitter right now.</p>

**The walk off.** "If I could have gotten on a plane and flown home, I would have done it."

**The back room.** She tells her coach she is finished. Cecile asks if she is sure. For once, she is. "If I survived that, I don't know how much else I can survive. Like I always say I'm a cat with nine lives, but I think that was my ninth."

**The phone call.** Her parents are in Texas, kept out of Tokyo by COVID rules. Adria, the sister she went into foster care with and the only person she cries in front of, had decided to nap through it. She wakes when the home phone rings, sees the name, and answers. Biles hears her voice and comes apart.

> "Mom, come pick me up, I'm scared."

</div>

She is twenty-four, the most decorated gymnast alive, nine thousand kilometres from Spring, Texas, and the sentence her body produces is the one a lost child says. Her parents had assumed a broken bone.

## Why Simone Biles didn't quit on her team in Tokyo

Within hours Charlie Kirk was on his podcast calling her "very selfish, she's immature and she's a shame to the country," adding "she's totally a sociopath" and "what kind of person skips the gold medal match?" Piers Morgan wrote that mental health had become the go-to excuse for poor performance in elite sport: "What a joke."

Give them their strongest version, because they had one. An Olympic team is four other women who trained five years, and one was going home without a medal because of a decision Biles made in a hallway. That cost landed on real people, and Biles has never argued otherwise.

What the critics did not know is the arithmetic. Olympic team finals run two-up two-count: two gymnasts per apparatus, both scores count, nothing thrown away. Biles was entered on everything. "If I got hurt on that vault, they couldn't replace me... We would have never won a medal. But since what happened happened, we went to the back. At that point, they could rule it as a mental injury and all of that stuff, and we got to put Jordan in."

There was one window. Withdraw before an injury and Chiles enters and the United States stays alive. Take one more vault, catch the floor with an ankle, and four women go home with nothing. Whether she ran that in the hallway or assembled it three years later is not recoverable, and barely changes the finding, because building a load-bearing justification after the fact does the same work. Either way the decision had to come out defensible to four other people. The United States won silver, and Biles spent the rest of the night as, in her words, "their loudest and best cheerleader."

The reason to read this as loyalty rather than self-preservation is what it cost her afterward, which she has never let herself off for. "That's something that I'll never forgive myself for... I wish I could have been in there with them in a way that I was supposed to." She could not look at Laurent Landi for years, and never said why: "I felt like I failed him. And I've never said that out loud." A woman protecting herself does not carry that. Making the correct call and still refusing to forgive the outcome is loyalty running ahead of self-regard, which is the least glamorous and most reliable thing about this type.

Coverage welded her withdrawal to <a href="/personality-analysis/naomi-osaka">Naomi Osaka</a>'s at the French Open weeks earlier. Osaka removed herself from a room that was extracting something from her; Biles stayed, cheered through the final, and carried the failure as a debt.

Nobody who called her weak had to sit with the ride home: the airport smile, a television hit in New York with her hair done, a convertible through a parade in her own neighbourhood, and only then permission to break.

> "I didn't want anybody to tell me that it was okay anymore. Because I'm tired of everybody telling me it's okay. It's okay to you. It's not okay to me. What happened was not okay."

## Why Simone Biles stayed at USA Gymnastics after it failed her

She was in that stadium for a reason, and it starts three years earlier. In January 2018 she disclosed that she was one of Larry Nassar's survivors. She had waited on purpose: "I wanted to be in a good enough place and to have the proper help lined up before I spoke out."

Every other survivor who had come forward was retired. Biles kept competing, and in September 2021 she told the Senate Judiciary Committee why:

> "I worked incredibly hard to make sure that my presence could maintain a connection between the failures and the competition at Tokyo 2020."

Her plainer version: "If there weren't a remaining survivor in the sport they would've just brushed it to the side."

Her testimony assigns blame twice, and the second half shows the personality. "To be clear, I blame Larry Nassar, and I also blame an entire system that enabled and perpetrated his abuse." She named USA Gymnastics, the USOPC and the FBI, then named the cost: "an exceptionally difficult burden for me to carry, particularly when required to travel to Tokyo, without the support of any of my family."

A Six's relationship to institutions is a standing argument between wanting a protector and knowing protectors fail, which is why Sixes so often stay inside organisations they have stopped believing in. Leaving ends the surveillance, and the surveillance is the entire point. It is the hallway at three years old, scaled up to a federal hearing room.

## How Simone Biles rebuilt her twisting, and what it costs per vault

Ask her for the lowest point of the Tokyo era and she does not name Tokyo.

"There were so many days I would come back in the gym and it was like one step forward, five steps back. As soon as I got lost one time, get lost, pack my bags, I'm out of there."

Laurent Landi kept walking her back to the pit and making her twist. She got through World Championships still praying mid-routine, and says one more day would have broken her.

The therapy is not decoration. On her arena tour she booked sessions in the ninety minutes before showtime, then did her makeup over the aftermath. "I'd be bawling my eyes out trying to put on my makeup because I'm talking to my therapist about the Olympic experience. Put on my face. Wow, hi Gold Over America, love to see you." Her frame for it: "I'm like an onion... I'm an open book anywhere else, but with trauma, it has to be by layers."

The turn came from the audience she had written off. They kept coming, and she broke down backstage: "I cannot believe people are still showing up. Why are they showing up? We didn't win... that's when I realized like holy crap. It's not about that."

When she finally asked her coaches to take her back to the Olympics, she brought reinforcements. "I brought my husband, you know, my love blanky." They said no, and counter-offered: back in the gym, rebuild the twisting, revisit the Olympics later.

She considered it for one beat, and her account of the beat is four words: "Do I get new coaches?" Then, immediately: "oh, you're right." That is what growth looks like on a Six, and it does not look like confidence. It looks like a woman who has stopped requiring certainty before she moves.

The love blanky has a name. Jonathan Owens is an NFL safety; they married in April 2023. In December 2023 he told <em>The Pivot Podcast</em> he had not known who she was when they met, and called himself "the catch." The internet spent weeks on it. Her account on _Call Her Daddy_ in 2024 runs in a fixed order. "I thought it was hilarious at first," then "they hurt my feelings... Why are you guys talking about my husband like this?" Then the ruling, which concedes nothing: "he has to have his moments too and I let him have it." The rule underneath is the one she has run since she was three. "Talk about me all you want, but don't come for my family."

She answers fear by putting a body in the room. Her brother's, then her husband's. On the vault, her coach's.

The Yurchenko double pike is the hardest vault any woman has competed. When Biles performs it, Laurent Landi stands on the landing mat and does not touch her. Under the code his presence alone is an automatic 0.5 deduction, which she paid at the 2023 World Championships in Antwerp on her way to having the skill named after her. Cecile Landi's reasoning: "one mistake can have serious consequences."

Half a point, in a sport decided by hundredths, on a vault she can land, bought against what happens if she cannot.

## What Simone Biles won at the Paris Olympics, and what it didn't settle

She got there hurt. Warming up floor in qualification on July 28, 2024, she came out of a tumbling pass and felt her left calf go. She taped it, competed all four events anyway, and posted the top all-around score in the field. Asked if she was okay, she answered with the number missing: "as good as I can be."

On the morning of the all-around final she had a therapy appointment. She had been booking sessions around the time difference all Games, and was uneasy about saying so, knowing which 2021 headline it would be filed under. Her framing in _Simone Biles Rising_: "It wasn't at a point of weakness, it was a point of strength."

Then she went and did it. Team gold on July 30, all-around gold on August 1 (eight years after the last one), vault gold on August 3. Her account of what had changed is not about her legs. "There was always a part of me that thought what if? But that was obviously my anxiety talking."

The last two days are the ones worth watching. She came off the beam in the event final and placed fifth, and her explanation went straight to the room: silent arena, no music, spectators shushed for cheering. She was exact about conditions, which is what she does with an outcome she did not choose. Then the floor final, silver behind Rebeca Andrade. On the podium Jordan Chiles asked whether they should bow. Biles answered in one word: "absolutely." The three of them made the first all-Black gymnastics podium in Olympic history, and Biles spent her press conference on the woman who beat her. "She's queen."

Her summary was, for her, unusually generous to herself. "I've accomplished way more than in my wildest dreams."

Now hold that against 2015. A third consecutive World title produced "did I do it?" Three Olympic golds produced a woman who, two years later, still cannot tell you whether she is finished.

## Simone Biles is 29 and the answer is still 50-50

In April 2026 she stood at the Laureus Awards in Madrid and CNN asked about Los Angeles. "I feel like we're still at a 50-50." She was specific about which half is open. "Mental health plays a big role in it because, physically, my coaches will get me in shape." She told L'Équipe she will be there either way: "whether on the apparatus or in the stands, I still don't know that."

Owens is in Indianapolis now, ninth NFL season. In June 2026 Biles was hospitalised for something she has never named, posted a photo of her wristbands from bed, called it one of the scariest experiences of her life, and thanked "my close circle who reached out, checked in, visited & or sent flowers." Nothing further. An open book about the facts, layers about the fear.

Then, in August 2026, she posted a picture of her locker at the World Champions Centre. Two words: "Just how I left it."

Not a comeback. Not a retirement. A photograph of a place she checked on, which is what she has done since she was three, walking down a hall in the dark to see whether the room was still the way she left it.

Every gymnast alive will tell you Simone Biles's gift is her air awareness, that impossible sense of where the ground is at every instant of every rotation. Almost nobody notices the other half of what Boorman said. She always knew where the ground was, and she never once looked at it. She was too scared to look.
