## Bottom-line verdict

I came in knowing the name and one blurry fact — a gymnast quit at an Olympics and people argued
about it. I left able to state who she is, why she matters, what the article claims, and what the
type answer is, without opening another tab for any of that. On the core job of this perspective,
the draft succeeds, and it succeeds by an unusual route: it explains almost nothing about
gymnastics as a sport and instead explains the two or three rules that carry meaning, then spends
its length on a single legible idea. I could restate the thesis after one read — she navigates by
a signal she cannot see, she has been checking that the room is still there since she was three,
and the day she is famous for stopping is the day that pattern became visible.

Two things stop me short of a pass, and neither is a comprehension failure.

First, the article's persuasive method is verbatim quotation, and I have no way to check a single
quote. The shared packet documents that one quotation was altered inside the quotation marks in
the direction that helps the argument (`"And I think that was my anxiety talking"` → `"But that
was obviously my anxiety talking"`), and that a second quotation, attributed to a named Netflix
docuseries, could not be sourced at all. For a reader with zero independent knowledge, quotation
marks are the entire trust mechanism. Those two defects strain it.

Second, one proper noun does real work and is never introduced. Larry Nassar appears twice as the
load-bearing element of an entire section and is never identified. I had to leave the page.

The value is high and the delight is real — the opening and closing lines about the ground are the
best thing here and the piece is built to land them. Fix the two quotations, introduce Nassar, and
gloss two pieces of jargon, and this clears my bar comfortably.

## What landed

**UNFAM-H1 — the ground, opened and closed.** Opening: `"No, that's scary. I don't want to look
at it. That's scary."` Closing: _"She always knew where the ground was, and she never once looked
at it. She was too scared to look."_ I understood the entire thesis from the last sentence, and it
reframed the first one. This is the reason the article works for someone who knows nothing; it
gives me a physical image to hold instead of a personality vocabulary to learn. **Must survive
revision intact, including the placement — the payoff only works because the opening withholds it.**

**UNFAM-H2 — the spotter arithmetic.** _"Half a point, in a sport decided by hundredths, on a vault
she can land, bought against what happens if she cannot."_ Twenty-three words taught me a scoring
rule I had never heard of and simultaneously made it evidence for the argument. This is the single
most efficient passage in the draft for my standpoint. **Must survive.**

**UNFAM-H3 — the Tokyo timeline section.** _"You sit forty-five minutes, then get exactly one
practice vault."_ The bolded beat structure (Back of the arena / The one-touch warm-up / Chalking
up / The vault / The landing / The walk off / The back room / The phone call) let me follow a
procedure I have never watched, in a sport I do not know, with no confusion about what happened
when. It is also where I stopped being able to put the article down. **Must survive as a form, not
just as content.**

**UNFAM-H4 — the sentence that made the thesis click.** _"She answers fear by putting a body in
the room. Her brother's, then her husband's. On the vault, her coach's."_ Before this line I
thought the husband material was a detour. After it, three unrelated sections were one idea.
**Must survive — it is the load-bearing connective tissue of the back half.**

**UNFAM-H5 — the foster-care paragraph's plain reasoning.** _"She could not stop the system from
moving anybody, lock a door, or ask a grown-up a question that produced a useful answer. What she
could do was get out of bed, walk down a hallway, and put her own body in the room... Verification
was the only power on offer."_ No jargon, no diagnosis, no pity. This is where an uninformed reader
decides whether to trust the article's psychology, and it earns it. **Must survive.**

**UNFAM-H6 — the critics are given their case before it is answered.** _"Give them their strongest
version, because they had one. An Olympic team is four other women who trained five years, and one
was going home without a medal because of a decision Biles made in a hallway."_ I had no prior
opinion to defend, so what I was watching for was whether the article was arguing or selling. This
paragraph is why I decided it was arguing. **Must survive, in this position — before the rebuttal.**

**UNFAM-H7 — the TL;DR accordion.** Five bullets that gave me the whole argument before I committed
to 4,500 words. As a reader who did not yet know whether I cared, this is what bought the article

**UNFAM-H8 — the Rabbit Hole is marked optional.** _"For the Enneagram nerds. The rest of the

## Red flags

### Blockers

**UNFAM-B1 — A quotation is altered inside the quotation marks, in the direction that strengthens
the article's argument.**

- **Location:** H2 8 ("What Simone Biles won at the Paris Olympics..."), the paragraph beginning
  _"Her account of what had changed is not about her legs."_
- **Draft text:** `"There was always a part of me that thought what if? But that was obviously my
anxiety talking."`
- **Published wording** (evidence packet, "Paris 2024" first-person section; source S-16,
  Olympics.com, marked `verified fact`): `"There was always a part of me that thought, 'What if?'
And I think that was my anxiety talking."` The packet marks the draft's wording `disputed claim`
  and states that the substitution "reverses the epistemic register of the sentence inside
  quotation marks."
- **Reader effect / trust problem:** I cannot check anything in this article. Quotation marks are
  the only instrument I have for telling reporting from interpretation, and the article leans on
  them more than most — it is built almost entirely out of quoted sentences. Converting her hedge
  ("I think") into certainty ("obviously") makes her sound like she has closed a question the rest
  of the article insists she has never closed. If a reader ever discovers one altered quote, every
  other quote becomes unreliable retroactively, including the ones doing the heaviest lifting
  ("Did I do it?", "Are we sure?").
- **Minimum viable repair:** restore verbatim, including "And I think." If the sentence rhythm
  suffers, cut around it rather than inside it.
- **Expected benefit:** removes the only confirmed instance of the article putting words in her
  mouth, and costs nothing argumentatively — the hedged version is arguably better evidence for a
  Type 6 reading than the confident one.
- **Confidence:** high. The packet marks source wording as verified and draft wording as disputed.
- **Acceptance test:** the string between the quotation marks matches S-16's published wording
  character for character; any removal is marked with an ellipsis.

**UNFAM-B2 — A direct quotation is attributed to a named source that the pipeline could not
locate.**

- **Location:** H2 8, the therapy-appointment paragraph: _Her framing in_ Simone Biles Rising: `"It
wasn't at a point of weakness, it was a point of strength."`
- **Evidence:** the packet's "Disputes and unresolved questions" item 1 calls this "the largest
  unresolved gap in the packet" — "The event is documented; the sentence in quotation marks is not,
  and it has no trail in the repository research file." Source ledger S-15 states explicitly that
  the cited outlet "**Does not carry the draft's exact sentence**," and the research-limitations
  section confirms the docuseries was never audited.
- **Reader effect / trust problem:** identical to B1 and arguably worse, because the attribution is
  specific enough to look verified. A quoted sentence with a named source is the strongest trust
  signal this article offers a reader who knows nothing; spending it on an unsourced sentence is
  the one move that cannot be defended if it is ever checked.
- **Minimum viable repair:** either (a) locate the sentence in _Simone Biles Rising_ Part 1/2 and
  cite it with a timestamp, or (b) drop the quotation marks and paraphrase the documented substance
  — that she had a therapy appointment the morning of the all-around final and framed it as
  strength rather than weakness, which S-15 does support. Option (b) needs no new research, so this
  blocker does not force a research hold.
- **Expected benefit:** the article stops asserting a verbatim sentence it cannot produce, while
  keeping the beat, which is a good one.
- **Confidence:** high, on the pipeline's own packet.
- **Acceptance test:** every sentence inside quotation marks in the draft maps to a source ledger
  entry that carries that exact wording; the "point of strength" line either gains a locatable
  citation or loses its quotation marks.

### Concerns

**UNFAM-C1 — Larry Nassar is used twice and never identified. (Highest priority.)**

- **Location:** H2 6, first sentence (_"In January 2018 she disclosed that she was one of Larry
  Nassar's survivors"_) and the Senate quote paragraph.
- **Reader effect:** this is the only point in the article where I stopped reading and searched. The
  section's argument is that she stayed inside _the institution that failed her_, which requires me
  to know that Nassar operated inside USA Gymnastics. I can infer it from _"an entire system that
  enabled and perpetrated his abuse"_ plus the naming of USA Gymnastics — but inferring is not the
  same as being told, and the scale (which is what makes staying remarkable) is never conveyed at
  all. Every other weight-bearing name in the draft gets a gloss; this one does not.
- **Evidence:** confirmed by grep that the body contains no descriptor for Nassar. The packet's
  timeline also does not identify him, so the gap is upstream of the draft. Role and conviction
  confirmed by search: USA Gymnastics national team medical coordinator from 1996; sentenced 2017–18
  to 60 years federal plus 40–175 years in Michigan; a Michigan judge put known victims at 265, with
  500+ later filing claims against his former employers.
- **Minimum viable repair:** an appositive on first mention — "Larry Nassar, the USA Gymnastics
  national team doctor later convicted of sexually abusing hundreds of athletes." Keep it flat and
  legally precise; do not editorialise.
- **Expected benefit:** H2 6 goes from inferable to legible, and its argument gains the weight it
  is currently assuming.
- **Confidence:** high.
- **Acceptance test:** a reader who has never heard the name can, by the end of H2 6's first
  paragraph, state who Nassar was and why USA Gymnastics is implicated in his conduct.

**UNFAM-C2 — "five named skills" is unglossed jargon inside the orientation paragraph.**

- **Expected benefit:** the credential that most distinguishes her actually registers.
- **Confidence:** high.
- **Acceptance test:** a reader can say what a "named skill" is at first encounter, without

**UNFAM-C3 — The two-up two-count mechanism is asserted, not derivable, and it is the hinge of the
article's central rebuttal.**

- **Evidence:** packet CLM-09 confirms "Two-up two-count is the correct Olympic team-final format"
  and rates the passage "rule verified, motive attributed," low risk _as written_ — i.e. the packet
  checked accuracy, not comprehensibility. The comprehension gap is mine to report.
- **Minimum viable repair:** one clause stating the actual mechanism that connects withdrawal to
  substitution eligibility. The editor should source this from the FIG/Olympic team-final rules
  rather than from my reconstruction, and must preserve the draft's existing and correct hedge

- **Expected benefit:** the rebuttal becomes something the reader sees rather than something the
  reader is told.
- **Confidence:** high on the gap; medium on the repair's exact wording, which needs a rules source.
- **Acceptance test:** a reader who has never watched gymnastics can restate in their own words why
