In a Diary of a CEO episode released on September 24, 2026, host Steven Bartlett asked Druski what he got out of making people laugh. The answer went back to elementary school. After class he would ask himself, "did I make the class laugh today?" Then he took the question to the other kids: "who's the funniest to you in this class?" When the answer wasn't him, he pushed. "Like, why is Mikey funnier than me? Tell me why." ([The Diary of a CEO, September 24, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UhzI1fg8rCA))

"It was almost like a competition in my head that nobody knew about, but it was in me," he said. Being named the funniest "made me feel successful. It felt like I won the Mark Twain Award of the school." He had described the same contest to GQ six years earlier: "Some kids would be like, yo I wanna be best dressed or I wanna be the best athlete. I used to literally say in my mind in elementary school, 'I wanna be the funniest this year, I wanna be the funniest kid in the class.'"

That boy, Drew Desbordes, grew up to be Druski, the sketch comedian who plays the boss of a fake label called Coulda Been Records. By 2025 he was paying for his shows through his own company, 4Lifers Entertainment; in 2026 he hosted the BET Awards and made [TIME's TIME100 Creators list](https://time.com/collection/time100-creators/2026/drew-desbordes/). The public version looks like a man who doesn't care what anyone thinks: a whiteface NASCAR fan, a parody widely read as Erika Kirk, a post asking "AM I CANCELLED?" That surface is why the obvious Enneagram read is Type 8. The kid running a private poll points somewhere else. He cared about the verdict, and he wanted to win it.

## What is Druski's personality type?

Druski most likely fits Enneagram Type 3, the Achiever, at medium confidence. In interviews six years apart he describes the same drive: to be ranked the funniest, to be "number one," and not to see himself as "a failure." Type 8, the boss he plays on camera, is the strongest alternative.

[Type 3](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-3) is organized around being seen as successful and worth something. Ambition alone can't show that, since every successful comedian has it. The better test is what Druski protects when the verdict turns against him. An [Eight](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-8) guards against being controlled, and critics usually get a counterpunch. From 2022 to 2026, Druski rarely argued back. He repaired when the hurt landed inside his own room or audience, stayed quiet toward critics outside it, and answered a factual accusation with facts. Even his sharpest reply, "Never needed no PR," [seemingly aimed](https://www.complex.com/pop-culture/a/treyalston/jack-harlow-druski-nutritionist) in 2024 at a rapper who said he paid for publicity, defended his standing.

That test is contested. An Eight can ignore critics beneath notice, lawyers and brands give anyone reasons to stay quiet, and turning backlash into reach is also what a [Type 7](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-7) does. His lightness sounds like a Seven's, too ("I don't care for none of that serious stuff," he told Bartlett, answering a question about brand safety). So the call rests mainly on what he says he is chasing. A Seven chases options and avoids limits; Druski ranks himself against others and names failure as the outcome he refused to accept. That holds in sources years apart: the funniest-kid contest he described to GQ in 2020, the rival he told Billboard in December 2025 he wanted to "outdo," and the "number one" goal he gave Bartlett in 2026.

## A house where the answer was never correct

He grew up in Gwinnett County, Georgia. His father flew for United Airlines and his mother worked for the CDC, he told Bartlett, and he sums up the household in one line: "We were a broken home. We weren't a broke home."

His mother gave the comedy its first stage. "She would put on these camcorders to let me be free," he said. [Rolling Stone reported in 2024](https://ca.rollingstone.com/culture/how-druski-learned-to-stop-being-a-bitch-and-embrace-his-comedic-voice/) that she sat him in front of the family video camera to hone his imitations.

In the same interview he described violence at home. His father, he said, "had great lessons, but his teaching skills was very tough and physical." He describes watching his brother beaten for wetting the bed, "not the normal belt type thing," and seeing "the choking and, you know, pushing her out the door and then locking the door" when his parents fought, with his father telling him, "you better not open that door," while he stood "in between a rock and a hard place." He says some of those memories are blurred. He also credits his father, who grew up in the "project buildings" of Northeast DC: "The lessons he had were very good." The episode came out on September 24, 2026, and this page was checked the same day, too soon for his father, a private person, to have responded publicly.

What he did with it, by his account, was try to lighten things. "I would always try to step in either after or during these moments to try to be like a coping mechanism for him," he said of his brother. He would be "the funny guy, maybe at the dinner after," trying to "lift everybody's vibe up." He is careful about cause: "I wouldn't say that my comedy necessarily came from that, but it definitely came from like a healing, wanting to make our situation better at home."

The house had a second rule. He would come home with a B, and his mother would say, "Oh, you could have done better." His father could turn a division problem into 40 minutes of alternatives. "It was almost never a correct answer in my household," he said. He is fair to her about it ("Not like it's not enough, but it's like you always can do better"), and he connects it to a working life with no off switch. Calling into Tom Segura's comedy podcast [2 Bears, 1 Cave](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oojm3YT7sys) in January 2025, his mother credited his "discipline" and "resilience."

He has since forgiven his father "in my head and also to him face to face," and calls their relationship "Very, very good." Even the forgiveness is framed as forward motion: "I cannot look for more success and look for the light at the end of the tunnel without forgiving any and everything in my past."

None of this settles his type, and this page doesn't claim the house made him a Three. A child who tries to comfort a sibling after a beating could become a Two, a Six or a Nine. He says it taught him to "read a room and sense any type of danger," which sounds as much like a Six's vigilance as anything. The Three reading takes something narrower from his childhood: a boy asking classmates to rank him, in a home where "you could have done better" was the standing answer.

## The semester he disappeared

After Georgia Gwinnett College he transferred to Georgia Southern, in Statesboro, and later dropped out. By his 2026 account, two guys he knew from his home county kept him at arm's length, needling him about having no car, no money for the game and the same shoes he played basketball in. "I felt like I wasn't deserving enough to be around them," he said. He stopped going to class for the whole second semester.

"I don't think I've been that depressed in my life, other than the semester right before I left," he told [Complex in 2021](https://www.complex.com/pop-culture/a/cmplxjewel/druski-profile-2021).

Lonely transfer students get depressed, so the depression proves nothing. How he tells it is the clue. The hurt is about being judged not worth bringing along, and about losing a role: at home and in high school he had been "the guy that brings the warmth to the room." Even the career he had picked was about visibility. "I took sports journalism to be maybe an ESPN sports cast just to get my face on TV."

The way out, as he tells it, came from researching "successful people" on YouTube and noticing that everyone he admired, from Steve Harvey to <a href="/personality-analysis/kevin-hart">Kevin Hart</a> and Jim Carrey, was a comedian.

## "Or die trying"

He left college around 21 or 22. His mother was disappointed, and his grandmother, GQ reported, threatened to cut him off completely. "I was hurt by that," he told GQ. "I was like watch, I'ma show y'all. I'ma prove it to y'all."

He made his mother a promise that he now calls "a little far." He would be a famous comedian on TV within a year, he told her, and if he wasn't, he would kill himself. She told him not to say things like that. Asked on Diary of a CEO what he had meant, he said, "I was chasing being great," and that he would otherwise "perceive myself as a failure." He added, "I knew I wasn't going to kill myself."

Over roughly the next year back in Atlanta, he says, he worked restaurant jobs and then fell into a "scamming circle" doing "quick, easy scams with banks or doing card scams," while only flirting with comedy. "I never was a bad guy," he said, but "the karma of this stuff was weighing on me." Complex had reported in 2021 that he did unspecified "illegal things" for money. The turn came when he and a friend were robbed at gunpoint in "a bad scamming situation gone left." "That was the last moment I said, okay, if I'm going to do anything, I'm going to become a comedian or die trying."

At 23 he wrote his goals on a sheet of paper, because his mother always said, "You can't do anything without writing things down." The first line: "No plan B, your plan A will work." When Bartlett asked whether hosting a tour, hosting an awards show and starring in a major movie were on the list, he said yes to all three. Filming sketches around his mother's house, he gave her follower counts. Had he made any money? "No but I have people watching."

None of this separates the types on its own: an Eight could aim a vow at doubters, the list items were Bartlett's prompts, and follower counts are every creator's first metric. The telling part is the stake he volunteered: whether he would see himself as a failure.

## The boss is a character he built

The followers came from characters. He posted his first sketch in October 2017, GQ reported, and [TIME](https://time.com/collection/time100-creators/2026/drew-desbordes/) credits an early frat-bro character, Kyle Rogger, with catching the attention of <a href="/personality-analysis/drake">Drake</a>, Lil Yachty and Jack Harlow. By 2019 he had a wider audience through Coulda Been Records, an American Idol parody in which he plays a label head, "a punishing combination of Simon Cowell and Puff Daddy who evaluates the talents of his fans," as GQ put it in 2020.

The boss act predates the money. On the Nike campus where Drake shot the "Laugh Now Cry Later" video, released in August 2020, Druski improvised his cameo and spent the day "walking around talking to people like I was the CEO," he told GQ. Afterward, he told Bartlett, "brands started to take me seriously" and "started to see me as an actual star."

Since 2025, movie stars such as <a href="/personality-analysis/timothee-chalamet">Timothée Chalamet</a> have co-hosted his audition videos. When Druski asked Mark Wahlberg, "You from the hood?" in October 2025, Wahlberg rose from his seat in mock threat, and the boss folded ("My bad") and made his own security the punchline ([Complex](https://www.complex.com/pop-culture/a/treyalston/mark-wahlberg-druski-hood)). Inside his own format, that is the joke, not evidence of temperament.

Now the Type 8 case at full strength. "We don't need anybody to tell us we can be on that TV show, or to kiss up to somebody to try to get in that door," he told Rolling Stone in 2024. "We make sure when we sign these deals with any of these big companies that my team is involved in the creative side," he said, adding that his writers are people "I grew up with." As of September 2026 he had not retracted or apologized for his whiteface sketches. And he says closeness makes him pull back: "once I get too close, I can't trust it." If the evidence stopped here, Eight would be a fair call.

## Paying for it himself to "show them"

"We tried to get Netflix, we tried to get Amazon, we tried to get Hulu," he told [Essence in 2025](https://www.essence.com/of-the-essence/the-business-of-being-druski/). "Everything was a no, back to back to back to back to back." On Diary of a CEO he added an FX show and an Amazon movie that fell through. So, he said, "instead of going and buying a car or another house or maybe a nice jewelry and all this stuff, I'm going to invest in myself."

It paid. Essence tallied the first season of _Coulda Been House_ at 58 million views across eight episodes and _Coulda Been Love_ at 80 million across seven, as of October 2025. [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/profile/drew-desbordes-druski/) put his creator earnings at $10 million in 2023, $14 million in 2025 and $20 million on its 2026 Top Creators list.

This is the core of the Eight case, and part of it fits. He refused to go back and pitch again: "I'm going to show them not by coming back and doubling back." That is what an Eight does. But the aim he described next is about being seen: "I'm going to show them by doing it myself and having them wanting what I did in reverse. And maybe they don't want that. Maybe they'll see what I was capable of doing and then wanting to do other things." An Eight builds his own company so that nobody can control him. Druski describes building his so that the companies that said no would see what he could do. In the Rolling Stone interview, the sentence right after "kiss up to somebody" was: "I think it's just amazing that I can create my fan base."

About a minute earlier he had told Bartlett, "Now they want to purchase these shows that we've made and do more in the future." Billboard reported in December 2025 that Netflix had greenlit a series based on his airport sketches. Selling to a platform that once passed doesn't show he gave up control. Owning the shows is also plain good business for any creator of his era, so economics explain part of this. The motive shows in what he says the ownership was for.

## Whiteface, Erika Kirk and the good-intent defense

On August 31, 2025, he spent the Cook Out Southern 500 at Darlington Raceway in full whiteface, after about four hours in the makeup chair, with a mullet wig, overalls and patriotic tattoos. He posted the sketch on September 2 as "That Guy who is just Proud to be AMERICAN" and wrote "AM I CANCELLED?" on Instagram. Billboard's Frank DiGiacomo scored it two days later: "The acid test for comedians? Never apologize when they stir up controversy and outrage." Druski, [he wrote](https://www.billboard.com/culture/events/druski-whiteface-comedians-response-nascar-1236059168/), "just passed the test." <a href="/personality-analysis/theo-von">Theo Von</a> praised the sketch.

Three months later, Druski told Billboard, "it really was never any backlash. Everybody loved it," and "I always knew that skit would have that kind of reach." On [CBS Mornings](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zoQcSg4OrtE) in February 2026 he said the character wasn't new: he had "produced that character multiple times before I did White Face," and "the greats have done it," naming <a href="/personality-analysis/eddie-murphy">Eddie Murphy</a> and Martin Lawrence. The Nation later reported that the same makeup artist had done an earlier whiteface video of his in May 2025.

On March 25, 2026, he posted "How Conservative Women in America act." He played the woman in whiteface, and his character says America must protect "all white men in America" because "they are the ones who matter most," as [The Nation](https://www.thenation.com/article/society/druski-erika-kirk-video-analysis/) described it. The sketch never named Erika Kirk, but it was widely read as her. Her husband, Charlie Kirk, had been [shot and killed](https://time.com/7316095/charlie-kirk-uvu-shooting-turning-point-usa/) at a Utah Valley University event on September 10, 2025, about six months earlier, and she had become CEO of Turning Point USA. The Nation counted over 400 million views across platforms. Senator Ted Cruz called it "beneath contempt." Historian Clare Corbould defended it in [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/druskis-viral-whiteface-skit-isnt-racism-its-satire-that-punches-up-at-power-279460): "Blackface and whiteface are not opposite and equal. Blackface punches down." On April 1, 2026, Donald Trump told Kirk at a White House lunch, "Sue their ass off," without naming anyone ([Daily Beast](https://www.thedailybeast.com/trump-begs-erika-kirk-to-sue-comedian-druski-for-mocking-maga/)). Later that month, Kirk said on _The Charlie Kirk Show_, "I have comedians dressing up in whiteface," without naming him.

His direct response was a joking Instagram post asking whether he had gone "too far," later deleted. A representative denied a reported cease-and-desist. On Diary of a CEO, he said he never knew whether Trump meant him and "just left it alone." When Bartlett teased that he was hoping so, he said, "I'm not looking to get sued by nobody." In July 2026 he revived the NASCAR character's "remarkably transformative look," as [Complex](https://www.complex.com/pop-culture/a/tracewilliamcowen/druski-revives-proud-american-character) put it, for a Fourth of July clip.

His standing defense hasn't changed in years. "It's always coming from a good place," he told Rolling Stone in 2024. "I never go and punch down in any anything that I do. Nothing is coming from a bad place," he told CBS. To Bartlett: "I'm not a political guy." "I am just the guy here for laughs." His comedy, he said, has nothing to do with the "political side or the race side and all that stuff."

The sketches are the Eight's strongest evidence: a large public risk, repeated, revived after the backlash and never taken back. "AM I CANCELLED?" and the "too far" post are taunts, which fit an Eight. The defense is where the Eight reading weakens. Druski never argues with Cruz or Kirk, and never says his targets deserved it. His defense reads as image protection: a man guarding a picture of himself as successful and good-hearted. An Eight ignoring critics beneath notice would owe them no account of his intentions; Druski keeps giving one. Legal caution explains some of the quiet. Billboard's acid test names the other half: in comedy, never apologizing is also a business model.

It also skips the harder question: whether a sketch titled for a category, but widely read as one identifiable, recently widowed woman, still hits its target. Corbould's argument settles who holds power, not that.

## Apologies, silences and one fight

His apologies follow a loose pattern. In 2022 he took down "That Friend that tries to make the girls OVERLY drink," a sketch in which he played a man who pushes women to drink so he can assault them. Audiences were split, Rolling Stone reported: some praised it for calling out the behavior, others found joking about rape insensitive. "I didn't want to continue to trigger people," he told Rolling Stone. "I think I just played the character too well."

In November 2024, during a Thanksgiving bit on <a href="/personality-analysis/kai-cenat">Kai Cenat</a>'s Mafiathon 2 livestream, a boy playing Pugsley, described on the stream as nine, cried after Druski repeatedly called him "fat." Minutes later, on the same stream, Druski apologized: "I got a couple drinks... but that ain't no excuse bro," and "I really do apologize." The boy's mother [called the apology insincere](https://www.dexerto.com/twitch/child-actors-mom-accuses-druski-of-mentally-assaulting-her-son-on-kai-cenat-stream-2993679/) on TikTok. Atlanta Black Star reported that her videos also used racial slurs about him. No later statement from Druski to her had surfaced as of September 2026.

In late August 2026, former NFL safety T.J. Ward called CTE, the subject of a new Druski sketch, "a life-taking disease" ([Us Weekly](https://www.yahoo.com/entertainment/celebrity/articles/former-nfl-star-t-j-215624581.html)). No reply from Druski had been reported by September 24.

Set those beside what he told Bartlett: "you can never show me something that I've done that was like, oh, that's punching down." The stream is the obvious counterexample. One reading of the pattern: he repairs when the hurt lands inside his own room or audience, and stays quiet toward critics outside it. The crying kid, viewers who said a sketch triggered them, and Jaxon Smith-Njigba, whose name he mangled at the February 2026 NFL Honors (he reached out, and "I didn't get no response back," he told CBS), got repairs. Cruz, the TikTok mother and a retired safety got silence. The rule is fitted after the fact, and the silences have plainer explanations: slurs, legal exposure, brand caution. An Eight would more likely have taken on Cruz. He hasn't.

When the charge was a matter of fact, he fought. After a March 2025 amended complaint added him to Ashley Parham's sexual assault lawsuit against Sean "Diddy" Combs, he called the allegation "[a fabricated lie](https://www.foxla.com/news/druski-obj-diddy-lawsuit)" within days. In August 2025, Judge Rita F. Lin denied his motion for sanctions but wrote that the allegations against him "[almost certainly lack a factual basis](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.437874/gov.uscourts.cand.437874.54.0_1.pdf)," citing phone and bank records that placed him in Georgia. In December 2025 the case was dismissed without prejudice for failure to prosecute, not decided on the merits. He called it "this frivolous lawsuit." The sanctions motion was a real counterpunch, the clearest exception to his quiet, but anyone would fight a sexual-assault accusation that records contradict.

## "I'm thinking about my image"

On Diary of a CEO, Druski describes his choices in terms of image, rank and reinvention. On turning down paid cameos in weak music videos on the way up: "I go grab this, but I'm thinking about my image." On deciding to become "a healthy, heavyset guy": "I understand what my image is." Brands, he said, "want to know that you are a good person to be around."

On rank: "No, I really want to be number one. I'm not really falling victim to being okay with being cool with number two or three." Ricky Bobby's "if you ain't first, you're last," from _Talladega Nights_, is a family joke, he said, that he took "seriously because that's really how I live my life." His 30-year goal is "to be the number one comedian of my generation and be the face of comedy for the next 30 years." Nine months earlier, [Billboard](https://www.billboard.com/music/features/druski-number-ones-billboard-cover-story-interview-1236132075/) had asked what makes a star. Part of his answer: "How can I outdo the guy who's somewhere in the world trying to outdo me?"

On rest: "I don't do any vacationing." He finds it "soothing to be in a chaos situation of work." Hosting the BET Awards was "something I always want to do in my life," and the next day he was on a plane. Does he feel fulfilled? "No, not yet." Happy? "I'm very happy."

The more successful he got, he said, the more he wondered whether a partner might be "in love with just Druski and not Drew at all," someone who never saw "the real side of who I actually am." He calls it "a question in every superstar's mind," answering Bartlett's suggestion that it was "a convenient excuse." That worry comes with fame and proves little, and the distrust fits an Eight or a Six as well. What fits a Three is that he wants the persona rebuilt. Asked, in a question left by a previous guest, who he would most want to be like in his next life, he named Michael Jackson: "He achieved real greatness." "He changed his persona." "He created a whole new entity, a whole new person."

## What the Type 3 reading leaves unexplained

The biggest gap is still the whiteface. Playing white characters is an old, well-rewarded bit for him, so the willingness needs no explaining. The escalation does: full makeup, a sketch widely read as one recently widowed woman, and a July 2026 return to the look after she appeared to call it out on air. Maybe views are his scoreboard, but that is a guess; an Eight who refuses to be cowed explains the return more directly.

The self-roast is another gap. "Part of his comedy is he points out his flaws," his friend Jack Harlow said in [their 2021 Complex cover story](https://stories.complex.com/jack-harlow-druski-interview-2021-cover-story/10/). "He makes himself look stupid." When his car was repossessed, he filmed it: "I actually made that like a content piece." That is not what image management usually looks like. Maybe laughing at himself first keeps him in control of the verdict, but that is a guess; it may be the openness Harlow sees.

The reading also leaves out the scamming period, when a shortcut beat the good-guy image he defends now, though he describes that year with regret.

The Seven reading keeps some ground, too. "People ask me all these deep questions," he told Bartlett, in the same answer about brand safety. "I'm like, yo, I don't look at anything like that." Much of his image management also has a plain professional explanation: he sells to brands. The Three reading is the best fit for what he says he wants. It is not a full account of the man.

## "I've seen it before"

On June 28, 2026, he hosted the BET Awards, then the youngest host in the show's history, checking off a goal from the list he wrote at 23. That night he also debuted "JOE," a [parody](https://consequence.net/2026/06/druski-joe-jackson-michael-biopic-parody-sketch/) built around the Michael Jackson biopic, playing Joe Jackson in heavy makeup.

On Diary of a CEO, Bartlett played him the moment when Joe threatens to take off his belt: "Fix your face, Michael, before I fix it for you." "Yeah, that was some aspects of Joe Jackson that my dad had," Druski said. "Some personality." Then: "It wasn't hard to rip that belt off, man. I've seen it before."

The boy who tried to cheer his brother up after the beatings now plays the man holding the belt, as a bit.

Bartlett asked him to picture the next five years as already done. "I became a superstar," Druski said. Then he described who would be gone: "You don't see the Druski that you once saw, the social media guy."
