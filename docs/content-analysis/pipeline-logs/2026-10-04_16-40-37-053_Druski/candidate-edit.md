In a Diary of a CEO episode released on September 24, 2026, host Steven Bartlett asked Druski what he got out of making people laugh. The answer went back to elementary school. After class he would ask himself, "did I make the class laugh today?" Then he took the question to the other kids: "who's the funniest to you in this class?" When the answer wasn't him, he pushed. "Like, why is Mikey funnier than me? Tell me why." ([The Diary of a CEO, September 24, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UhzI1fg8rCA))

"It was almost like a competition in my head that nobody knew about, but it was in me," he said. Being named the funniest "made me feel successful. It felt like I won the Mark Twain Award of the school." He had described the same contest to [GQ](https://www.gq.com/story/druski-hip-hop-favorite-comedian-drake-jack-harlow-odell-beckham-jr) six years earlier: "Some kids would be like, yo I wanna be best dressed or I wanna be the best athlete. I used to literally say in my mind in elementary school, 'I wanna be the funniest this year, I wanna be the funniest kid in the class.'"

That boy, Drew Desbordes, grew up to be Druski, the sketch comedian who plays the boss of a fake label called Coulda Been Records. By 2025 he was paying for his shows through his own company, 4Lifers Entertainment; in 2026 he hosted the BET Awards and made [TIME's TIME100 Creators list](https://time.com/collection/time100-creators/2026/drew-desbordes/). The public version looks like a man who doesn't care what anyone thinks: a whiteface NASCAR fan, a parody widely read as Erika Kirk (Charlie Kirk's widow and successor at Turning Point USA), a post asking "AM I CANCELLED?" That surface is why the obvious Enneagram read is Type 8. The kid running a private poll points somewhere else. He cared about the verdict, and he wanted to win it.

## What is Druski's personality type?

Druski most likely fits Enneagram Type 3, the Achiever, at medium confidence. He runs on other people's verdict: as a boy he polled classmates on who was funniest, and before he made any money he counted followers. Type 8 is the strongest alternative: he self-funded his shows rather than re-pitch the studios that turned him down.

[Type 3](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-3) is organized around being seen as successful and worth something. Wanting to be first can't show that, since an [Eight](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-8) wants the top spot too. The difference is what the win is for. An Eight wants to be beyond anyone's control; a [Type 7](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-7) wants options and an exit from limits. Druski keeps asking to be ranked, watched and seen as capable.

The rank thread holds in sources years apart: the funniest-kid contest he described to GQ in 2020, the unnamed competitor he told Billboard in December 2025 he wanted to "outdo," and the "number one" goal he gave Bartlett in 2026. The failure stake is narrower: it comes from one answer on Diary of a CEO, taped during a promotional run, and "number one" alone could come from an Eight. What tips the call is verdict-seeking that predates that run: the classroom poll, the "I'ma prove it to y'all" he aimed at his family after dropping out, and his 2024 pride in building a fan base without the studios.

How he answers critics, taken up with the whiteface sketches below, is weaker evidence, because nearly all of it came in answer to interviewers.

## A house where the answer was never correct

He grew up in Gwinnett County, Georgia. His father was an airline pilot and his mother worked for the CDC, he told Bartlett: "We were a broken home. We weren't a broke home."

He was studying fame early. As a kid, [TIME reported in September 2026](https://time.com/article/2026/09/30/druski-time100-next/), he watched Entertainment Tonight and TMZ, "studying how paparazzi would follow stars around in the streets," and he likes to recall that people in Gwinnett called him a "local celebrity."

On Diary of a CEO he also described violence at home. His father, he said, "had great lessons, but his teaching skills was very tough and physical." He describes watching his brother beaten for wetting the bed, "not the normal belt type thing," and seeing "the choking and, you know, pushing her out the door and then locking the door" when his parents fought. He says some of those memories are blurred. He also credits his father, who grew up in the "project buildings" of Northeast DC: "The lessons he had were very good." The episode came out on September 24, 2026; a search on October 4, 2026, found no public response from his father, a private person.

What he did with it, by his account, was try to lighten things. "I would always try to step in either after or during these moments to try to be like a coping mechanism for him," he said of his brother. He is careful about cause: "I wouldn't say that my comedy necessarily came from that, but it definitely came from like a healing, wanting to make our situation better at home."

The house had a second rule. He would come home with a B, and his mother would say, "Oh, you could have done better." "It was almost never a correct answer in my household," he said. He is fair to her about it ("Not like it's not enough, but it's like you always can do better"), and he connects it to a working life with no off switch.

None of this settles his type, and this page doesn't claim the house made him a Three. A child who comforts a sibling after a beating could become a Two, a Six, a Seven or a Nine. He says the house taught him to "read a room and sense any type of danger" and "any type of happiness, sadness, anything," a Six's vigilance or a Two's attunement as easily as a Three's. Bartlett saw it live: after some 700 interviews, he said, Druski was one of the first guests to stop mid-answer and say, "You look like you had something to say there, Steve." The Three reading takes something narrower: a boy who studied how stars got followed, asked classmates to rank him, and lived in a home where "you could have done better" was the standing answer.

He has since forgiven his father "in my head and also to him face to face," and calls their relationship "Very, very good." Even the forgiveness is framed as forward motion: "I cannot look for more success and look for the light at the end of the tunnel without forgiving any and everything in my past."

## The semester he disappeared

After Georgia Gwinnett College he transferred to Georgia Southern, in Statesboro, and later dropped out. By his 2026 account, two acquaintances from his home county kept him at arm's length and mocked him for being broke. "I felt like I wasn't deserving enough to be around them," he said. He stopped going to class for the whole second semester. "I don't think I've been that depressed in my life, other than the semester right before I left," he told [Complex in 2021](https://www.complex.com/pop-culture/a/cmplxjewel/druski-profile-2021).

Lonely transfer students get depressed, so the depression proves nothing. How he tells it is the clue: the hurt is about being judged not worth bringing along, and about losing a role. At home and in high school he had been "the guy that brings the warmth to the room." Even the career he had picked was about visibility. "I took sports journalism to be maybe an ESPN sports cast just to get my face on TV." The way out, as he tells it, came from researching "successful people" on YouTube and noticing that everyone he admired, from Steve Harvey to <a href="/personality-analysis/kevin-hart">Kevin Hart</a> and Jim Carrey, was a comedian.

## "Or die trying"

He left college around 21 or 22. His mother was disappointed, and his grandmother, GQ reported, threatened to cut him off completely. "I was hurt by that," he told GQ. "I was like watch, I'ma show y'all. I'ma prove it to y'all."

He made his mother a promise that he now calls "a little far." He would be a famous comedian on TV within a year, he told her, and if he wasn't, he would kill himself. She told him not to say things like that. Asked on Diary of a CEO what he had meant, he said, "I was chasing being great," and that he would otherwise "perceive myself as a failure." He added, "I knew I wasn't going to kill myself." "Great" was Bartlett's word, from his recap of the vow; "failure" was Druski's.

The year passed with no TV and no sketches; "I didn't do it immediately," he said. He worked restaurant jobs in Atlanta, then fell into a "scamming circle" doing "quick, easy scams with banks or doing card scams." "I never was a bad guy," he said, but "the karma of this stuff was weighing on me." The turn came when he and a friend were robbed at gunpoint in "a bad scamming situation gone left." "That was the last moment I said, okay, if I'm going to do anything, I'm going to become a comedian or die trying."

At 23 he wrote his goals on a sheet of paper. The first line: "No plan B, your plan A will work." When Bartlett asked whether hosting a tour, hosting an awards show and starring in a major movie were on the list, he said yes to all three. An Eight could aim a vow at doubters too, and the list items were Bartlett's prompts. The telling part is the stake he volunteered: whether he would see himself as a failure.

Filming sketches around his mother's house, he gave her follower counts. Had he made any money? "No but I have people watching."

## The boss is a character he built

The followers came from characters. He posted his first sketch in October 2017, GQ reported. By 2019 Coulda Been Records, an American Idol parody in which he plays a label head, had widened his audience; GQ called the character "a punishing combination of Simon Cowell and Puff Daddy who evaluates the talents of his fans."

The boss act predates the money. On the Nike campus where <a href="/personality-analysis/drake">Drake</a> shot the "Laugh Now Cry Later" video, released in August 2020, Druski improvised his cameo and spent the day "walking around talking to people like I was the CEO," he told GQ.

The career itself had a mentor. Kevin Hart told him to "stop being a bitch" and put in the stand-up work, [Rolling Stone](https://ca.rollingstone.com/culture/how-druski-learned-to-stop-being-a-bitch-and-embrace-his-comedic-voice/) reported in 2024, and with Hart's counsel he took a 30-plus-stop tour on the road. On [Club Shay Shay](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E7J2R5zOfD8) in January 2023, he recalled Hart telling him to "go and do your own thing." Asked whether he had been afraid to, he said, per the episode's captions, "you could say that."

## Paying for it himself to "show them"

Here the Type 8 case is at full strength. "We tried to get Netflix, we tried to get Amazon, we tried to get Hulu," he told [Essence in 2025](https://www.essence.com/of-the-essence/the-business-of-being-druski/). "Everything was a no, back to back to back to back to back." So, he told Bartlett, "instead of going and buying a car or another house or maybe a nice jewelry and all this stuff, I'm going to invest in myself." The money went into YouTube series such as Coulda Been Love, which Essence counted at 80 million views as of October 2025. [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/profile/drew-desbordes-druski/) put his creator earnings at $10 million in 2023 and $20 million on its 2026 Top Creators list.

He would not go back and ask. "We don't need anybody to tell us we can be on that TV show, or to kiss up to somebody to try to get in that door," he told Rolling Stone in 2024, adding that in big-company deals "my team is involved in the creative side." In July 2025 he told [AfroTech](https://afrotech.com/druski-snipes) the shows' success "kind of proved to me that we can do this by ourselves... We don't need to have anybody else." Add the boss he plays, whiteface sketches he had not retracted as of October 2026, and a sanctions motion against the lawyers who sued him, and Eight is a fair call.

By September 2026 the companies were coming back. "Now they want to purchase these shows that we've made," he told Bartlett. [TIME reported](https://time.com/article/2026/09/30/druski-time100-next/) on September 30, 2026, that Netflix had "snapped up" Collect & Praise, a series built on his [January 2026 megachurch-pastor sketch](https://religionnews.com/2026/01/20/druskis-viral-video-skit-sparks-criticism-on-megachurch-culture/), and that Livestream From Hell, a film with Hart and Kai Cenat, was due on Prime Video at month's end.

His key sentence about the refusal supports both readings. "I'm going to show them not by coming back and doubling back," he told Bartlett. That is what an Eight does. Then: "I'm going to show them by doing it myself and having them wanting what I did in reverse. And maybe they don't want that. Maybe they'll see what I was capable of doing and then wanting to do other things." Reversing who wants whom is leverage, an Eight reading; being seen as capable is a Three's. An Eight builds his own company so that nobody can control him. Druski's July 2025 account sounded like that; in September 2026 he described building his so that the companies that said no would see what he could do.

A few details lean Three without settling it. In the Rolling Stone interview, the sentence right after "kiss up to somebody" was: "I think it's just amazing that I can create my fan base." And the independence had a coach, which sits oddly with an Eight's appetite for autonomy. Owning the shows is also plain good business for any creator of his era, so economics explain part of this. On ownership, the evidence is mixed.

## Whiteface, Erika Kirk and the good-intent defense

On August 31, 2025, he spent the Cook Out Southern 500 at Darlington Raceway in full whiteface, after about four hours in the makeup chair. He posted the sketch on September 2 as "That Guy who is just Proud to be AMERICAN" and wrote "AM I CANCELLED?" on Instagram. Two days later, [Billboard](https://www.billboard.com/culture/events/druski-whiteface-comedians-response-nascar-1236059168/) called never apologizing "the acid test for comedians" and said Druski "just passed the test."

In December 2025, Druski told Billboard, "it really was never any backlash. Everybody loved it," and "I always knew that skit would have that kind of reach." He had played a "white boy accepted by the Hood" in whiteface that May, [Complex reported](https://www.complex.com/pop-culture/a/cmplxtara-mahadevan/druski-white-guy-in-the-hood-skit), and on [CBS Mornings](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zoQcSg4OrtE) in February 2026 he said "the greats have done it," naming <a href="/personality-analysis/eddie-murphy">Eddie Murphy</a> and Martin Lawrence.

On March 25, 2026, he posted "How Conservative Women in America act," playing the woman in whiteface; his character says America must protect "all white men in America" because "they are the ones who matter most," as [The Nation](https://www.thenation.com/article/society/druski-erika-kirk-video-analysis/) described it. The sketch never named Erika Kirk, but it was widely read as her. Her husband had been [shot and killed](https://time.com/7316095/charlie-kirk-uvu-shooting-turning-point-usa/) at a Utah Valley University event on September 10, 2025, about six months earlier. Senator Ted Cruz called it "beneath contempt." Historian Clare Corbould defended it in [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/druskis-viral-whiteface-skit-isnt-racism-its-satire-that-punches-up-at-power-279460): "Blackface and whiteface are not opposite and equal. Blackface punches down." On April 1, 2026, Donald Trump told Kirk, "Sue their ass off," without naming anyone ([Daily Beast](https://www.thedailybeast.com/trump-begs-erika-kirk-to-sue-comedian-druski-for-mocking-maga/)).

His direct response was a joking Instagram post asking whether he had gone "too far," which [appears to have since been deleted](https://www.aol.com/comedian-druski-responds-erika-kirk-232200291.html). Of Trump, he told Bartlett: "I'm not looking to get sued by nobody." For the Fourth of July 2026 he posted a clip of the NASCAR character's "remarkably transformative look," which [Complex](https://www.complex.com/pop-culture/a/tracewilliamcowen/druski-revives-proud-american-character) said "would appear to stem from" a BET Awards promo shoot.

His defense, given to interviewers who asked, has held since 2024. "It's always coming from a good place," he told Rolling Stone that year. Asked on CBS whether there was a line he wouldn't cross, he said, "I think I never go and punch down in any anything that I do. Nothing is coming from a bad place," and that his work comes from "a good place... even if it does seem that I crossed the line or that something went too far." When Bartlett put the question as one of advertisers' "brand safety," Druski answered, "I am just the guy here for laughs." To TIME in late September 2026: "I'm not no politician. I'm just a funny guy."

The sketches are the Eight case's strongest evidence: a large public risk, repeated and, as of October 2026, never taken back, with taunts in place of apologies. His defense does little to offset them: each line answered an interviewer, and with brand partners watching, any comedian would talk about intent. Legal caution explains some of his quiet, and in comedy, as Billboard's acid test suggests, never apologizing is also a business model.

The defense also skips the question his critics kept raising: whether a sketch titled for a category, but widely read as one identifiable, recently widowed woman, still hits its target. Corbould's argument settles who holds power, not that. In The Nation, Fatima B. Jalloh took it on directly. "Erika Kirk is fair game for parody," they wrote: "While she is indeed a widow," she is also, in Jalloh's view, the public face of the politics the sketch mocks. Mocking her was "undeniably insensitive. But it's also undeniably funny."

## Apologies, silences and one fight

From 2022 to 2026, Druski rarely argued back. He repaired when the hurt landed inside his own room or audience, gave critics outside it no direct reply, and fought one factual accusation. The rule is fitted after the fact, and the silences have plainer explanations: slurs, legal exposure, brand caution.

The repairs first. In 2022 he took down "That Friend that tries to make the girls OVERLY drink," a sketch in which he played a man who pushes women to drink so he can assault them. Some viewers commended it for calling out the behavior and others found it insensitive, Rolling Stone reported. "I didn't want to continue to trigger people," he said. "I think I just played the character too well."

In November 2024, during a Thanksgiving bit on <a href="/personality-analysis/kai-cenat">Kai Cenat</a>'s Mafiathon 2, a month-long Twitch livestream, a boy playing Pugsley, whose mother said he was nine, cried after Druski repeatedly called him "fat." The unlabeled stream audio shows a messier sequence than insult-then-sorry: a voice that appears to be Druski's first pushed back at the room ("y'all want to have fat phobia when it come to the kid but not me"), and his first apology came after someone said, "he's a kid." Minutes later he apologized again: "I got a couple drinks... but that ain't no excuse bro," and "I really do apologize." Cenat later said Druski "knew he was wrong" and had apologized twice, [Dexerto reported](https://www.dexerto.com/twitch/child-actors-mom-accuses-druski-of-mentally-assaulting-her-son-on-kai-cenat-stream-2993679/).

Set those beside what he told Bartlett: "you can never show me something that I've done that was like, oh, that's punching down." The stream is the obvious counterexample.

Then the silences. Cruz and Kirk got no direct reply. The boy's mother rejected the apology as insincere in TikTok videos that, Atlanta Black Star reported, also used racial slurs about him; no later statement from Druski to her had surfaced as of October 4, 2026. On August 26, 2026, he posted a sketch about a football player whose concussion sends him spiraling. Former NFL safety T.J. Ward [called CTE](https://www.yahoo.com/entertainment/celebrity/articles/former-nfl-star-t-j-215624581.html) "a life-taking disease," and other players called the video insensitive. Druski's answer, to [TIME](https://time.com/article/2026/09/30/druski-time100-next/): "Comedy comes from real-life things, and sometimes putting up that mirror, some people can get sensitive to the subject matter." That cuts against this page's reading: no apology and a shrug at the people offended, though it names no critic and defends the material, not his ground.

When the charge was a matter of fact, he fought. A March 2025 amended complaint added him to Ashley Parham's sexual assault lawsuit against Sean "Diddy" Combs. He called the allegation "[a fabricated lie](https://www.foxla.com/news/druski-obj-diddy-lawsuit)" within days and later moved for sanctions against her lawyers. In August 2025 Judge Rita F. Lin wrote that the allegations against him "[almost certainly lack a factual basis](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.437874/gov.uscourts.cand.437874.54.0_1.pdf)," citing phone and bank records that placed him in Georgia. She declined to sanction the lawyers for naming him, finding they had a reasonable basis when they filed, but ordered them to show cause why they should not be sanctioned for pressing the claims after receiving his records, allegations she said "appear to have no reasonable factual basis." In December 2025 the case was dismissed without prejudice for failure to prosecute, not decided on the merits. The sanctions motion was, as of October 2026, the clearest counterpunch on record, but anyone would fight a sexual-assault accusation that records contradict.

## "I'm thinking about my image"

On the way up, he turned down paid cameos in music videos he thought were weak, "a quick $2,000" when he had little: "I go grab this, but I'm thinking about my image." "Image" is brand language any comedian who lives on brand deals uses, but the refusal cost money he needed.

On rank: "No, I really want to be number one. I'm not really falling victim to being okay with being cool with number two or three." An Eight could say that. What he said next sounds more like a Three: greatness meant being measured against Eddie Murphy, Kevin Hart, Kobe, Jordan and LeBron, and "just to be in that conversation and to be remembered and to leave a legacy, that's what matters." In December 2025, asked by [Billboard](https://www.billboard.com/music/features/druski-number-ones-billboard-cover-story-interview-1236132075/) what makes a star, he said there are "no days off" and asked, "How can I outdo the guy who's somewhere in the world trying to outdo me?" To TIME he pitched himself as "the modern-day Eddie Murphy or the modern-day Kevin Hart."

"I don't do any vacationing," he said. He finds it "soothing to be in a chaos situation of work and juggling multiple projects at once," as much a Seven's pleasure as a Three's. Does he feel fulfilled? "No, not yet." Happy? "I'm very happy."

The ambition includes the persona. Asked, in the show's closing tradition, a question left by the previous guest, who he would most want to be like in his next life, he named Michael Jackson: "He achieved real greatness." "He changed his persona." "He created a whole new entity, a whole new person."

## What the Type 3 reading leaves unexplained

The biggest gap is still the whiteface. Playing white characters is an old, well-rewarded bit for him, so the willingness needs no explaining. The escalation does: from a character to four hours of makeup in 2025, then to a sketch widely read as one woman, a widow who had succeeded her husband at the head of a political movement, in 2026. Maybe views are his scoreboard, but that is a guess; an Eight who refuses to be cowed explains the escalation more directly.

The self-roast is another gap. "He makes himself look stupid," his friend Jack Harlow said in [their 2021 Complex cover story](https://stories.complex.com/jack-harlow-druski-interview-2021-cover-story/12/). When his car was repossessed, he filmed it: "I actually made that like a content piece." That is not what image management usually looks like. Maybe laughing at himself first keeps him in control of the verdict, but that is a guess; it may be the openness Harlow sees.

So is the way he talks about his father's house. When people ask whether his past weighs on his career, his answer, he told Bartlett, is: "How could I be broken if I feel like I've already gone to my furthest extent?" That is a survivor's armor, and it sounds more like an Eight than a Three. He turns it toward climbing, though: "I can only continue to push myself to higher levels."

The reading also leaves out the scamming period, when a shortcut beat the good-guy image he defends now, though he describes that year with regret. And the Seven reading keeps some ground: answering the brand-safety question, he said, "I don't care for none of that serious stuff. People ask me all these deep questions. I'm like, yo, I don't look at anything like that." Much of his image management also has a plain professional explanation: he sells to brands, and his most quotable ambitions came while he had a series and two films to promote. The Three reading is the best fit for what he says he wants. It is not a full account of the man.

## "I've seen it before"

On June 28, 2026, he hosted the BET Awards, then the youngest host in the show's history, checking off a goal from the list he wrote at 23. The tour had come earlier; the movie, a supporting role as Emma Stone's security guard in Universal's [The Catch](https://www.complex.com/pop-culture/a/markelibert/druski-catch-emma-stone-chris-pine-universal), is scheduled for May 21, 2027. "Yeah, that was some aspects of Joe Jackson that my dad had," Druski said. "Some personality." Then: "It wasn't hard to rip that belt off, man. I've seen it before."
