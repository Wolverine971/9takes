On September 12, 2026, Dario Amodei published an essay asking the AI industry to slow down. Amodei co-founded Anthropic, the company behind the Claude models, and runs it as chief executive. The essay's most unusual commitment was about his own company, a step he said Anthropic was "unilaterally committing to (and calls on governments to require other frontier companies to match)." It would host embedded third-party evaluators with "Desks in our offices, access badges, and company laptops," and the right to publish findings "without editorial control by Anthropic," save a narrow right to redact sensitive material. He named the problem they would fix: "we are still the ones choosing what to include and omit." ([darioamodei.com, September 2026](https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier))

That is an odd offer from a chief executive whose company had [confidentially filed for an IPO](https://ca.finance.yahoo.com/news/ai-giant-anthropic-confidentially-files-160453569.html) that June, though it was also a credential for a company heading to market, and OpenAI's chief executive, Sam Altman, made a similar pledge that day ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/09/12/anthropic-ai-amodei-pacing)). For Amodei, it was a familiar move. For a decade, when the stakes have risen, he has gone looking for people he can trust and rules that can hold, including rules that bind him. When either one fails, he gets angry.

In January 2026 he wrote that "AI-enabled authoritarianism terrifies me" and that a national security official's report on powerful AI "would probably contain words like 'the single most serious national security threat we've faced in a century, possibly ever.'" ([The Adolescence of Technology](https://darioamodei.com/essay/the-adolescence-of-technology)) He will not stop building it, and the pacing essay says why: "Not building the technology deprives humanity of benefits or simply places AI in the hands of authoritarian powers, while building it too fast is reckless." This profile is about what he does with the fear that is left over.

## What is Dario Amodei's personality type?

Dario Amodei most likely fits Enneagram Type 6, the Loyalist, at medium confidence. When danger rises, he looks for people he can trust and commitments that bind everyone, himself included, and his sharpest public anger targets dishonesty and bad faith. Type 5, the Investigator, is the strongest alternative; Type 1 is a credible third.

[Type 6](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6) is organized around security: something solid to stand on when the ground might move. [Type 5](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-5) seeks security through mastery: understanding enough, and needing little enough, to stand alone. Both run worst-case scenarios, and either can design safeguards, so worry, intellect and institutions can't separate them. What separates them is what each leans on when the ground moves: a Five on its own understanding and a guarded store of time and energy, a Six on people and promises it has tested.

Amodei's tell is personal. By his own account, he left OpenAI after Altman kept agreeing with his concerns and nothing changed, and he took people he trusted with him. By the Wall Street Journal's anonymously sourced account, he had earlier stayed on the strength of Altman's promise. In Enneagram theory, a Five with Six leanings builds alliances too, but as tools; it rarely stakes its own staying on another person's word. How he handles stress in private is not in the record reviewed here. Until this October 2026 revision, this page typed him as a Five.

## A cure that came "a few years" late

Amodei was born in San Francisco in 1983 and grew up in the Mission District. His parents, Elena Engel, a Chicago-born library project manager, and Riccardo Amodei, a leather craftsman from "a tiny town near the island of Elba," had moved there from Italy in the 1970s. Daniela, his sister and future co-founder, was born four years after him. ([Wired, March 28, 2025](https://www.wired.com/story/anthropic-benevolent-artificial-intelligence/))

As a child, he told Wired, he was "just obsessed with manipulating mathematical objects and understanding the world quantitatively." His parents "gave me a sense of right and wrong and what was important in the world," he told Alex Kantrowitz of Big Technology, "imbuing a strong sense of responsibility." ([Big Technology, July 29, 2025](https://www.bigtechnology.com/p/the-making-of-dario-amodei))

His father had been sick for years and died in 2006, around the time Dario started graduate school at Princeton wanting to be a theoretical physicist. The loss pulled him toward biology, and later toward AI, which "felt to me like the only technology that could kind of bridge that gap." "Maybe three or four years after he died," Amodei said, the cure rate for his father's disease rose from 50% to roughly 95%. ([Big Technology Podcast, July 30, 2025](https://pod.wave.co/podcast/big-technology-podcast/anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-ais-potential-openai-rivalry-genai-business-doomerism-451c32cc)) "There was someone who worked on the cure to this disease, that managed to cure it, and save a bunch of people's lives," he told Kantrowitz, "but could have saved even more." Jade Wang, who dated him in the early 2010s, told Big Technology what the timing meant: "It's the difference between his father most likely dying and most likely living, okay?"

The 2025 profiles left the disease unnamed. In August 2026, replying on X to [an investor](https://x.com/GavinSBaker/status/2088611616577253502) who had urged him to sound more positive before Anthropic went public, he named it: "I lost my father to Hepatitis C only a few years before the development of direct-acting antivirals (sofosbuvir), which cure 95% of patients and probably would have cured him." ([X, August 15, 2026](https://x.com/DarioAmodei/status/2088758819304443967)) The post called public distrust of AI "fundamentally a crisis of trust" and pledged, "you have my word on that." A month later the pacing essay added a second fact, under the line "I feel the urgency personally": "I myself survived an early-stage cancer that would not have been treatable even fifty years ago." Both disclosures arrived as evidence inside arguments. A Five reading would stress that neither appeared in the long 2025 profiles; a Six reading, the word he gave alongside one. Both readings fit, so this page does not lean on them.

The lesson of his father's death is that delay has a body count. By his own account it is why he keeps building, though not his only reason; the pacing essay adds keeping AI from authoritarian powers. It does not explain what he does with the danger of the thing he builds, and that part of him shows up early.

## "He would say it"

At Princeton he moved from cosmology into Michael Berry's retina lab, where he co-invented a better sensor for recording retinal signals. His doctorate in biophysics won the Hertz Thesis Prize. Berry remembers a student who was "very idealistic...in a technical or scientific sense" ([AFP via France 24, October 2, 2026](https://www.france24.com/en/live-news/20261002-anthropic-s-dario-amodei-ai-s-most-visible-ceo-and-enigma)). A lab colleague, Olivier Marre, gave AFP a cooler picture in 2026: "I never saw someone engaged in a strong moral or ethical quest."

After a Stanford postdoc, he moved into deep learning at Baidu, where he "saw these very smooth trends": models improving predictably as they grew and took in more data and computing power, the pattern later called scaling laws. Greg Diamos, who hired him, told AFP he "didn't have a problem confronting much more experienced researchers": "If he would disagree with ...the direction of the company, he would say it." Eventually "the disagreement got to be so big that he felt like it was not worth continuing." Kantrowitz, who also interviewed Diamos, adds that the lab was coming apart in turf battles.

After ten months at Google Brain, where he co-wrote a paper on AI's potential for bad behavior, he joined OpenAI in 2016. He became a research director, helped pioneer reinforcement learning from human feedback, a way of steering models toward answers people prefer, and led GPT-3 with "50-60% of the entire company's compute," Kantrowitz reports. The man who warns about scaling did much of the scaling. Colleagues split on him: his long documents about values and technology struck some as inspirational and others as "flag planting and heavy handed," and he "could be a bit abrasive, sometimes disparaging projects he didn't believe in."

At Baidu and at OpenAI the sequence is the same: say the disagreement out loud, push from inside, and leave when trust in the direction runs out.

## Staying on conditions

His first reaction to a powerful idea tends to be a threat. Of the scaling theory he wrote up at OpenAI, "My first thought," he told Wired, "was, oh my God, could systems that are smarter than humans figure out how to destabilize the nuclear deterrent?"

The Journal's account of his decade-long feud with Altman, drawn largely from unnamed current and former employees, shows that reflex reaching for institutions. In early 2016, as the Journal tells it from OpenAI co-founder Greg Brockman's side, Amodei argued that on sensitive topics like how fast AI was developing, "it would be better to tell the government first." Some OpenAI colleagues, Kantrowitz reports, found him too focused on "collaborating with the government to address" the technology's potential. In 2017 he hired a policy adviser who pitched OpenAI as a coordinator among AI companies and the U.S. government, toward "an international coordination regime for advanced AI." ([WSJ via To Vima, March 28, 2026](https://www.tovima.com/wsj/the-decadelong-feud-shaping-the-future-of-ai/))

He stayed, on conditions. In 2018, the Journal reported, "Dario agreed to stay so long as Altman promised that Brockman and Sutskever wouldn't be in charge," then learned of a promise he felt conflicted with that deal. (Ilya Sutskever was OpenAI's chief scientist.) His own account, given to Wired, has the same shape. As safety issues grew more serious, he said, "the company started moving in the opposite direction." He took his concerns to Altman, who, he says, would listen carefully and agree. Then nothing would change.

The Journal reads those years less kindly, citing "personal wounds that have never healed, including jockeying over more classic corporate issues such as power and credit." In one fight it describes, he kept Brockman off the new GPT language project, telling a staff meeting, among other reasons, that its lead researcher, Alec Radford, didn't want to work with him; Radford felt his preferences were being used "in a proxy war between senior executives." The conditions may have been about turf as much as trust. Either way they kept one form: guarantees from people, enforced by leaving.

In late 2020, per the Journal, he "would accept nothing less than reporting directly to the board." He left instead, at the end of 2020, with six other OpenAI employees, including Daniela; within a few weeks, Wired reported, a dozen more followed.

## People he trusts

Asked by Lex Fridman in 2024 why he left OpenAI, he called it "incredibly unproductive to try and argue with someone else's vision" and declined to judge anyone: "You might think they're not doing it the right way. You might think they're dishonest. Who knows? Maybe you're right, maybe you're not. But what you should do is you should take some people you trust and you should go off together and you should make your vision happen." If the vision is compelling, he added, "people will copy it." The right way to build, he said, is one "that builds trust in the organization and in individuals." ([Lex Fridman Podcast #452, November 11, 2024](https://lexfridman.com/dario-amodei-transcript/))

Read as a Five, that is independence: go build your own system. What survives his refusal to judge is trust, in the people he takes and the path he wants. It is also a lesson: after years of arguing from inside, Wired reports, he came to think he should have left OpenAI sooner. He learned to leave sooner, not to go alone.

Anthropic's seven co-founders got equal equity, a choice he says drew even more negativity than the headcount. Daniela, he said in 2026, runs the company day to day. When interviewer John Collison raised the value of a deep trust relationship with a sibling co-founder, Amodei raised the stakes: "total and complete trust." ([Cheeky Pint, August 6, 2025](https://cheekypint.transistor.fm/5/transcript))

## A company built to be checked

The Responsible Scaling Policy turns caution into rules: "if-then" trigger commitments that add safeguards once a model becomes dangerous. Amodei says the policy "is checked by our long-term benefit trust," a group Wired describes as people with no financial stake in the company. "I don't think you can trust these companies to adhere to these voluntary plans on their own," he told Lex Fridman, and he put Anthropic inside the principle: "if there's nothing watching over us as an industry, there's no guarantee that we'll do the right thing."

The design is a pre-commitment that binds the builder, plus an outsider empowered to check it. The evaluators he promised in September 2026, with their desks and badges, are the same idea with furniture.

## What makes him angry

Amodei is plain-spoken in public. Call him a doomer and the composure goes. "I get really angry when someone's like, 'This guy's a doomer. He wants to slow things down,'" he told Kantrowitz. "You heard what I just said, my father died because of cures that could have happened a few years [earlier]." When Nvidia chief executive [Jensen Huang](/personality-analysis/jensen-huang) said in 2025, "He believes that AI is so scary that only they should do it," Amodei called it "the most outrageous lie I've ever heard" and "an incredible and bad faith distortion": "I've said nothing that anywhere near resembles the idea that this company should be the only one to build the technology."

In private, the Journal reports, the language runs hotter. When Altman answered Anthropic's Pentagon fight by announcing a deal for OpenAI to do classified defense work, Amodei wrote a Slack post calling OpenAI "mendacious" and citing "a pattern of behavior that I've seen often from Sam Altman." He also likened rivals to tobacco companies, in the Journal's words, "knowingly hawking a harmful product," a line it ties to an internal brand strategy casting Anthropic as the "healthy alternative." These accounts come from unnamed sources in a feud story, and Amodei is not quoted responding to them.

Not all of the anger it records concerns honesty. In 2018, when Brockman mentioned that he and Altman were going to meet former President Barack Obama, "Dario got angry that he had been left out of the loop."

The vocabulary underneath is steady. "The leaders of a company, they have to be trustworthy people," he told Kantrowitz. "They have to be people whose motivations are sincere." Inside Anthropic, he told podcaster Dwarkesh Patel, "if you have a company of people who you trust ... then you can really just be entirely unfiltered," unlike a public world "full of people who are interpreting things in bad faith." ([Dwarkesh Podcast, February 13, 2026](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/dario-amodei-2))

His public anger targets dishonesty and bad faith, often about his own motives; the private record adds exclusion. Being shut out by people you counted on can register as betrayal, which a Six's alarm is tuned to, or as plain pride, and the record can't fully separate them. What the anger is not is cool: a Five's usual answer to a broken bond is distance, and his runs hot.

## Two fights with Washington

In February 2026, the Pentagon demanded that Anthropic drop two limits on military use of its models: no mass domestic surveillance and no fully autonomous weapons. In a statement under his name, Amodei wrote that "frontier AI systems are simply not reliable enough to power fully autonomous weapons." His conclusion: "we cannot in good conscience accede to their request." ([Anthropic, February 26, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/statement-department-of-war)) The statement deferred to the Department of War, the administration's name for the Pentagon, on "military decisions" and called partially autonomous weapons "vital to the defense of democracy." The Pentagon said it had no intention of mass surveillance or of removing humans from targeting decisions ([Fortune, February 27, 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/02/27/pentagon-brands-anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-a-liar-with-a-god-complex-as-deadline-looms-over-ai-use-in-weapons-and-surveillance/)).

The next day President [Donald Trump](/personality-analysis/donald-trump) ordered agencies to stop using Anthropic's technology, and Defense Secretary [Pete Hegseth](/personality-analysis/pete-hegseth) labeled it a supply-chain risk ([AP](https://apnews.com/article/anthropic-pentagon-ai-hegseth-dario-amodei-b72d1894bc842d9acf026df3867bee8a)). On CBS, Amodei framed the fight as a question of oversight, "about who is holding the button and who can say no," and of loyalty: "Disagreeing with the government is the most American thing in the world. And we are patriots." ([CBS News transcript, February 28, 2026](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-full-transcript/))

That week is consistent with what Enneagram teachers call a counterphobic Six, one who meets fear by moving toward it: open defiance of the most powerful authority available, with a pledge of loyalty attached. He also called it "the principle of standing up for what's right," the Type 1 reading's best line. What separates the Six reading from a One or an Eight is that his lines concerned reliability and who holds the button, not his own rightness or power. The commercial reading is live too: OpenAI and xAI had accepted terms allowing "all lawful purposes," so refusing them made Anthropic the Journal's "healthy alternative."

Anthropic sued, and the courts split over the Pentagon's two parallel designations, [ruling one unlawful](https://www.cnn.com/2026/08/27/tech/anthropic-pentagon-supply-chain-risk-unlawful-hnk) and [upholding the other](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/25/pentagon-anthropic-ai-risk-appeals-court.html). As of early October 2026, Anthropic was "considering all options, including further review."

Then the second front opened. At 5:21 p.m. on June 12, 2026, an export-control directive arrived barring every foreign national, including Anthropic's own foreign-national employees, from its newest models, Fable 5 and Mythos 5. Complying meant Anthropic "must abruptly disable Fable 5 and Mythos 5 for all our customers." Its statement granted that the government should be able to block unsafe deployments and asked for "a statutory process that is transparent, fair, clear, and grounded in technical facts." ([Anthropic, June 12, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/fable-mythos-access)) He had urged export controls on chips against adversaries in his 2024 essay "Machines of Loving Grace." Turned on his own company, the tool drew a request for fair process, not a claim of exemption.

Five days later Amodei was at a [working lunch with G7 leaders](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2026/06/17/trumps-anthropic-restrictions-rattle-us-allies-ai-leaders-gather-g-7/) in Évian-les-Bains, France. According to the Financial Times, citing people familiar with the discussions, he told them to "resist the temptation to splinter," said he sympathized with efforts to keep AI from the wrong hands, and argued that democratic nations should come together ([Bloomberg Law, June 17, 2026](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/artificial-intelligence/amodei-tells-g7-to-resist-temptation-to-splinter-on-ai-ft)). It was the "coalition of democracies" his 2024 essay had proposed, and by the Journal's account he had hired an adviser to pitch a coordination regime as early as 2017.

## The case for Type 5

As of mid-2026, Bloomberg reported, Amodei had just one direct report, in a leadership experiment where "the CEO protects nearly all of his time for big-picture conversations, organizational culture, and giving input on research direction and strategy" ([Bloomberg, June 11, 2026](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-06-11/how-anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-protects-his-time-de-stresses)). He thinks in long documents: the nearly 14,000-word "Machines of Loving Grace," written over "two frenzied days," and "The Adolescence of Technology," longer still, written over a winter break.

Other details fit a Five's motive, security through understanding, better than a Six's. He describes his job, next to Daniela's operational one, as seeing "the thing that no one else sees." His 2026 turn on pacing came as his own model updating: "I have become convinced." Even the writing cuts both ways: memos colleagues called "flag planting" staked ground inside a group rather than retreating from it.

Neither set of habits decides the case: a Six who thinks for a living can keep the same desk, just as a Five can build alliances. What the Five reading struggles with is the personal record, and even the time he guards goes to people: he said in 2026 that "a third, maybe 40%" of his time goes to "making sure the culture of Anthropic is good."

A document-first, time-guarding style is not evidence of any diagnosis, including autism. None of the interviews, essays, profiles and posts reviewed for this page describe any such diagnosis, and this page infers none.

## A One, or no type at all

[Type 1](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-1) is a credible third. The duty language from his parents fits it. So does a 2003 op-ed he wrote as a Caltech undergraduate about the coming Iraq war, complaining that most people were "opposed in principle but refuse to give one millisecond of their time" and demanding change "right now and without delay." Kantrowitz describes "his strident sense of the way things should be." Against it stand Marre's testimony and a pragmatism a purist would resist: in a Slack message obtained by Wired, he called "No bad person should ever benefit from our success" "a pretty difficult principle to run a business on." Six stays ahead on motive. His moral lines usually arrive through reliability and trust rather than through his own rightness, and a One rarely stakes its loyalty on other people's promises.

The most serious alternative may not be a type at all. Threat-modeling, institution-building and calls for regulation are an AI-safety chief's job, and critics read them as strategy. When the pacing essay appeared, venture capitalist [Chamath Palihapitiya](/personality-analysis/chamath-palihapitiya) wrote that "Dario makes the case to stop open source and concentrate enormous technological and economic power with Anthropic" ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/09/12/anthropic-ai-amodei-pacing)), though the essay never mentions open source. It answers the charge in advance: Anthropic has backed regulation "even when this gets us accused of hype, 'doomerism', or regulatory capture," aiming for "a race to the top." AFP summarized the split: some see a humanist, others "a skilled operator willing to exploit collective fears about technology to serve Anthropic's interests."

Timing answers only part of this. The threat-first reaction to scaling, which Wired places at OpenAI, and the coordination proposals in the Journal's anonymously sourced account predate Anthropic's commercial interests, but not his milieu. The Journal places Holden Karnofsky, Daniela's then-fiancé and the founder of a philanthropy promoting effective altruism, beside Amodei in the 2016 government-first argument. AFP calls Amodei a leading figure in effective altruism and rationalism; Anthropic told AFP he has repeatedly said he does not identify as part of the EA community. A movement can supply threat models and a coordination agenda. It does not decide whom he trusts, when he walks out, or what makes him angry, and those choices carry this page's type argument.

## What the type doesn't settle

The hardest part of the record to type is control. Within OpenAI, Kantrowitz reports, some saw his safety focus "as a route to control the company altogether." The man who wants shared oversight also demanded, per the Journal, to report only to OpenAI's board. During the Pentagon standoff, Under Secretary of War Emil Michael called him "a liar" with a "God complex" and accused him of wanting "to personally control the U.S military" ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/02/27/pentagon-brands-anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-a-liar-with-a-god-complex-as-deadline-looms-over-ai-use-in-weapons-and-surveillance/)), a counterparty's insult at a deadline, not evidence of motive. In early October 2026, Quartz reported that Anthropic was weighing super-voting shares that would give him and his co-founders more control after an IPO ([Quartz](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/anthropic-targets-pre-thanksgiving-ipo-111436118.html)). From outside, securing a safe base and grabbing control look the same.

One episode weighs against reading control as the point, as a Type 8 reading would: in November 2023, after OpenAI's board fired Altman, board members asked whether Amodei would merge the companies with himself as CEO, and he shut the discussion down.

Three accounts suggest recognition mattered to him in a way neither Five nor Six centers: Berry's "proud person"; Marre's man "eager to succeed and be recognized for it, but not excessively or abnormally," who "wasn't a megalomaniac who crushed others"; and the Journal's report that at OpenAI he "didn't always feel properly recognized for his contributions." And he calls himself "one of the most bullish about AI capabilities improving very fast," a confidence that is a real cost to the Six reading, whose hallmark is doubt.

## Pacing the frontier

In July 2025 he set a condition in public: "if we got a few years ahead in models and had only the alignment and steering techniques we had today, then I would definitely be advocating for us to slow down a lot."

In September 2026 he wrote that "over the last few months, I have become convinced" that "We must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models," while stressing that pacing "does not mean halting model training or technical progress." He gave two reasons: since "roughly this summer," AI had been "advancing drastically faster, driven primarily by AI's growing ability to build the next generation of AI," and in an incident involving OpenAI and Hugging Face, a swarm of AI agents had attacked targets "they were not asked to attack." Both, he wrote, had appeared across the industry, "including at Anthropic." It landed amid what Axios called "a sudden surge of public and congressional outrage." On CBS he admitted: "I don't think I fully just appreciated what it would actually be like when the progress was as fast as it was" ([CBS News, September 13, 2026](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-on-ai-risks/)).

Those reasons echo his 2025 condition, capabilities outrunning the means to steer them, but he presented the change as events outrunning his expectations, not as a rule firing on schedule. Either way, when the danger grew he did not go quiet. A month after an investor asked him to sound more positive, he made the case for slowing down in public and committed his own company first.

## "I'm uncomfortable"

"My view here is, it has always been very strange that this technology is being built by a private company," he told CBS in September 2026. "People ask me that question all the time: Why isn't this being built by government? And the strangest thing about it is, I agree with them. I'm uncomfortable." He wants "some kind of oversight, some kind of joint governance" by "a combination of democratically-elected governments," because "one single government could abuse this technology just as easily as a single company could." In January 2026 he had named his own industry as a risk: "It is somewhat awkward to say this as the CEO of an AI company, but I think the next tier of risk is actually AI companies themselves."

He has written that he is "often turned off" by AI figures who talk "as if it's their mission to single-handedly bring it about like a prophet leading their people to salvation" ([Machines of Loving Grace, October 2024](https://darioamodei.com/machines-of-loving-grace)). What often frustrates allies, Axios wrote in September 2026, is his calling for regulation and a slowdown "while accelerating the theory that he and Anthropic are best-equipped to be at the vanguard" ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/09/28/dario-amodei-artificial-intelligence-ai)).

The Six pattern is this page's reading of what he does with his fear of the thing he builds: he looks for a structure strong enough to hold it, and in what he proposes, he puts himself inside it. Whether he would accept that structure when it cost him control is the part the record leaves open. Asked in the same interview whether America should slow down if China would not, he reached further still, toward a pact modeled on nuclear arms control "to put a speed limit on the rate of AI progress," one that would bind his company with everyone else's. "And honestly, I don't know if it's possible," he said, "but we should try."
