In 2008, [Paul Graham](/personality-analysis/paul-graham) listed the qualities that come up when investors predict good outcomes for startups: "toughness, adaptability, determination." Then he named a founder who had them. "Sam Altman has it. You could parachute him into an island full of cannibals and come back in 5 years and he'd be the king" ([_A Fundraising Survival Guide_, 2008](https://www.paulgraham.com/fundraising.html)). Altman was 23.

On April 10, 2026, the day someone threw a Molotov cocktail at his home ([CNBC, April 10, 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/04/10/sam-altman-house-hit-with-molotov-cocktail-openai-office-threatened.html)), Altman published a blog post that mixed pride with apology: "I am not proud of being conflict-averse, which has caused great pain for me and OpenAI" ([his blog](https://blog.samaltman.com/2279512)).

Read side by side, the two lines describe one capacity from opposite ends. The founder who can win any island reads each room and gives it what it wants. The executive whom his biographer, Keach Hagey, calls "not great at dealing with conflict" will "basically tell people what they want to hear."

Samuel Harris Altman was born in Chicago on April 22, 1985. He has led OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT, since 2019, apart from five days in November 2023 when its board fired him, and he still held the job in early October 2026 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Altman); [Reuters](https://www.staradvertiser.com/2026/10/04/breaking-news/openais-altman-says-ai-benefits-warrant-accepting-some-risks/)). Those five days are now a film. This profile reads him from public evidence, not from access to his mind, and it is not a diagnosis.

## What is Sam Altman's personality type?

Sam Altman most likely fits Enneagram Type 3, the Achiever, at medium confidence. His biographer and former colleagues describe a long habit of telling people what they want to hear, and he has called his own conflict aversion a flaw that "caused great pain." Type 4 is the strongest alternative; Type 8 is a serious second.

[Type 3](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-3) is organized around being valuable, and being seen as valuable, through achievement. Ambition can't settle the question, since nearly every founder has it. The test here is narrower: what Altman does when keeping a room's approval conflicts with saying what he thinks or feels. A [Type 4](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-4) tends to protect the honest expression of feeling, even at a cost. A Three tends to adjust the presented self to what wins the room and narrate the feelings later. An [Eight](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-8) tends to confront and doesn't need to be liked.

Earlier versions of this page read Altman as a Type 4. A fuller check of the record, including several corrected quotes, moved the lead to Type 3.

## "I have to win, and I'm in charge of everything"

Altman grew up in Clayton, Missouri, outside St. Louis, the eldest of four siblings ([The New Yorker, 2026](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/04/13/sam-altman-may-control-our-future-can-he-be-trusted)). Over dinner with his brothers in 2016, with a New Yorker writer at the table, Jack recalled a board game: "Sam won every single game of Samurai when we were kids because he always declared himself the Samurai leader: 'I have to win, and I'm in charge of everything'" ([The New Yorker, 2016](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2016/10/10/sam-altmans-manifest-destiny)). It is a brother's joke about a board game, and also the first entry in a long file.

There was also a secret. "Growing up gay in the Midwest in the two-thousands was not the most awesome thing," he told The New Yorker in 2016. "And finding AOL chat rooms was transformative. Secrets are bad when you're eleven or twelve."

At about 17, after a Christian group boycotted an assembly about sexuality at the John Burroughs School, he addressed the whole school and announced that he was gay. He later recalled sitting in the hallway "changing the section where I told my own story back and forth like three times, because I just couldn't do it," before deciding: "I'll just tell everybody I'm gay. Whatever. Like, what's going to happen to me?" ([_First Contact_, 2020](https://web.archive.org/web/20250928083807/https://mostlyhuman.com/how-silicon-valleys-wunderkind-came-out-in-a-midwest-high-school/)). His college counsellor, Madelyn Gray, told The New Yorker: "What Sam did changed the school."

He says he was already "mostly out." Even so, this is the clearest early evidence that he will spend social standing to say something true about himself, and it is the Four reading's best exhibit.

In 2026 he told The New Yorker that at sixteen or seventeen he was physically attacked and called homophobic slurs in St. Louis. He declined to give details on the record, saying a fuller telling would "make me look like I'm manipulative or playing for sympathy," and he dismissed the idea that the attack, or his sexuality broadly, was significant to his identity. He offered one hedged guess about its legacy: "probably that has, like, some deep-seated psychological thing—that I think I'm over but I'm not—about not wanting more conflict." That is his speculation, and this profile builds nothing on it.

## Adaptability, as Paul Graham saw it

Altman left Stanford after two years to co-found Loopt, a location-sharing app, in 2005, at 19. It was in Y Combinator's first batch, which is how Graham came to back him ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Altman)). By 2009 Graham was writing that on "questions of strategy or ambition I ask 'What would Sama do?'" (["Five Founders"](https://www.paulgraham.com/5founders.html)). In 2016 he was blunter: "Sam is extremely good at becoming powerful."

Inside Loopt, the talent had a cost. According to Hagey's biography, _The Optimist_, as reported by The New Yorker, groups of senior employees "concerned with Altman's leadership and lack of transparency" twice asked the board to fire him, though "Altman inspired fierce loyalty, too." Some colleagues were "struck by his tendency to exaggerate, even about trivial things." An older employee, Mark Jacobstein, told Hagey: "There's a blurring between 'I think I can maybe accomplish this thing' and 'I have already accomplished this thing' that in its most toxic form leads to Theranos." A board member denied that the attempts to remove Altman were serious.

Karen Hao's book _Empire of AI_ (2025) quotes an unnamed colleague from those years who found him "so attentive" and added: "He's so good at adjusting to what you say, and you really feel like you're making progress with him. And then you realize over time that you're actually just running in place" ([pp. 34-35, via a compilation of excerpts](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/gm7Jovt93tSaToCgJ/sam-altman-s-track-record-of-manipulation-some-quotes-from)). Loopt sold for $43.4 million in 2012.

Read generously, this is a founder doing a founder's job: a company without a finished product has to sell what it will become. Through the type lens, Jacobstein's "blurring" is the telling detail. It describes someone who comes to believe the version of himself that wins the room. That kind of self-deception, more than plain lying, is the classic Type 3 trap, and a former OpenAI board member would describe the same thing years later.

## The nice guy in public

In 2014 Altman succeeded Graham as president of Y Combinator. Graham told The New Yorker in 2016 that when he made the offer in his kitchen, "he smiled, like, it worked. I had never seen an uncontrolled smile from Sam." The word "uncontrolled" implies that the usual face was controlled.

Hao describes the new president's makeover: he "traded in T-shirts and cargo shorts for fitted Henleys and jeans" and "learned to talk less, ask more questions, and project a thoughtful modesty with a furrowed brow. In private settings and with close friends, he still showed flashes of anger and frustration. In public ones and with acquaintances, he embodied the nice guy." He "avoided expressing negative emotions, avoided confrontation, avoided saying no to people" (_Empire of AI_, p. 42). Hao's sources are mostly unnamed, but it is the record's clearest account of a public self fitted to a role. An unnamed YC insider told The New Yorker in 2016: "Sam's a little too focussed on glory—he puts his personal brand way out front."

The same 2016 profile caught Altman describing his own inner weather: "When someone examines a photo and says, 'Oh, he's feeling this and this and this,' all these subtle emotions, I look on with alien intrigue." His mother said: "Sam does keep an awful lot tied up inside." He also said: "The missing circuit in my brain, the circuit that would make me care what people think about me, is a real gift."

Three years later he told Tyler Cowen that "all of us, myself included, are much more affected by what other people think of us and our ideas than we like to admit" ([Conversations with Tyler, January 2019](https://conversationswithtyler.com/episodes/sam-altman/)). A Four usually reports caring intensely about being seen accurately. Altman's 2016 self-portrait is closer to someone who brackets feeling in order to function, and his 2019 line suggests that not caring was part of the picture rather than the whole of it.

## Five days in November

On November 17, 2023, Altman was in Las Vegas for a Formula 1 race when the OpenAI board fired him on a video call, saying he "was not consistently candid in his communications with the board" ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/openai-announces-leadership-transition/)). That night he posted that it had been "sorta like reading your own eulogy while you're still alive. the outpouring of love is awesome" ([his post](https://x.com/sama/status/1725742088317534446)).

Altman set up what he called a "sort of government-in-exile" at home, with advisers including Airbnb's [Brian Chesky](/personality-analysis/brian-chesky) and the crisis-communications manager Chris Lehane (The New Yorker, 2026). When two board members called about his return, he told Bloomberg in 2025, "I was initially just supermad and said no." Then he set a condition: "Only if the whole board quits" ([Bloomberg Businessweek, January 2025](https://www.bloomberg.com/features/2025-sam-altman-interview/)). Within days, 745 of OpenAI's 770 employees signed a letter threatening to quit unless the board resigned, and with Microsoft's [Satya Nadella](/personality-analysis/satya-nadella) mediating, he was back within five days ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Removal_of_Sam_Altman_from_OpenAI)).

In a tense call after the firing, as the board pressed him to acknowledge a pattern of deception, "This is just so fucked up," he said repeatedly, according to people on the call. He also said: "I can't change my personality." Altman says he doesn't recall the exchange. "It's possible I meant something like 'I do try to be a unifying force,'" he told The New Yorker, attributing the criticism to a tendency, especially early in his career, "to be too much of a conflict avoider." A board member read it differently: "What it meant was 'I have this trait where I lie to people, and I'm not going to stop.'"

Four months later he told [Lex Fridman](/personality-analysis/lex-fridman) the firing was "definitely the most painful professional experience of my life, and chaotic and shameful and upsetting and a bunch of other negative things" ([transcript](https://lexfridman.com/sam-altman-2-transcript/)). The old Four reading leaned on "shameful," but it is one item in a run-on list, and a public firing would shame most people.

More telling is the shape of those days. In the moment he ran a campaign: advisers, a public story, a post that turned rejection into an "outpouring of love." He made hard demands while control of the company was at stake. The feelings came later, named in interviews.

Those days are now _Artificial_, Luca Guadagnino's biographical comedy-drama written by Simon Rich, with Andrew Garfield as Altman. It has its world premiere at the New York Film Festival on October 5, 2026, and Neon opens it in U.S. theaters on December 25 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_%282026_film%29); [Film at Lincoln Center](https://www.filmlinc.org/nyff2026/daily/luca-guadagninos-artificial-will-world-premiere-in-the-64th-new-york-film-festival-main-slate/)). A dramatization is not evidence, and nothing here draws on it. Of the trailer, Altman said on September 29: "I mean, looks like an awful movie, but I appreciate people's creativity, and I really do support that" ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/29/openai-devday-2026-live-updates.html)).

## "Just tells people what they want to hear"

The board's statement gave no specifics. People involved have since filled the silence, and every account is contested.

Ilya Sutskever, OpenAI's co-founder and chief scientist, wrote memos for the board. One begins with a list headed "Sam exhibits a consistent pattern of . . ." whose first item is "Lying." In one of them, he seemed concerned about entrusting the technology to someone who "just tells people what they want to hear" (The New Yorker, 2026). Sutskever later testified that much of the material came secondhand from [Mira Murati](/personality-analysis/mira-murati) ([Decrypt, November 2025](https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/inside-deposition-showed-openai-nearly-235547341.html)), who told The New Yorker: "Everything I shared was accurate, and I stand behind all of it."

Helen Toner, one of the four directors who fired him, said in May 2024 that for years Altman had made the board's job "really difficult" by "withholding information." Among her examples: "We learned about ChatGPT on Twitter" ([TED AI Show](https://www.ted.com/pages/what-really-went-down-at-openai-and-the-future-of-regulation-w-helen-toner)). Board chair Bret Taylor answered that OpenAI was "disappointed that Ms. Toner continues to revisit these issues" ([The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/2024/5/28/24166713/openai-helen-toner-explains-why-sam-altman-was-fired)), and Altman told Bloomberg, "I don't think I was doing things that were sneaky." In March 2024, OpenAI's summary of an outside review by the law firm WilmerHale reported "a breakdown in trust," found that the old board "acted within its broad discretion," and concluded that "his conduct did not mandate removal" ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/review-completed-altman-brockman-to-continue-to-lead-openai/)). No written report was released. Altman rejoined the board.

The least adversarial witness is his biographer. Hagey, who interviewed Altman for _The Optimist_, put it plainly: "he is not great at dealing with conflict, so he'll basically tell people what they want to hear. That causes a lot of sturm und drang in the management ranks, and it's a pattern." She traced the lost trust through "his first startup and very famously at OpenAI, as well as at Y Combinator," and added a caveat: "I think it's a typical critique of people who have the salesman skill set" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/01/sam-altman-biographer-keach-hagey-explains-why-the-openai-ceo-was-born-for-this-moment/)). Hao, in a 2025 excerpt for The Atlantic, reported that when two teams disagreed, "he often agreed in private with each of their perspectives" ([The Atlantic, May 2025](https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/archive/2025/05/karen-hao-empire-of-ai-excerpt/682798/)).

An unnamed board member told The New Yorker that Altman combines "a strong desire to please people, to be liked in any given interaction" with "almost a sociopathic lack of concern for the consequences that may come from deceiving someone." That is an adversary's characterization, not a diagnosis. Sue Yoon, a former board member, was gentler. Altman was "not this Machiavellian villain," she said. "He's too caught up in his own self-belief." Altman called the article "incendiary"; its co-author Ronan Farrow said that "OpenAI is not disputing the core facts" ([SF Standard](https://sfstandard.com/pacific-standard-time/2026/04/15/sam-altman-ronan-farrow-openai-pst-podcast/)).

## Why Type 3 leads

Put the accounts side by side and one behavior recurs across three organizations and two decades: Altman accommodates whoever is in front of him rather than risk a fight. Colleagues from Loopt onward, board members and his biographer describe it, and so does he. He told The New Yorker in 2026 that his main flaw as a manager had been his eagerness to avoid conflict. "Now I'm very happy to fire people quickly," he said.

Conflict avoidance alone could belong to a [Nine](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-9) or a [Two](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-2). Three things tip it toward Three.

First, the drive to win and hold the top job never lets up. In September 2017, in a fight over control of OpenAI, Sutskever and Brockman wrote to him: "We don't understand why the CEO title is so important to you. Your stated reasons have changed, and it's hard to really understand what's driving it" ([court-released email](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/5jjk4CDnj9tA7ugxr/openai-email-archives-from-musk-v-altman-and-openai-blog)). In 2019 he wrote that he wants each new project to be one that, "if successful, will make the rest of my career look like a footnote" (["How To Be Successful"](https://blog.samaltman.com/how-to-be-successful)). The New Yorker's reporters, after interviews with more than 100 people, wrote that most of their sources shared the judgment that "Altman has a relentless will to power." His own account: "I weirdly have very little personal ambition." A long climb paired with a sincere-sounding denial fits the Three's blind spot, where the achieving self and the mission blur together.

Second, his feelings tend to arrive after the fact, and in public. He has twice described himself as low in emotional attunement: the "alien intrigue" of 2016 and, to Bloomberg in 2025, "I'm not a high-EQ person." Hao reports that in his YC years he "avoided expressing negative emotions," and his mother says he keeps "an awful lot tied up inside." The hurt, shame and melancholy in the record mostly surface in podcasts, posts and profiles once the moment has passed. A Four tends to lead with feeling as it happens. A Three tends to set it aside to function, then narrate it.

Third, he watches the picture of himself closely. In 2016 he wrote that [Donald Trump](/personality-analysis/donald-trump) "represents an unprecedented threat to America" ([Sam Altman, October 2016](https://blog.samaltman.com/the-2016-election)). In January 2025, at the White House announcement of the Stargate AI-infrastructure project, he told Trump, "We wouldn't be able to do this without you, Mr. President" ([transcript](https://singjupost.com/transcript-of-trump-press-conference-announcing-ai-infrastructure-project-called-stargate/)). The next day he posted that he had changed his view: "i wish i had done more of my own thinking and definitely fell in the npc trap" ([his post](https://x.com/sama/status/1882234406662000833)). Plenty of executives courted the new administration for business reasons. What fits the Three reading is the framing: a turn toward power, presented as independent thinking.

The same attention shows up elsewhere. He withheld details of the teenage assault lest a fuller telling "make me look like I'm manipulative or playing for sympathy." He framed his regret about equity around "conspiracy theories." After the attack on his home, he wrote that he had "underestimated the power of words and narratives." What he guards is an image of being credible, effective and likeable, the Three's currency, more than an image of being distinctive, the Four's.

## The case for Type 4

The Four reading has real evidence, starting with the speech at 17 and continuing in a vocabulary pure operators rarely use. In 2016, on AI narrowing what humans do best, he said: "I'm on Team Human. I don't have a good logical reason why I'm sad, except that the class of things that humans are better at continues to narrow." After a moment: "'Melancholy' is a better word than 'sad.'" In July 2026, passing along an idea from Naval Ravikant, he called the bad parts of life "all part of the like interestingness of and the kind of like emotional depth and range," and said: "I find it like fairly easy to be grateful for the bad days" ([Relentless, July 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vv3CEAS_w34)).

Its best adult exhibit is private. On February 18, 2023, while [Elon Musk](/personality-analysis/elon-musk) was criticizing OpenAI in public, Altman emailed him: "well, you're my hero and that's what it feels like when you attack openai. i am tremendously thankful for everything you've done to help–i don't think openai would have happened without you–and it really fucking hurts when you publicly attack openai" ([email released in Musk's lawsuit](https://chatgptiseatingtheworld.com/2026/01/19/2023-email-sam-altman-called-elon-musk-his-hero-but-said-he-was-hurt-by-musks-then-criticism-of-openai/)). In public he called Musk "clearly a bully" ([Honestly with Bari Weiss, December 2024](https://www.thefp.com/p/sam-altman-openai-feud-with-elon)) and met Musk's $97.4 billion bid for OpenAI's nonprofit in February 2025 with a joke: "no thank you but we will buy twitter for $9.74 billion if you want" ([his post](https://x.com/sama/status/1889059531625464090)). The email is raw; the public lines win the exchange without making him look like the aggressor.

The old Four case also leaned on money. His 2023 pay from OpenAI was $76,001 ([Bloomberg, November 2024](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/ip-law/openai-ceo-received-76-001-in-pay-last-year-filing-shows)), and as of May 2026 he held no direct equity in the company. At the DealBook Summit in December 2024 he called the role "my childhood dream job." He has also said he wishes he had taken equity, because "i think it would have led to far fewer conspiracy theories" ([X, October 2025](https://x.com/sama/status/1983941806393024762)). As evidence of indifference to status, the salary is weaker than it looks, and he frames the regret in terms of how it looks.

Each Four exhibit has limits. Altman says he was already "mostly out" before the speech, and in 2024 he told [The Advocate](https://www.advocate.com/news/sam-altman-openai-exclusive): "I don't really think about being a gay CEO. I don't look at it that way." "Melancholy" was about humanity's prospects, not his own, and "emotional depth and range" is Naval's idea, offered as founder advice. The Four's core motive, protecting an authentic self even when it costs approval, is hard to find in the adult record, where the costly choices mostly run the other way. A Four with a strong Three wing, working as a public CEO, could look a lot like this, which is why Four remains the live alternative.

## The case for Type 8

An Eight reading has exhibits too: the Samurai leader; Graham's "extremely good at becoming powerful"; "Only if the whole board quits"; and a former employee's recollection, reported by The New Yorker, that he once said, "I don't care about money. I care more about power." He has refused a powerful counterpart when control was at stake. In 2017 Sutskever and Brockman credited him as a counterweight to Musk in a negotiation where "Sam stood his ground," and in 2026 he wrote that he was proud of how he "held the line on not being willing to agree to the unilateral control" Musk wanted.

Eights confront, and they don't need to be liked in each interaction. Altman confronts openly when control of the institution is on the line, as in 2017 and 2023, and accommodates almost everywhere else, by his own account and others'. Nine and Two explain the accommodation, and the attentiveness colleagues describe gives Two some support, but neither explains two decades of reaching for, and holding, the top job.

## The sister's lawsuit

Altman's sister, Annie, has said publicly since at least November 2021 that she experienced abuse from her siblings, mostly from Sam ([her post](https://x.com/anniealtman108/status/1459696444802142213)). In January 2025 she sued him in federal court in Missouri, alleging that he sexually abused her beginning when she was 3 and he was 12 ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/openai-ceo-sam-altman-denies-sexual-abuse-allegations-made-sister-laws-rcna186759)). Sam, their mother and their brothers Jack and Max responded in a joint statement: "All of these claims are utterly untrue. This situation causes immense pain to our entire family" ([statement](https://x.com/sama/status/1876780763653263770)).

On March 20, 2026, the judge dismissed her common-law claims as time-barred and allowed an amended complaint, which she filed on April 1 under a Missouri statute for childhood sexual abuse claims. Altman is countersuing her for defamation ([Reuters, via U.S. News](https://www.usnews.com/news/top-news/articles/2026-03-20/judge-for-now-dismisses-lawsuit-by-sam-altmans-sister-accusing-openai-ceo-of-sexual-abuse)). As of September 30, 2026, the case was active, and a December 2025 order had set a jury trial for May 3, 2027 ([court docket](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/69520118/altman-v-altman/)). The New Yorker wrote that it "could not substantiate Annie's account."

The allegation is unresolved, and it is not evidence for or against any personality type. An honest profile can't leave it out.

## What the type doesn't explain

Some of the record sits awkwardly with any type. The speech at 17 still reads as courage an image-manager wouldn't spend, and the private email to Musk as plain hurt. In 2022 he said he had started his career as "a very anxious, high-strung person, much more than I realized," and that unstructured meditation left him with "this very deep-felt sense that there is no self that I can still find to identify with in any way at all" ([podcast transcript](https://www.artofaccomplishment.com/podcast/sam-altman-leading-with-crippling-anxiety-discovering-meditation-and-building-intelligence-with-self-awareness-3)). Anxiety of that kind fits a [Six](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6) as well as anything, though Threes whose worth rides on performance are often anxious too.

The largest open question concerns the work. In May 2024 Jan Leike, who led OpenAI's superalignment work, resigned, posting that "safety culture and processes have taken a backseat to shiny products" ([his post](https://x.com/janleike/status/1791498184671605209)), and the team was disbanded. In September 2026 Altman swung the other way. "I agree with Dario that we need to pace the frontier," he posted, endorsing [Dario Amodei](/personality-analysis/dario-amodei)'s commitment to independent evaluators and saying OpenAI would do the same ([his post](https://x.com/sama/status/2098811563415150910)). He told Fortune that "right now would be an ill-advised moment to go public," three months after OpenAI announced a confidential IPO filing ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/09/12/sam-altman-openai-ipo-delay-ill-advised-moment-safety-concerns/)). A Three reading sees market timing; a reading of sincere recalibration sees conviction. Whether outside evaluators actually work inside OpenAI is the test, and as of October 5, 2026, it is unsettled.

## Nostalgic for the present

Fatherhood is the clearest change in the recent record. NBC News reported in January 2024 that Altman had married Oliver Mulherin ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/sam-altman-oliver-mulherin-ollie-wedding-married-partner-boyfriend-rcna133512)), and their son was born in February 2025. "i have never felt such love," he posted ([Sam Altman on X](https://x.com/sama/status/1893423520866033865)). Of the first hour, he told Bloomberg: "I am being like neuro chemically hacked. But I'm like noticing it happening" ([Bloomberg video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yTu0ak4GoyM)).

On February 2, 2026, he went "off-script for a minute" on X. He had built an app with OpenAI's Codex, asked it for feature ideas, and found that "at least a couple of them were better than I was thinking of. I felt a little useless and it was sad." Then: "I am sure we will figure out much better and more interesting ways to spend our time, and amazing new ways to be useful to each other, but I am feeling nostalgic for the present" ([his post](https://x.com/sama/status/2018444309750862333)).

A Four reading hears wistfulness. A Three reading hears the sting of being outdone, by a product he sells, at something he was good at, and a choice to say so in public. Both fit the sentence.

Graham predicted that Altman would end up king of any island, and he has, more than once. The 2026 apology describes the price of the same gift: rooms kept happy, fights put off, trust spent later. Whether he can lose a room on purpose is what the next few years will show.
