In 2008, [Paul Graham](/personality-analysis/paul-graham), the essayist who co-founded the startup accelerator Y Combinator, told founders to be independent of investors. When YC predicts good outcomes, he wrote, "the qualities that come up in the supporting arguments are toughness, adaptability, determination." Then: "Sam Altman has it. You could parachute him into an island full of cannibals and come back in 5 years and he'd be the king." He credited Altman's "deal-making ability" and his knack for convincing investors "that you'll succeed with or without them" ([_A Fundraising Survival Guide_, 2008](https://www.paulgraham.com/fundraising.html)). Altman was 23.

On April 10, 2026, days after The New Yorker published an investigation asking whether he could be trusted ([The New Yorker, April 2026](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/04/13/sam-altman-may-control-our-future-can-he-be-trusted)), and on the day someone threw a Molotov cocktail at his home ([CNBC, April 10, 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/04/10/sam-altman-house-hit-with-molotov-cocktail-openai-office-threatened.html)), Altman posted a mix of pride and apology. He was proud to have "held the line on not being willing to agree to the unilateral control" Elon Musk wanted over OpenAI. But: "I am not proud of being conflict-averse, which has caused great pain for me and OpenAI" ([his blog](https://blog.samaltman.com/2279512)).

Graham was praising independence; the 2026 line confesses a costly habit of keeping the peace. Read side by side, the two lines describe one capacity from opposite ends. In this profile's reading, not Graham's, that capacity is persuasion. The New Yorker called his "greatest strength" the "ability to convince disparate groups that what he wants and what they need are one and the same." His biographer, Keach Hagey, names the cost: he is "not great at dealing with conflict, so he'll basically tell people what they want to hear" ([TechCrunch, 2025](https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/01/sam-altman-biographer-keach-hagey-explains-why-the-openai-ceo-was-born-for-this-moment/)).

Altman was born in Chicago on April 22, 1985. He has led OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT, since 2019, apart from five days in November 2023 when its board fired him, and he still held the job in early October 2026 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Altman); [Reuters](https://www.staradvertiser.com/2026/10/04/breaking-news/openais-altman-says-ai-benefits-warrant-accepting-some-risks/)). This profile reads him from public evidence; it is not a diagnosis.

## What is Sam Altman's personality type?

Sam Altman most likely fits Enneagram Type 3, the Achiever, at medium confidence. Across two decades, colleagues and his biographer describe him adjusting to win each room while steering it toward what he wants, and he has called his conflict aversion a flaw that "caused great pain." Type 4 is the strongest alternative; Type 8 is a serious second.

[Type 3](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-3) is organized around being valuable, and being seen as valuable, through achievement. Ambition can't settle the question, since nearly every founder has it. The test here is narrower: what Altman does when keeping a room's approval conflicts with saying what he thinks or feels. A [Type 4](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-4) tends to protect the honest expression of feeling, even at a cost. A Three tends to adjust the presented self to what wins the room. An [Eight](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-8) tends to confront and doesn't need to be liked.

Earlier versions of this page read Altman as a Type 4; Four now ranks second, for the reasons below.

## "I have to win, and I'm in charge of everything"

Altman grew up in Clayton, Missouri, outside St. Louis, the eldest of four siblings ([The New Yorker, 2026](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/04/13/sam-altman-may-control-our-future-can-he-be-trusted)). At a 2016 family dinner, his brother Jack told a New Yorker writer: "Sam won every single game of Samurai when we were kids because he always declared himself the Samurai leader: 'I have to win, and I'm in charge of everything'" ([The New Yorker, 2016](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2016/10/10/sam-altmans-manifest-destiny)). It is a brother's joke about a board game, and also the first entry in a long file.

At about 17, after a Christian group boycotted an assembly about sexuality at the John Burroughs School, he addressed the whole school, announced that he was gay and, in The New Yorker's paraphrase, asked "whether the school wanted to be a repressive place or one open to different ideas." Recalling the speech in 2020, he said he had rewritten his own story "back and forth like three times, because I just couldn't do it" ([_First Contact_](https://web.archive.org/web/20250928083807/https://mostlyhuman.com/how-silicon-valleys-wunderkind-came-out-in-a-midwest-high-school/)).

He says he was already "mostly out." Even so, this is the clearest early evidence that he will spend social standing to say something true about himself, and to challenge an institution in public. It is the Four reading's best exhibit.

In 2026 he told The New Yorker that at sixteen or seventeen he was physically attacked and called homophobic slurs in St. Louis. He declined to give details on the record and offered one hedged guess about its legacy: "probably that has, like, some deep-seated psychological thing—that I think I'm over but I'm not—about not wanting more conflict." That is his speculation, and this profile builds nothing on it.

## Adaptability, as Paul Graham saw it

Altman left Stanford after two years to co-found Loopt, a location-sharing app, in 2005, at 19. It was in Y Combinator's first batch, which is how Graham came to back him ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Altman)).

Inside Loopt, the talent had a cost. According to Hagey's biography, _The Optimist_, as reported by The New Yorker, senior employees "concerned with Altman's leadership and lack of transparency" twice asked the board to fire him. An older employee, Mark Jacobstein, told Hagey: "There's a blurring between 'I think I can maybe accomplish this thing' and 'I have already accomplished this thing' that in its most toxic form leads to Theranos," the blood-testing startup built on false claims ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theranos)). A board member denied that the attempts to remove Altman were serious.

Karen Hao's _Empire of AI_ (2025) quotes an unnamed colleague from then: "He's so attentive. But then part of it is he uses that to figure out how to influence you in different ways. He's so good at adjusting to what you say, and you really feel like you're making progress with him" ([pp. 34-35, via a critic's compilation of excerpts](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/gm7Jovt93tSaToCgJ/sam-altman-s-track-record-of-manipulation-some-quotes-from)). In 2012, after failing to gain significant user traction, Loopt sold for $43.4 million.

Read generously, this is a founder doing a founder's job: a company without a finished product has to sell what it will become. Through the type lens, Jacobstein's "blurring" is the telling detail. It describes someone who comes to believe the version of himself that wins the room. That kind of self-deception, more than plain lying, is the classic Type 3 trap, and a former OpenAI board member would describe the same thing years later.

## The nice guy in public

In 2014 Altman succeeded Graham as president of Y Combinator. Graham told The New Yorker in 2016 that when he made the offer, "he smiled, like, it worked. I had never seen an uncontrolled smile from Sam." The word "uncontrolled" implies that the usual face was controlled.

In a passage the same compilation places in his YC years (p. 42), Hao writes: "In private settings and with close friends, he still showed flashes of anger and frustration. In public ones and with acquaintances, he embodied the nice guy." After a cut the compilation marks, he "avoided expressing negative emotions, avoided confrontation, avoided saying no to people." Her sources are mostly unnamed, and this profile could not check the passage against the book.

Waving off a suggestion in 2016 that he had Asperger's, he said: "When someone examines a photo and says, 'Oh, he's feeling this and this and this,' all these subtle emotions, I look on with alien intrigue." In 2025, discussing how much tension he had sensed on OpenAI's board, he told Bloomberg: "I'm not a high-EQ person" ([Bloomberg Businessweek, January 2025](https://www.bloomberg.com/features/2025-sam-altman-interview/)). Yet the Loopt colleague describes a man who studies people to influence them. The accounts fit if what he reads is what a person wants rather than what a person feels.

How he left YC in 2019, as he took over OpenAI, is disputed. Altman says he was never fired. Graham has tweeted that "we didn't want him to leave, just to choose" between YC and OpenAI, and told The New Yorker, "All we could do was apply moral pressure." The magazine, citing YC partners and contemporaneous materials, reported that the parting "was not entirely mutual" and, secondhand, that Graham once told colleagues "Sam had been lying to us all the time."

## Five days in November

OpenAI began in 2015 as a nonprofit whose board, The New Yorker notes, "had a duty to prioritize the safety of humanity over the company's success." It added a "capped-profit" company in 2019 ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/openai-lp/)) and in 2025 moved toward "a normal capital structure where everyone has stock" ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/evolving-our-structure/)). A board charged with safety had to trust what its CEO told it.

On November 17, 2023, Altman was in Las Vegas for a Formula 1 race when the board fired him on a video call, saying he "was not consistently candid in his communications with the board" ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/openai-announces-leadership-transition/)). That night he posted that it had been "sorta like reading your own eulogy while you're still alive. the outpouring of love is awesome" ([his post](https://x.com/sama/status/1725742088317534446)).

At home he set up what he called a "sort of government-in-exile," where advisers including Airbnb's [Brian Chesky](/personality-analysis/brian-chesky) and the crisis-communications manager Chris Lehane built a public case for his return (The New Yorker, 2026). Others supplied leverage. Co-founder Greg Brockman quit in protest ([his post](https://x.com/gdb/status/1725667410387378559)). Microsoft, OpenAI's biggest strategic shareholder, announced through [Satya Nadella](/personality-analysis/satya-nadella) that Altman and Brockman would join it "to lead a new advanced AI research team" ([Nadella's post](https://x.com/satyanadella/status/1726509045803336122)). And 745 of OpenAI's 770 employees signed a letter threatening to quit unless the board resigned ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Removal_of_Sam_Altman_from_OpenAI)). The campaign was his; the leverage was mostly other people's.

When two board members called about his return, he told Bloomberg, "I was initially just supermad and said no." His condition: "Only if the whole board quits." Looking back: "I wish I had taken a different tack than that." The board named Emmett Shear CEO instead, which Altman called "real deception." Under pressure from employees and investors, it gave way within five days.

On a tense call after the firing, as the board pressed him to acknowledge a pattern of deception, "This is just so fucked up," he said repeatedly, according to people on the call. He also said: "I can't change my personality." Altman says he doesn't recall the exchange. "It's possible I meant something like 'I do try to be a unifying force,'" he told The New Yorker, crediting the trait with his company's success and attributing the criticism to a tendency, especially early on, "to be too much of a conflict avoider." A board member read it differently: "What it meant was 'I have this trait where I lie to people, and I'm not going to stop.'"

Four months later he told [Lex Fridman](/personality-analysis/lex-fridman) the firing was "definitely the most painful professional experience of my life, and chaotic and shameful and upsetting and a bunch of other negative things" ([transcript](https://lexfridman.com/sam-altman-2-transcript/)). A Four reading leans on "shameful," but it is one item in a run-on list, and a public firing would shame most people.

Those days are now _Artificial_, a Luca Guadagnino comedy-drama starring Andrew Garfield, scheduled to premiere at the New York Film Festival on October 5, 2026, with a U.S. release set for December 25, 2026 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_%282026_film%29)). A dramatization is not evidence, and nothing here draws on it.

## "Just tells people what they want to hear"

The board gave no specifics. Those involved have since filled the silence, and every account is contested.

OpenAI co-founder Ilya Sutskever, then its chief scientist, wrote memos for the board. One begins with a list headed "Sam exhibits a consistent pattern of . . ." whose first item is "Lying." In one of them, he seemed concerned about entrusting the technology to someone who "just tells people what they want to hear" (The New Yorker, 2026). On November 20, 2023, Sutskever posted, "I deeply regret my participation in the board's actions," and signed the employees' letter ([his post](https://x.com/ilyasut/status/1726590052392956028); [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2023/11/20/ilya-sutskever-openai-cofounder-deeply-regrets-resign/)), regretting the action without retracting the memos. In a 2025 deposition he called the process "rushed," said he had weighed removal for "at least a year," and said much of the material came secondhand from [Mira Murati](/personality-analysis/mira-murati), interim CEO during the crisis ([Decrypt](https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/inside-deposition-showed-openai-nearly-235547341.html)). Murati told The New Yorker: "Everything I shared was accurate, and I stand behind all of it."

Helen Toner, one of the four directors who fired him, said in May 2024 that Altman made the board's job "really difficult" by "withholding information" and that "Sam started lying to other board members in order to try and push me off the board" ([TED AI Show](https://www.ted.com/pages/what-really-went-down-at-openai-and-the-future-of-regulation-w-helen-toner)). OpenAI said it was "disappointed that Ms. Toner continues to revisit these issues" ([The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/2024/5/28/24166713/openai-helen-toner-explains-why-sam-altman-was-fired)). Altman told Bloomberg, "I don't think I was doing things that were sneaky," but conceded that board members "were not happy with the way that I tried to get them off the board. Lesson learned on that." OpenAI said a 2023 Bloomberg report that he misrepresented directors' views in that effort "significantly differs from Sam's recollection" ([Bloomberg, via Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/technology/companies/more-details-surface-openais-sam-altman-ouster-was-result-of-drawn-out-tensions-1.1702107924401)). A review by the law firm WilmerHale, as OpenAI summarized it in March 2024, found that "his conduct did not mandate removal" ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/review-completed-altman-brockman-to-continue-to-lead-openai/)). No written report was released.

The least adversarial witness is his biographer. Hagey traced the lost trust through "his first startup and very famously at OpenAI, as well as at Y Combinator," and added a caveat: "I think it's a typical critique of people who have the salesman skill set" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/01/sam-altman-biographer-keach-hagey-explains-why-the-openai-ceo-was-born-for-this-moment/)).

An unnamed board member told The New Yorker that Altman combines "a strong desire to please people, to be liked in any given interaction" with "almost a sociopathic lack of concern for the consequences that may come from deceiving someone." That is an adversary's characterization, not a diagnosis. Sue Yoon, a former board member, was gentler: "He's too caught up in his own self-belief." Altman called the article "incendiary"; its co-author Ronan Farrow said that "OpenAI is not disputing the core facts" ([SF Standard](https://sfstandard.com/pacific-standard-time/2026/04/15/sam-altman-ronan-farrow-openai-pst-podcast/)).

Asked under oath in May 2026, in Musk's lawsuit, whether he always tells the truth, Altman said, "I believe I'm a truthful person," then: "I'm sure there is some time in my life when I have not" ([The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2026/05/15/tech/sam-altman-defense-testimony-elon-musk-trial)). The jury found that Musk had sued too late, a ruling on timing, not honesty; Musk's lawyer said he would appeal ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/openai-elon-musk-case-verdict-rcna345655)).

## Why Type 3 leads

Put the accounts side by side and one behavior recurs across three organizations and two decades. In the room, Altman accommodates whoever is in front of him rather than risk a fight; when two teams disagreed, Hao reported, "he often agreed in private with each of their perspectives" ([The Atlantic, May 2025](https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/archive/2025/05/karen-hao-empire-of-ai-excerpt/682798/)). Outside the room, as the board fight showed, he pursues his aims indirectly. He names the trait himself, but mostly while answering charges of dishonesty, so "conflict-averse" is best read as his framing of disputed behavior, not as another witness.

The strongest rival to any type is the job: a fundraiser is paid to tell each audience what it wants. The habit persisted when it hurt him, at Loopt, at YC and in the 2023 firing. But it began with his first job, so timing can't separate craft from motive, which is much of why confidence stays at medium.

Accommodation alone could belong to a [Nine](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-9) or a [Two](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-2). What tips it toward Three is that the accommodation serves his own direction. Hao reports, via the compilation, that he privately sympathized with OpenAI's safety team, while "In private conversations with Applied," the product side, "he pressed them to keep going" (p. 240). A Nine's merging tends to dissolve its own agenda; a Two's attentiveness seeks to be needed. His agenda survives every room. A [Seven](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-7), the other look-alike for an upbeat founder, guards freedom and options; Altman fought for years over one title.

The prize is the top job. In September 2017, in a fight over control of OpenAI, Sutskever and Brockman wrote to him: "We don't understand why the CEO title is so important to you. Your stated reasons have changed" ([court-released email](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/5jjk4CDnj9tA7ugxr/openai-email-archives-from-musk-v-altman-and-openai-blog)). The next morning, after Musk had threatened to stop funding OpenAI unless it stayed a nonprofit, Altman replied: "i remain enthusiastic about the non-profit structure!" Eighteen months later OpenAI created its capped-profit company, with Altman as CEO. Capital needs explain that shift; what fits Three is a reply that gave an angry funder the sentence he wanted.

His own account runs the other way: "I weirdly have very little personal ambition," he told The New Yorker, after describing himself as "very ambitious as far as, like, my hope for humanity." He may experience the drive as mission. What would tell the two apart is whether he ever gives up the top role, or a public win, because the mission requires it.

His public face is managed, and feeling shows mostly in private: Graham's "uncontrolled smile," Hao's private "flashes of anger and frustration," the email to Musk below. The feeling is not delayed; he was "supermad" on the board's call and, hours after the attack on his home, "awake in the middle of the night and pissed." But a reserved Four also holds suffering in. What separates Three from Four is what the public face protects.

In 2016 he called [Donald Trump](/personality-analysis/donald-trump) "an unprecedented threat to America" ([his blog](https://blog.samaltman.com/the-2016-election)). In January 2025, at a White House event for the Stargate AI-infrastructure project, he told Trump, "We wouldn't be able to do this without you, Mr. President" ([transcript](https://singjupost.com/transcript-of-trump-press-conference-announcing-ai-infrastructure-project-called-stargate/)), and the next day posted: "i wish i had done more of my own thinking and definitely fell in the npc trap" ([his post](https://x.com/sama/status/1882234406662000833)). Plenty of executives courted the new administration for business reasons. What fits the Three reading is the framing: a turn toward power, presented as independent thinking. The appeal to independence has a Four-like ring; the move it explained did not. Where looking credible and looking distinctive pull apart, as in his regret, below, about having no equity, he picks credible. What he guards is an image of being credible, effective and likeable, the Three's currency, more than being distinctive, the Four's.

## The case for Type 4

The Four reading has real evidence, starting with the speech at 17 and a vocabulary pure operators rarely use. In 2016, sad that AI was narrowing what humans do best, he corrected himself: "'Melancholy' is a better word than 'sad.'"

Its best adult exhibit is private. On February 18, 2023, while [Elon Musk](/personality-analysis/elon-musk) was criticizing OpenAI in public, Altman emailed him: "well, you're my hero and that's what it feels like when you attack openai." He added that "it really fucking hurts when you publicly attack openai" ([email released in Musk's lawsuit](https://chatgptiseatingtheworld.com/2026/01/19/2023-email-sam-altman-called-elon-musk-his-hero-but-said-he-was-hurt-by-musks-then-criticism-of-openai/)). In public he called Musk "clearly a bully" ([Honestly with Bari Weiss, December 2024](https://www.thefp.com/p/sam-altman-openai-feud-with-elon)) and met Musk's $97.4 billion bid for OpenAI's nonprofit with a joke: "no thank you but we will buy twitter for $9.74 billion if you want" ([his post](https://x.com/sama/status/1889059531625464090)). The email is raw; the public lines win the exchange without making him look like the aggressor.

A Four case can also point to money. His 2023 pay from OpenAI was $76,001 ([Bloomberg](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/ip-law/openai-ceo-received-76-001-in-pay-last-year-filing-shows)), and as of May 2026 he held no direct equity in the company. But he has said he wishes he had taken equity, because "i think it would have led to far fewer conspiracy theories" ([X, October 2025](https://x.com/sama/status/1983941806393024762)). As evidence of indifference to status, the salary is weaker than it looks, and he frames the regret in terms of how it looks.

Each Four exhibit has limits. Altman says he was already "mostly out" before the speech, and in 2024 he told [The Advocate](https://www.advocate.com/news/sam-altman-openai-exclusive): "I don't really think about being a gay CEO." "Melancholy" was about humanity's prospects, not his own. The Four's core motive, protecting an authentic self even when it costs approval, is hard to find in the adult record, where the costly choices mostly run the other way. A reserved Four, or one with a strong Three wing, could look like this as a public CEO. Four outranks Eight because it explains what Three can't, the speech and the Musk email, while Eight fails the accommodation test outright.

## The case for Type 8

An Eight reading has exhibits too: the Samurai leader; Graham's 2016 verdict, "Sam is extremely good at becoming powerful"; "Only if the whole board quits"; The New Yorker's 2016 description of him as "quick to smile, but also quick to anger"; and a former employee's recollection that he once said, "I don't care about money. I care more about power." In 2017 Sutskever and Brockman credited him as a counterweight to Musk in a negotiation where "Sam stood his ground."

Eights confront, and they don't need to be liked in each interaction. Altman confronts openly when control of the institution is on the line, as in 2017 and 2023. Elsewhere he accommodates in the room and, when he moves against people, does it indirectly: trying to get board members off the board, by his own account, and misleading other directors to do it, by Toner's. An Eight tends to fight in the open. Sidelining opponents while keeping the public face clean fits a Three better.

## What the type doesn't explain

Some of the record sits awkwardly with any type. The speech at 17 challenged a school in public, the kind of open confrontation the conflict-averse thesis doesn't predict. The email to Musk reads as plain hurt. And in 2016 he said: "The missing circuit in my brain, the circuit that would make me care what people think about me, is a real gift. Most people want to be accepted, so they won't take risks that could make them look crazy." That cuts against the approval motive at Three's core. In 2019 he told Tyler Cowen that "all of us, myself included, are much more affected by what other people think of us and our ideas than we like to admit" ([Conversations with Tyler](https://conversationswithtyler.com/episodes/sam-altman/)), which qualifies the boast without making it support for Three. If he keeps taking risks that cost approval outside fights over control, the Three reading weakens.

There is also an anxious strand. In 2022 he said he had started his career as "a very anxious, high-strung person, much more than I realized" ([podcast transcript](https://www.artofaccomplishment.com/podcast/sam-altman-leading-with-crippling-anxiety-discovering-meditation-and-building-intelligence-with-self-awareness-3)). In 2016 he listed disaster preparations from "guns, gold, potassium iodide" to "gas masks from the Israeli Defense Force," and [Peter Thiel](/personality-analysis/peter-thiel) called him "an optimist yet a survivalist." In 2023 he told TIME the prepper image had been "caricatured very wildly" ([TIME](https://time.com/6288584/openai-sam-altman-full-interview/)). That fits a [Six](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6) as well as anything here. It doesn't displace Three, because his costly, repeated choices concern winning rooms and the top job, not securing support or certainty, and performance-driven Threes are often anxious too. It remains the portrait's largest gap.

The largest open question concerns the work. In May 2024 Jan Leike, who led OpenAI's "superalignment" team on long-term AI risk, quit, posting that "safety culture and processes have taken a backseat to shiny products" ([his post](https://x.com/janleike/status/1791498184671605209)), and the team was disbanded ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/05/17/openai-superalignment-sutskever-leike.html)). Altman replied the same day: "he's right we have a lot more to do; we are committed to doing it" ([his post](https://x.com/sama/status/1791543264090472660)). In September 2026 he made it specific: "I agree with Dario that we need to pace the frontier." He backed a pledge by [Dario Amodei](/personality-analysis/dario-amodei), who left OpenAI's safety team in 2020 to co-found the rival Anthropic, to give independent evaluators "employee-like access," and said OpenAI would do the same ([his post](https://x.com/sama/status/2098811563415150910)). Whether outside evaluators actually work inside OpenAI is the test, and as of October 5, 2026, it is unsettled. It tests follow-through, not type: a Three can recalibrate sincerely.

## The sister's lawsuit

Altman's sister, Annie, has said publicly since at least November 2021 that she experienced abuse from her siblings, mostly from Sam ([her post](https://x.com/anniealtman108/status/1459696444802142213)). In January 2025 she sued him in federal court in Missouri, alleging that he sexually abused her beginning when she was 3 and he was 12 ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/openai-ceo-sam-altman-denies-sexual-abuse-allegations-made-sister-laws-rcna186759)). Sam, their mother and their brothers Jack and Max responded in a joint statement: "All of these claims are utterly untrue. This situation causes immense pain to our entire family" ([statement](https://x.com/sama/status/1876780763653263770)).

On March 20, 2026, the judge dismissed her common-law claims as time-barred and allowed an amended complaint, which she filed on April 1 under a Missouri statute for childhood sexual abuse claims ([Reuters, via U.S. News](https://www.usnews.com/news/top-news/articles/2026-03-20/judge-for-now-dismisses-lawsuit-by-sam-altmans-sister-accusing-openai-ceo-of-sexual-abuse)). As of September 30, 2026, the date of the latest docket entry, the case and Altman's defamation countersuit were active, a defense motion to dismiss was pending, and a December 2025 order had set a jury trial for May 3, 2027 ([court docket](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/69520118/altman-v-altman/)). The New Yorker wrote that it "could not substantiate Annie's account."

The allegation is unresolved, and it is not evidence for or against any personality type. An honest profile can't leave it out.

## Nostalgic for the present

On February 2, 2026, going "off-script for a minute" on X, he wrote that after building an app with OpenAI's Codex, he asked it for feature ideas, and "at least a couple of them were better than I was thinking of. I felt a little useless and it was sad." He added: "I am sure we will figure out much better and more interesting ways to spend our time, and amazing new ways to be useful to each other, but I am feeling nostalgic for the present" ([his post](https://x.com/sama/status/2018444309750862333)).

A Four reading hears wistfulness. A Three reading hears the sting of being outdone, by a product he sells, at something he was good at, and a choice to say so in public. Both fit the sentence. What he named was lost usefulness, the Three's fear, not the lost options a Seven would mourn.

Graham predicted that Altman would end up king of any island, and he has, more than once, though by The New Yorker's account Graham's own island was one where partners stopped trusting him. The 2026 apology describes the price of the same gift: rooms kept happy, fights put off, trust spent later. He told The New Yorker that he has changed: "Now I'm very happy to fire people quickly." That is a self-assessment, not yet a record. Whether he can lose a room on purpose is what the next few years will show.
