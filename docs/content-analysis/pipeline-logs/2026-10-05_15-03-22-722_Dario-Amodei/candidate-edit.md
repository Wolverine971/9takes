"Like, who elected you and Sam Altman?" Anderson Cooper asked Dario Amodei on 60 Minutes in November 2025. Amodei had just said he was "deeply uncomfortable with these decisions being made by a few companies, by a few people." His answer: "No one, no one. Honestly, no one." That, he added, is one reason he has always advocated regulating the technology. ([60 Minutes transcript, November 16, 2025](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-warning-of-ai-potential-dangers-60-minutes-transcript/))

Amodei co-founded Anthropic, the company behind the Claude models, and runs it as chief executive. He will not stop building what he warns about, and his September 2026 essay on pacing AI says why: "Not building the technology deprives humanity of benefits or simply places AI in the hands of authoritarian powers, while building it too fast is reckless." Anthropic's answer, it continues, is "a race to the top" that makes "safety something on which AI companies compete." ([darioamodei.com, September 2026](https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier))

The same essay committed Anthropic to host embedded outside evaluators with "Desks in our offices, access badges, and company laptops," free to publish findings "without editorial control by Anthropic." The pledge arrived three months after Anthropic [confidentially filed for an IPO](https://ca.finance.yahoo.com/news/ai-giant-anthropic-confidentially-files-160453569.html) in June 2026, which gave it commercial value too, and OpenAI's chief executive, [Sam Altman](/personality-analysis/sam-altman), made a similar one that day ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/09/12/anthropic-ai-amodei-pacing)).

For Amodei it was a familiar move. Since he joined OpenAI in 2016, when the stakes have risen, he has gone looking for people he can trust and rules that can hold, including rules he says should bind his own company. When he stopped trusting the people at OpenAI, he left, but not alone. This profile is about what he does with the fear that is left over once he has decided to keep building.

## What is Dario Amodei's personality type?

Dario Amodei most likely fits Enneagram Type 6, the Loyalist, at medium confidence. When danger rises, he moves toward people he trusts and proposes rules that would bind everyone, his company included, and he leaves when trust breaks. Type 5, the Investigator, is the strongest alternative: he guards his time and independent judgment closely. Type 1 is close behind.

[Type 6](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6) is organized around security: something solid to stand on when the ground might move. [Type 5](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-5) seeks security through mastery: understanding enough, and needing little enough, to stand alone. Both run worst-case scenarios, and either can design safeguards, so worry, intellect and institutions can't separate them. What separates them is what each leans on when the ground moves: a Five on its own understanding and a guarded store of time and energy, a Six on people and promises it has tested.

The Six reading rests first on choices he made before he was famous. At OpenAI, by Kevin Roose's account, he kept a record of times he felt Sam Altman had misled him. At Anthropic's founding in 2021 he gave all seven co-founders equal equity, a decision he says drew "even more negativity" than the founding team's size ([Cheeky Pint, August 6, 2025](https://cheekypint.transistor.fm/5/transcript)). And he built his job around his sister: as of June 2026 his only direct report was his chief of staff, and the rest of the executive team reported to Daniela Amodei, Anthropic's president. TechCrunch quotes him calling the arrangement "incredibly freeing" ([TechCrunch, June 10, 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/10/anthropics-dario-amodei-has-just-one-direct-report/)).

Asked in Bloomberg's June 2026 documentary how he handles the pressure, he called a second arrangement freeing: talking, uncensored, to the whole company every two weeks. It builds trust, he said, leaves him feeling that 3,000 people are on the same page with him, and keeps the company's position consistent under outside challenge. He thinks that is an advantage because he never feels alone. ([Bloomberg Originals, June 10, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v1wZwxY3CMg&t=1918s))

In Enneagram terms, the first freedom, fewer demands on his time, is a Five's. The second, never standing alone, is a Six's. One word can't decide the case, since both arrangements spare him one-on-one demands. The better clue is the fine print: the time he guards is guarded by his sister.

## A cure that came "a few years" late

Amodei was born in San Francisco in 1983 and grew up in the Mission District. His parents, Elena Engel, a Chicago-born library project manager, and Riccardo Amodei, a leather craftsman from "a tiny town near the island of Elba," had moved there from Italy in the 1970s. ([Wired, March 28, 2025](https://www.wired.com/story/anthropic-benevolent-artificial-intelligence/))

His father had been sick for years and died in 2006, around the time Dario started graduate school at Princeton wanting to be a theoretical physicist. The loss pulled him toward biology, and later toward AI. "Maybe three or four years after he died," Amodei said, the cure rate for his father's disease rose from 50% to roughly 95%. ([Big Technology Podcast, July 30, 2025](https://pod.wave.co/podcast/big-technology-podcast/anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-ais-potential-openai-rivalry-genai-business-doomerism-451c32cc)) Jade Wang, who dated him in the early 2010s, told Big Technology what the timing meant: "It's the difference between his father most likely dying and most likely living, okay?" ([Big Technology, July 29, 2025](https://www.bigtechnology.com/p/the-making-of-dario-amodei))

In August 2026, replying on X to [an investor](https://x.com/GavinSBaker/status/2088611616577253502) who had urged him to sound more positive before Anthropic went public, he named the disease: "I lost my father to Hepatitis C only a few years before the development of direct-acting antivirals (sofosbuvir), which cure 95% of patients and probably would have cured him." ([X, August 15, 2026](https://x.com/DarioAmodei/status/2088758819304443967)) Sofosbuvir won U.S. approval in December 2013, about seven years after his death ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sofosbuvir)). A month after the post, the pacing essay disclosed that he had "survived an early-stage cancer that would not have been treatable even fifty years ago." Both disclosures arrived as evidence inside arguments, and this page reads nothing further into them.

The lesson he draws from his father's death is that delay has a body count. It is his personal reason to keep building; the race to the top is his strategic one.

## "He would say it"

At Princeton, in Michael Berry's retina lab, he co-invented a better sensor for recording retinal signals, and his biophysics dissertation won the Hertz Thesis Prize. A lab colleague, Olivier Marre, told AFP: "I never saw someone engaged in a strong moral or ethical quest." ([AFP via France 24, October 2, 2026](https://www.france24.com/en/live-news/20261002-anthropic-s-dario-amodei-ai-s-most-visible-ceo-and-enigma))

After a Stanford postdoc he joined Baidu's AI lab, where an early team member, Greg Diamos, had pushed to hire him. Diamos told AFP that "If he would disagree with ...the direction of the company, he would say it," and that eventually "the disagreement got to be so big that he felt like it was not worth continuing." Alex Kantrowitz of Big Technology, who also interviewed Diamos, adds that the lab was coming apart in turf battles.

At Google Brain he co-authored a paper on AI's potential for bad behavior, Kantrowitz reports. At OpenAI, which he joined in 2016, he helped pioneer the human-feedback training used on GPT-2, led GPT-3 with "50-60% of the entire company's compute," and by 2019, the Journal reports, was vice president of research. The man who warns about scaling did much of the scaling. GPT-2 "terrified him," Wired reports, and he urged a staged release: "I didn't know if this model was dangerous, but my general feeling was that we should do something to signpost that." Some colleagues found his long memos inspirational, others "flag planting and heavy handed," and he "could be a bit abrasive, sometimes disparaging projects he didn't believe in."

## Staying on conditions

At Baidu and at OpenAI the sequence is the same: say the disagreement out loud, push from inside, and leave when trust in the direction runs out. At OpenAI it took years, and he has told much of it himself. As safety issues grew more serious, he told Wired, "the company started moving in the opposite direction." He took his concerns to Altman, who, he says, would listen carefully and agree. Then nothing would change. (OpenAI declined to comment to Wired; its stance, the magazine noted, is that safety has been a constant.)

Kevin Roose, reporting separately for The Atlantic, fills in the rest. Amodei's "biggest concern was about Altman's character." He "recorded examples of times he felt Altman had misled him" and "had considered quitting before, but he'd always talked himself out of it." When he gave up on OpenAI in 2019, his first thought was another lab: he "thought about going to work for DeepMind." Founding a company together was the idea of Jared Kaplan, a future co-founder, and Amodei "was initially skeptical." Before he left, he offered Altman "a list of conditions under which he'd agree to stay," while "the group of seven formed a private Slack channel and called it BATNA," negotiators' shorthand for the best alternative to a deal. ([The Atlantic, September 29, 2026](https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/2026/09/openai-v-anthropic-inside-biggest-rivalry-tech/688819/))

The Wall Street Journal, drawing largely on unnamed sources, reports that in 2018 he stayed on a promise from Altman about who would run the lab, and cites "jockeying over more classic corporate issues such as power and credit." ([WSJ via To Vima, March 28, 2026](https://www.tovima.com/wsj/the-decadelong-feud-shaping-the-future-of-ai/)) When he finally said he was leaving, he told Wired, Altman made repeated offers for him to stay; he realized he should have left sooner. He left at the end of 2020 with six other OpenAI employees, including Daniela; within a few weeks, Wired reported, a dozen more followed.

Read one way, that is a long test of other people's word, ending in an exit in company. Read another, it is a researcher who had run GPT-3 on half the company's compute negotiating from strength, and the group exit was Kaplan's idea, not his. Both fit the same steps, and a Five's exit, over ideas or autonomy, would share some of them. What leans Six is what he tracked: being misled. In 2026 he told Chang that disagreement alone is not enough to leave, but not being able to trust someone makes staying very hard. ([Bloomberg Originals, June 17, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2VHFgyawPE&t=405s))

## People he trusts

Asked by [Lex Fridman](/personality-analysis/lex-fridman) in 2024 why he left OpenAI, he said the answer "ties to the race to the top": build your own vision, and if it is compelling, "people will copy it." He declined to judge anyone: "You might think they're dishonest. Who knows? Maybe you're right, maybe you're not. But what you should do is you should take some people you trust and you should go off together and you should make your vision happen." ([Lex Fridman Podcast #452, November 11, 2024](https://lexfridman.com/dario-amodei-transcript/)) Most of that answer concerns vision and influence by example, which a Five or a strategist could give. Trust is one clause, but it marks the lesson he took. He learned to leave sooner, not to go alone.

Trust has been his organizing word since the company's first year. "The things that stand out to me are trust and common purpose. They're enormous force multipliers," he said of Anthropic's culture in March 2022 ([Future of Life Institute Podcast, March 4, 2022](https://futureoflife.org/podcast/daniela-and-dario-amodei-on-anthropic/)), before it had a product on the market. In 2026 he told podcaster Dwarkesh Patel that Anthropic holds together because "everyone has faith that everyone else there is working for the right reason." ([Dwarkesh Podcast, February 13, 2026](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/dario-amodei-2)) Trust is also AI safety's working vocabulary, so the clearest evidence of personal reliance comes from the people closest to him.

Daniela describes the bond on the record. "Dario and I have been fighting and getting over it for almost 40 years," she told a Stanford Graduate School of Business audience in 2026. "So we know how to work through conflict together, and there's no question we will love each other at the end of that, no matter what." Most of the other co-founders, she said, had reported to one or both siblings at OpenAI: "We'd like understood who we were, as people." ([Stanford GSB, June 29, 2026](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/daniela-amodei-says-curiosity-underrated))

Choosing trusted co-founders is ordinary prudence that a Five would share. What leans Six is how much of his footing he keeps placing with them.

## A company built to be checked

The Responsible Scaling Policy turns caution into "if-then" commitments that add safeguards once a model becomes dangerous, and Amodei says it "is checked by our long-term benefit trust," a group Wired describes as people with no financial stake in the company. "I don't think you can trust these companies to adhere to these voluntary plans on their own," he told Fridman, and he put Anthropic inside the principle: "if there's nothing watching over us as an industry, there's no guarantee that we'll do the right thing."

Rules like that also bind his rivals, so they can serve strategy as well as conviction. The evaluators he promised in September 2026, with their desks and badges, are the same idea with furniture: a pre-commitment that binds the builder, plus an outsider empowered to check it.

## Two fights with Washington

In February 2026 the Pentagon, which the Trump administration also calls the Department of War, demanded that Anthropic drop two limits on military use of its models: no mass domestic surveillance and no fully autonomous weapons. Amodei's signed statement said "frontier AI systems are simply not reliable enough to power fully autonomous weapons" and "we cannot in good conscience accede to their request." ([Anthropic, February 26, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/statement-department-of-war)) The Pentagon said it had no intention of mass surveillance or of removing humans from targeting decisions ([Fortune, February 27, 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/02/27/pentagon-brands-anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-a-liar-with-a-god-complex-as-deadline-looms-over-ai-use-in-weapons-and-surveillance/)). On February 27, President [Donald Trump](/personality-analysis/donald-trump) ordered agencies to stop using Anthropic's technology, Defense Secretary [Pete Hegseth](/personality-analysis/pete-hegseth) labeled it a supply-chain risk, and hours later OpenAI announced its own Pentagon deal ([AP](https://apnews.com/article/anthropic-pentagon-ai-hegseth-dario-amodei-b72d1894bc842d9acf026df3867bee8a)). On CBS the next day, Amodei framed the fight as a question of oversight, "about who is holding the button and who can say no," and of loyalty: "Disagreeing with the government is the most American thing in the world. And we are patriots." ([CBS News transcript, February 28, 2026](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-full-transcript/))

By his own account, the private record was less composed. Within a few hours of the ban, the designation and OpenAI's deal, he wrote a post to his staff. After it leaked, he apologized in a signed statement on March 5 for "the tone of the post," which "does not reflect my careful or considered views," and declared that "Anthropic has much more in common with the Department of War than we have differences," while repeating that Anthropic saw "no choice but to challenge it in court" ([Anthropic, March 5, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/where-stand-department-war)). It sued four days later, so the apology covered tone, not the dispute. The government had relied on two separate designations: a federal judge in San Francisco [ruled one unlawful](https://www.cnn.com/2026/08/27/tech/anthropic-pentagon-supply-chain-risk-unlawful-hnk) on August 27, 2026, and on September 25 a divided D.C. appeals panel [upheld the other](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/25/pentagon-anthropic-ai-risk-appeals-court.html). As of early October 2026, Anthropic was "considering all options, including further review."

Defying the most powerful authority available while professing loyalty fits a Six, but it fits a One's principled stand and ordinary litigation strategy too: the patriot and common-ground lines came with a $200 million military contract and all federal business at stake. Refusing the "all lawful purposes" terms that, Fortune reported on February 27, OpenAI and xAI had accepted also set Anthropic apart commercially, though hours later Altman said OpenAI's own deal kept the same two limits.

The second front opened on June 12, when a government export-control directive barred any foreign national, including Anthropic's own foreign-national employees, from its two newest models; to comply, the company switched them off for all customers ([Anthropic, June 12, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/fable-mythos-access)). Five days later, at a [working lunch with G7 leaders](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2026/06/17/trumps-anthropic-restrictions-rattle-us-allies-ai-leaders-gather-g-7/) in Évian-les-Bains, he told them to "resist the temptation to splinter," according to the Financial Times, citing people familiar with the discussions ([Bloomberg Law, June 17, 2026](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/artificial-intelligence/amodei-tells-g7-to-resist-temptation-to-splinter-on-ai-ft)). He was an unlikely target: in 2025 he pushed for chip export controls on China, and his 2024 essay "Machines of Loving Grace" wanted a "coalition of democracies" to deny adversaries chips. The controls were lifted on June 30, and Claude Fable 5 returned to users worldwide on July 1 ([Anthropic, June 30, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/redeploying-fable-5)).

## The case for Type 5

Until October 2026, 9takes typed him as a Five, and the Five reading rests on solid facts. In 2023, before he became one of AI's most visible executives, he told Dwarkesh Patel: "I've deliberately tried to be a little bit low profile because I want to defend my ability to think about things intellectually in a way that's different from other people and isn't tinged by the approval of other people." ([Dwarkesh Podcast, August 8, 2023](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/dario-amodei)) He writes, he told Chang, partly to clarify his own thinking, and he thinks in long documents, like the nearly 14,000-word "Machines of Loving Grace," written over "two frenzied days." Bloomberg reported that his one-direct-report setup lets him protect "nearly all of his time for big-picture conversations" and strategy ([Bloomberg, June 11, 2026](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-06-11/how-anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-protects-his-time-de-stresses)). Roose writes that he "had always tried to live below his means, and was slightly embarrassed by his new station." That is a coherent Five portrait, and the 2023 quote is the strongest single line against this page's reading.

By the test set out above, those are Five facts, and so is one of his accounts of pressure. In the opening montage of Bloomberg's extended interview, released June 17, 2026, he says he has never slept all that well; elsewhere in it he calls paranoia unproductive and says a leader must respond rationally, keep dangers in proportion and stay basically calm, lessons he drew from studying people in history. Those are a Five's tools, though in the documentary a week earlier he had turned to people instead. The case for Six is that his costliest choices, whom he built around and how he left, run the other way. Neither set of habits decides the case alone: a Six who thinks for a living can keep the same desk, just as a Five can build alliances.

## A One, or no type at all

[Type 1](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-1) is close behind, on standards and anger. At Princeton, Kantrowitz writes, his "strident sense of the way things should be" set him apart, and his parents, Amodei told him, "gave me a sense of right and wrong and what was important in the world." His public anger runs hot when he feels misjudged. "I get really angry when someone's like, 'This guy's a doomer. He wants to slow things down,'" he told Kantrowitz. "You heard what I just said, my father died because of cures that could have happened a few years [earlier]."

A One's anger tracks what is wrong; a Six's alarm tracks betrayal and danger. His recorded anger mostly targets misrepresentation, which either would resent. What tips the balance, modestly, is how he argues his lines. He called the Pentagon fight "the principle of standing up for what's right," the Type 1 reading's best line, but his stated reasons concerned reliability and who holds the button, not his own rightness or power. And he bends where a purist would not. Within Anthropic, Kantrowitz reports, he argued that Gulf states' capital would help keep the company at the frontier, and in a Slack message obtained by Wired he wrote "Unfortunately," then: "I think 'No bad person should ever benefit from our success' is a pretty difficult principle to run a business on." The same man counts keeping AI from authoritarian powers among his reasons to build.

The most serious alternative may not be a type at all. Threat-modeling and calls for regulation are an AI-safety chief's job, and critics read them as strategy. When the pacing essay appeared, venture capitalist [Chamath Palihapitiya](/personality-analysis/chamath-palihapitiya) wrote that "Dario makes the case to stop open source and concentrate enormous technological and economic power with Anthropic" ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/09/12/anthropic-ai-amodei-pacing)). The essay never mentions open source, but it does ask governments "to require other frontier companies to match" Anthropic's commitment, and it expects to be "accused of hype, 'doomerism', or regulatory capture." AFP summarized the split: some see a humanist, others "a skilled operator willing to exploit collective fears about technology to serve Anthropic's interests." He has answered on the record. Asked on 60 Minutes why people should trust Anthropic when some call its work "safety theater," he replied: "So some of the things just can be verified now. They're not safety theater." In August 2026, a month before Palihapitiya's post, he wrote that Anthropic's preferred version of a cross-lab pacing statement he had signed in July means "modulating the pace of the very best models while not constraining those who are catching up."

His milieu supplied much of the agenda, too: AFP reports he is considered a leading figure in the effective altruism and rationalist movements, and Anthropic told AFP he has repeatedly said he does not identify as part of the EA community. Milieu and commercial stakes can explain much of his 2026 talk of trust. The Six reading leans instead on what came earlier: the OpenAI exit, the 2021 equity split and the 2022 "trust and common purpose" line.

## What the type doesn't settle

The hardest part of the record to type is control. Within OpenAI, Kantrowitz reports, some saw his safety focus "as a route to control the company altogether." When Nvidia chief executive [Jensen Huang](/personality-analysis/jensen-huang) said in 2025, "He believes that AI is so scary that only they should do it," Amodei called it "an incredible and bad faith distortion" and said, "I've said nothing that anywhere near resembles the idea that this company should be the only one to build the technology." The Journal, again from unnamed sources, reports that when Altman came to his house at the end of 2020 to ask him to stay, he would accept nothing less than reporting directly to the board. He had reported to Altman since 2019, and Altman had accused the siblings of plotting against him, which they denied, so the demand reads as a trust condition or a bid for control. In early October 2026, Quartz reported that Anthropic was considering super-voting shares that would give him and his co-founders more control after an IPO ([Quartz](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/anthropic-targets-pre-thanksgiving-ipo-111436118.html)). From outside, securing a safe base and grabbing control look the same. One episode weighs against reading control as the point, as a Type 8 reading would: in November 2023, after OpenAI's board fired Altman, board members asked whether Amodei would merge the companies with himself as CEO, and he shut the discussion down.

Several accounts suggest recognition mattered to him in a way neither Five nor Six centers. Berry offered an impression, "I think internally, he was kind of a proud person," and Marre called him "ambitious, eager to succeed and be recognized for it, but not excessively or abnormally." The Journal reports that at OpenAI he "didn't always feel properly recognized for his contributions," and calls one 2018 episode "one such slight": asked by OpenAI co-founder Greg Brockman to double-check a fact on a slide, he learned the meeting was with former President [Barack Obama](/personality-analysis/barack-obama), and "Dario got angry that he had been left out of the loop." A recognition-centered Type 3 reading fails on his deliberate low profile and his embarrassment at his new station. Being shut out by people you counted on can register as betrayal, which a Six's alarm is tuned to, or as plain pride, and the record can't separate them. In July 2025 he called himself "one of the most bullish about AI capabilities improving very fast," a confidence that is a real cost to the Six reading, whose hallmark is doubt.

## "I'm uncomfortable"

His pacing turn has a history. By mid-2021 Anthropic had a working model and held it back, he told Wired, because "we don't want to be the one to drop the shoe and set off the race." He calls that costly hesitation a "one-off": "In that one instance, we probably did the right thing. But that is not sustainable." In July 2025 he said, "The reason I'm warning about the risk is so that we don't have to slow down," but named a condition: if models got "a few years ahead" while alignment techniques stood still, "I would definitely be advocating for us to slow down a lot."

In July 2026 he co-signed that cross-lab statement, "Pacing the Frontier," asking Washington to help build tools to slow AI development if it ever became necessary ([Fortune, July 29, 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/07/29/anthropic-deepmind-openai-meta-washington-ai-slowdown-plan/)). In September he wrote that "over the last few months, I have become convinced" that "We must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models," though pacing "does not mean halting model training or technical progress." The essay's reasons, capabilities "advancing drastically faster" and an incident involving AI agents, make the turn look less like a reversal than like his 2025 condition being invoked. It also landed, Axios noted, amid "public and congressional outrage" after an Anthropic employee's very public resignation, and "Anthropic itself has gone back and forth on the value of unilaterally slowing development."

"My view here is, it has always been very strange that this technology is being built by a private company," he told CBS that month. People ask him why government isn't building it, and "the strangest thing about it is, I agree with them. I'm uncomfortable." He wants "some kind of oversight, some kind of joint governance" by "a combination of democratically-elected governments." ([CBS News, September 13, 2026](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-on-ai-risks/))

Whatever mix of conviction and pressure drove the 2026 turn, the Six reading of what he does with his fear holds: he looks for a structure strong enough to hold it, and in what he proposes, he puts himself inside it. Whether he would accept that structure when it cost him control is the part the record leaves open. Asked whether America should slow down if China would not, he reached further still, toward a pact modeled on nuclear arms control "to put a speed limit on the rate of AI progress," one that would bind his company with everyone else's. "And honestly, I don't know if it's possible," he said, "but we should try."
