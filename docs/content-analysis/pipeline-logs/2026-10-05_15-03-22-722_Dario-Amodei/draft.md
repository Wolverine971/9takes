"Like, who elected you and Sam Altman?" Anderson Cooper asked Dario Amodei on 60 Minutes in November 2025. Amodei had just said he was "deeply uncomfortable with these decisions being made by a few companies, by a few people." His answer: "No one, no one. Honestly, no one." That, he added, is one reason he has always advocated regulating the technology. ([60 Minutes transcript, November 16, 2025](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-warning-of-ai-potential-dangers-60-minutes-transcript/))

Amodei co-founded Anthropic, the company behind the Claude models, and runs it as chief executive. He will not stop building what he warns about, and his September 2026 essay on pacing AI says why: "Not building the technology deprives humanity of benefits or simply places AI in the hands of authoritarian powers, while building it too fast is reckless." ([darioamodei.com, September 2026](https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier))

The same essay committed Anthropic to host embedded outside evaluators with "Desks in our offices, access badges, and company laptops," and the right to publish findings "without editorial control by Anthropic," save a narrow right to redact sensitive material. The problem they would fix, he wrote: "we are still the ones choosing what to include and omit." The pledge doubled as a credential for a company that had [confidentially filed for an IPO](https://ca.finance.yahoo.com/news/ai-giant-anthropic-confidentially-files-160453569.html) in June, and OpenAI's chief executive, [Sam Altman](/personality-analysis/sam-altman), made a similar one that day ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/09/12/anthropic-ai-amodei-pacing)).

For Amodei it was a familiar move. For a decade, when the stakes have risen, he has gone looking for people he can trust and rules that can hold, including rules that bind him. When he stopped trusting the people at OpenAI, he left, but not alone. This profile is about what he does with the fear that is left over once he has decided to keep building.

## What is Dario Amodei's personality type?

Dario Amodei most likely fits Enneagram Type 6, the Loyalist, at medium confidence. When danger rises, he moves toward people he trusts and rules that bind everyone, himself included, and he leaves when trust breaks. Type 5, the Investigator, is the strongest alternative: he guards his time and his independent judgment closely. Type 1 is a credible third.

[Type 6](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6) is organized around security: something solid to stand on when the ground might move. [Type 5](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-5) seeks security through mastery: understanding enough, and needing little enough, to stand alone. Both run worst-case scenarios, and either can design safeguards, so worry, intellect and institutions can't separate them. What separates them is what each leans on when the ground moves: a Five on its own understanding and a guarded store of time and energy, a Six on people and promises it has tested.

His own vocabulary marks the fork. In 2026 he told Bloomberg's Emily Chang that he has a single direct report, an arrangement TechCrunch quotes him calling "incredibly freeing" ([TechCrunch, June 10, 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/10/anthropics-dario-amodei-has-just-one-direct-report/)). Asked in Bloomberg's documentary how he handles the pressure, he used the word again about something else: talking, "totally uncensored," to the whole company every two weeks. He said that "not only does it build trust, it's very freeing for me, where I feel that I have 3,000 people who are on the same page as me." It is, he added, "an incredible advantage because I never feel like I'm alone." ([Bloomberg Originals, June 10, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v1wZwxY3CMg&t=1918s))

In Enneagram terms, the first freedom, fewer demands on his time, is a Five's. The second, never standing alone, is a Six's. This page reads the second as the deeper one, because it governs the choices that have cost him most: whom he trusts, when he leaves and where he turns when he is afraid. Until this October 2026 revision, this page typed him as a Five.

## A cure that came "a few years" late

Amodei was born in San Francisco in 1983 and grew up in the Mission District. His parents, Elena Engel, a Chicago-born library project manager, and Riccardo Amodei, a leather craftsman from "a tiny town near the island of Elba," had moved there from Italy in the 1970s. Daniela, his sister and future co-founder, was born four years after him. ([Wired, March 28, 2025](https://www.wired.com/story/anthropic-benevolent-artificial-intelligence/))

His father had been sick for years and died in 2006, around the time Dario started graduate school at Princeton wanting to be a theoretical physicist. The loss pulled him toward biology, and later toward AI. "Maybe three or four years after he died," Amodei said, the cure rate for his father's disease rose from 50% to roughly 95%. ([Big Technology Podcast, July 30, 2025](https://pod.wave.co/podcast/big-technology-podcast/anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-ais-potential-openai-rivalry-genai-business-doomerism-451c32cc)) Jade Wang, who dated him in the early 2010s, told Big Technology what the timing meant: "It's the difference between his father most likely dying and most likely living, okay?" ([Big Technology, July 29, 2025](https://www.bigtechnology.com/p/the-making-of-dario-amodei))

In August 2026, replying on X to [an investor](https://x.com/GavinSBaker/status/2088611616577253502) who had urged him to sound more positive before Anthropic went public, he named the disease: "I lost my father to Hepatitis C only a few years before the development of direct-acting antivirals (sofosbuvir), which cure 95% of patients and probably would have cured him." ([X, August 15, 2026](https://x.com/DarioAmodei/status/2088758819304443967)) A month later the pacing essay disclosed that he had "survived an early-stage cancer that would not have been treatable even fifty years ago." Both disclosures arrived as evidence inside arguments, and this page reads nothing further into them.

The lesson of his father's death is that delay has a body count. By his own account it is why he keeps building, though not his only reason; the pacing essay adds keeping AI from authoritarian powers.

## "He would say it"

At Princeton, in Michael Berry's retina lab, he co-invented a better sensor for recording retinal signals, and his biophysics dissertation won the Hertz Thesis Prize. A lab colleague, Olivier Marre, told AFP: "I never saw someone engaged in a strong moral or ethical quest." ([AFP via France 24, October 2, 2026](https://www.france24.com/en/live-news/20261002-anthropic-s-dario-amodei-ai-s-most-visible-ceo-and-enigma))

After a Stanford postdoc he joined Baidu's AI lab, where an early team member, Greg Diamos, had pushed to hire him. There he "saw these very smooth trends" as models grew, the pattern later called scaling laws. Diamos told AFP that "If he would disagree with ...the direction of the company, he would say it," and that eventually "the disagreement got to be so big that he felt like it was not worth continuing." Alex Kantrowitz of Big Technology, who also interviewed Diamos, adds that the lab was coming apart in turf battles.

At OpenAI, which he joined in 2016 after a stint at Google Brain, he became a research director and led GPT-3 with "50-60% of the entire company's compute," Kantrowitz reports. The man who warns about scaling did much of the scaling. Of the scaling theory he wrote up there, "My first thought," he told Wired, "was, oh my God, could systems that are smarter than humans figure out how to destabilize the nuclear deterrent?" Colleagues split on him: his long documents about values and technology struck some as inspirational and others as "flag planting and heavy handed," and he "could be a bit abrasive, sometimes disparaging projects he didn't believe in."

## Staying on conditions

At Baidu and at OpenAI the sequence is the same: say the disagreement out loud, push from inside, and leave when trust in the direction runs out. At OpenAI it took years, and he has told much of it himself. As safety issues grew more serious, he told Wired, "the company started moving in the opposite direction." He took his concerns to Altman, who, he says, would listen carefully and agree. Then nothing would change. (OpenAI declined to comment to Wired; its stance, the magazine noted, is that safety has been a constant.)

Kevin Roose, reporting separately for The Atlantic, fills in the rest. Amodei "recorded examples of times he felt Altman had misled him" and "had considered quitting before, but he'd always talked himself out of it." When he gave up on OpenAI in 2019, his first thought was another lab: he "thought about going to work for DeepMind." Founding a company together was the idea of Jared Kaplan, a future co-founder, and Amodei "was initially skeptical." Before he left, he offered Altman "a list of conditions under which he'd agree to stay," while "the group of seven formed a private Slack channel and called it BATNA," negotiators' shorthand for the best alternative to a deal. ([The Atlantic, September 29, 2026](https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/2026/09/openai-v-anthropic-inside-biggest-rivalry-tech/688819/))

The Wall Street Journal, drawing largely on unnamed sources, reports an earlier round in 2018: "Dario agreed to stay so long as Altman promised that Brockman and Sutskever wouldn't be in charge," then learned of a promise he felt conflicted with that deal. (Greg Brockman and Ilya Sutskever were OpenAI co-founders.) The Journal also cites "jockeying over more classic corporate issues such as power and credit." ([WSJ via To Vima, March 28, 2026](https://www.tovima.com/wsj/the-decadelong-feud-shaping-the-future-of-ai/)) The conditions may have been about turf as much as trust. Either way they kept one form: guarantees from people, enforced by leaving.

When he finally said he was leaving, he told Wired, Altman made repeated offers for him to stay; he realized he should have left sooner. He left at the end of 2020 with six other OpenAI employees, including Daniela; within a few weeks, Wired reported, a dozen more followed.

That is a long test of other people's word, ending in an exit in company. Each step is a bet on people rather than a retreat into his own understanding. In 2026 he stated the rule. Disagreement "alone is not sufficient to leave," he told Chang, but feeling that "you can't trust someone" makes staying very hard. ([Bloomberg Originals, June 17, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2VHFgyawPE&t=405s))

## People he trusts

Asked by [Lex Fridman](/personality-analysis/lex-fridman) in 2024 why he left OpenAI, he declined to judge anyone: "You might think they're not doing it the right way. You might think they're dishonest. Who knows? Maybe you're right, maybe you're not. But what you should do is you should take some people you trust and you should go off together and you should make your vision happen." ([Lex Fridman Podcast #452, November 11, 2024](https://lexfridman.com/dario-amodei-transcript/)) Read as a Five, that advice is independence: go build your own system. What survives his refusal to judge is trust, in the people he takes and the path he wants. He learned to leave sooner, not to go alone.

Trust has been his organizing word since the company's first year. "The things that stand out to me are trust and common purpose. They're enormous force multipliers," he said of Anthropic's culture in March 2022. ([Future of Life Institute Podcast, March 4, 2022](https://futureoflife.org/podcast/daniela-and-dario-amodei-on-anthropic/)) The seven co-founders got equal equity, a choice he says drew even more negativity than the headcount, and he credits their shared history with letting them "always be on the same page," the phrase he would use for his whole company the next year. ([Cheeky Pint, August 6, 2025](https://cheekypint.transistor.fm/5/transcript)) In 2026 he told podcaster Dwarkesh Patel that Anthropic holds together because "everyone has faith that everyone else there is working for the right reason." ([Dwarkesh Podcast, February 13, 2026](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/dario-amodei-2))

Daniela describes the bond on the record. "Dario and I have been fighting and getting over it for almost 40 years," she told a Stanford Graduate School of Business audience in 2026. "So we know how to work through conflict together, and there's no question we will love each other at the end of that, no matter what." Most of the other co-founders, she said, had reported to one or both siblings at OpenAI: "We'd like understood who we were, as people." ([Stanford GSB, June 29, 2026](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/daniela-amodei-says-curiosity-underrated))

Choosing trusted co-founders is ordinary prudence that a Five would share. What stands out is how long trust has stayed his central word.

## A company built to be checked

The Responsible Scaling Policy turns caution into "if-then" commitments that add safeguards once a model becomes dangerous, and Amodei says it "is checked by our long-term benefit trust," a group Wired describes as people with no financial stake in the company. "I don't think you can trust these companies to adhere to these voluntary plans on their own," he told Fridman, and he put Anthropic inside the principle: "if there's nothing watching over us as an industry, there's no guarantee that we'll do the right thing."

He sorts the field by trust and wants rules for everyone else. "There are some players who are more trustworthy than others," he told Chang, and "the trustworthy actors need to get together and put the untrustworthy actors in a position where they kind of have to adopt the same standards." ([Bloomberg Originals, June 17, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2VHFgyawPE&t=542s)) In August 2026 he wrote that "At their best, institutions can vest power in ideas rather than people, and thereby decentralize that power." ([X, August 15, 2026](https://x.com/DarioAmodei/status/2088758816376807762)) Rules like that also bind his rivals, so they are strategy as well as conviction. The evaluators he promised in September 2026, with their desks and badges, are the same idea with furniture: a pre-commitment that binds the builder, plus an outsider empowered to check it.

## "Then I speak up louder"

In Enneagram theory, a Five meets rising danger by pulling back to think. Amodei, in his own account, gets louder. "As we get closer and closer to the more powerful models, I'm speaking up more and more and I'm taking more and more drastic actions," he said on the Big Technology Podcast in July 2025, and when safety work falls behind, "then I speak up louder," though "Every time I do that, I get attacked by many of my peers." Worry, in his telling, is a reason to prepare: "I worry a lot about the unknowns. I don't think we can predict everything for sure. But precisely because of that, we're trying to predict everything we can," he told 60 Minutes.

Call him a doomer and the composure goes. "I get really angry when someone's like, 'This guy's a doomer. He wants to slow things down,'" he told Kantrowitz. "You heard what I just said, my father died because of cures that could have happened a few years [earlier]." When Nvidia chief executive [Jensen Huang](/personality-analysis/jensen-huang) said in 2025, "He believes that AI is so scary that only they should do it," Amodei called it "an incredible and bad faith distortion."

His public anger targets dishonesty and bad faith, often about his own motives. In Enneagram terms, a Five's usual answer to a broken bond is distance. His is heat, and then repair, as the worst week of his 2026 showed.

## Two fights with Washington

In February 2026 the Pentagon demanded that Anthropic drop two limits on military use of its models: no mass domestic surveillance and no fully autonomous weapons. Amodei's signed statement said "frontier AI systems are simply not reliable enough to power fully autonomous weapons" and "we cannot in good conscience accede to their request." ([Anthropic, February 26, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/statement-department-of-war)) The Pentagon said it had no intention of mass surveillance or of removing humans from targeting decisions ([Fortune, February 27, 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/02/27/pentagon-brands-anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-a-liar-with-a-god-complex-as-deadline-looms-over-ai-use-in-weapons-and-surveillance/)). The next day President [Donald Trump](/personality-analysis/donald-trump) ordered agencies to stop using Anthropic's technology, and Defense Secretary [Pete Hegseth](/personality-analysis/pete-hegseth) labeled it a supply-chain risk ([AP](https://apnews.com/article/anthropic-pentagon-ai-hegseth-dario-amodei-b72d1894bc842d9acf026df3867bee8a)). On CBS, Amodei framed the fight as a question of oversight, "about who is holding the button and who can say no," and of loyalty: "Disagreeing with the government is the most American thing in the world. And we are patriots." ([CBS News transcript, February 28, 2026](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-full-transcript/))

The private record was less composed. Within a few hours of the ban, the designation and OpenAI's announcement of its own Pentagon deal, he wrote a post to his staff. After it leaked, he apologized in a signed statement for "the tone of the post," which "does not reflect my careful or considered views," and declared that "Anthropic has much more in common with the Department of War than we have differences." ([Anthropic, March 5, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/where-stand-department-war)) Four days after the apology, Anthropic sued. The courts have since [ruled one of the Pentagon's two designations unlawful](https://www.cnn.com/2026/08/27/tech/anthropic-pentagon-supply-chain-risk-unlawful-hnk) and [upheld the other](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/25/pentagon-anthropic-ai-risk-appeals-court.html); as of early October 2026, Anthropic was "considering all options, including further review."

Defiance of the most powerful authority available, paired with a pledge of loyalty, is what Enneagram teachers call the counterphobic Six, who meets fear by moving toward it. He also called the fight "the principle of standing up for what's right," the Type 1 reading's best line, but his lines concerned reliability and who holds the button, not his own rightness or power. The commercial reading is live too: Fortune reported on February 27 that OpenAI and xAI had accepted Pentagon terms allowing "all lawful purposes," so refusing them set Anthropic apart, though hours later Altman said OpenAI's own deal kept the same two limits.

The second front opened in June, when an export-control directive forced Anthropic to switch off its newest models for all customers. The company asked for "a statutory process that is transparent, fair, clear, and grounded in technical facts," not an exemption ([Anthropic, June 12, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/fable-mythos-access)). Five days later, at a [working lunch with G7 leaders](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2026/06/17/trumps-anthropic-restrictions-rattle-us-allies-ai-leaders-gather-g-7/) in Évian-les-Bains, he told them to "resist the temptation to splinter," according to the Financial Times, citing people familiar with the discussions ([Bloomberg Law, June 17, 2026](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/artificial-intelligence/amodei-tells-g7-to-resist-temptation-to-splinter-on-ai-ft)). It was the "coalition of democracies" his 2024 essay "Machines of Loving Grace" had proposed, and by the Journal's account he had hired an adviser to pitch a coordination regime as early as 2017.

## The case for Type 5

The Five reading, which this page held until October 2026, rests on solid facts. In 2023, before he became one of AI's most visible executives, he told Dwarkesh Patel: "I've deliberately tried to be a little bit low profile because I want to defend my ability to think about things intellectually in a way that's different from other people and isn't tinged by the approval of other people." ([Dwarkesh Podcast, August 8, 2023](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/dario-amodei)) He writes, he told Bloomberg, "to clarify my own thinking so that I kind of know what to do next," and he thinks in long documents, like the nearly 14,000-word "Machines of Loving Grace," written over "two frenzied days." Bloomberg described his one-direct-report setup as one in which "the CEO protects nearly all of his time for big-picture conversations, organizational culture, and giving input on research direction and strategy" ([Bloomberg, June 11, 2026](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-06-11/how-anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-protects-his-time-de-stresses)). Roose writes that he "had always tried to live below his means, and was slightly embarrassed by his new station." That is a coherent Five portrait, and the 2023 quote is the strongest single line against this page's reading.

Two things keep Six ahead. The guarded resources go to a group: he said in 2026 that "a third, maybe 40%" of his time goes to "making sure the culture of Anthropic is good," and that he writes also "to create a common reference point across me and others." And what he guards is his judgment, not his footing. When the ground moved, at OpenAI, in the Pentagon fight and under the pressure of 2026, he fell back on people, and said so.

Neither set of habits decides the case alone: a Six who thinks for a living can keep the same desk, just as a Five can build alliances.

A document-first, time-guarding style is not evidence of any diagnosis, including autism. None of the interviews, essays, profiles and posts reviewed for this page describe any such diagnosis, and this page infers none.

## A One, or no type at all

[Type 1](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-1) is a credible third. His parents "gave me a sense of right and wrong and what was important in the world," he told Kantrowitz, and as a Caltech undergraduate in 2003 he complained in print that most people were "opposed in principle but refuse to give one millisecond of their time" on the coming Iraq war. Against it stand Marre's testimony and a pragmatism a purist would resist: in a Slack message obtained by Wired, he called "No bad person should ever benefit from our success" "a pretty difficult principle to run a business on." His moral lines usually arrive through reliability and trust rather than through his own rightness.

The most serious alternative may not be a type at all. Threat-modeling and calls for regulation are an AI-safety chief's job, and critics read them as strategy. When the pacing essay appeared, venture capitalist [Chamath Palihapitiya](/personality-analysis/chamath-palihapitiya) wrote that "Dario makes the case to stop open source and concentrate enormous technological and economic power with Anthropic" ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/09/12/anthropic-ai-amodei-pacing)), though the essay never mentions open source. AFP summarized the split: some see a humanist, others "a skilled operator willing to exploit collective fears about technology to serve Anthropic's interests."

His milieu supplied much of the agenda, too. In the Journal's account of a 2016 debate, he argued alongside Holden Karnofsky, Daniela's then-fiancé and the founder of a philanthropy promoting effective altruism, that on how fast AI was developing, "it would be better to tell the government first." Anthropic told AFP he has repeatedly said he does not identify as part of the EA community. A movement can supply threat models and a coordination agenda. It does not decide whom he trusts, when he walks out, or what makes him angry, and those choices carry this page's type argument.

## What the type doesn't settle

The hardest part of the record to type is control. Within OpenAI, Kantrowitz reports, some saw his safety focus "as a route to control the company altogether," and before he left, per the Journal, he would accept nothing less than reporting directly to the board. In early October 2026, Quartz reported that Anthropic was considering super-voting shares that would give him and his co-founders more control after an IPO ([Quartz](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/anthropic-targets-pre-thanksgiving-ipo-111436118.html)). From outside, securing a safe base and grabbing control look the same. One episode weighs against reading control as the point, as a Type 8 reading would: in November 2023, after OpenAI's board fired Altman, board members asked whether Amodei would merge the companies with himself as CEO, and he shut the discussion down.

Several accounts suggest recognition mattered to him in a way neither Five nor Six centers. Berry, whose lab he worked in, called him "kind of a proud person." The Journal reports that at OpenAI he "didn't always feel properly recognized for his contributions," and that in 2018, when Brockman mentioned that he and Altman were going to meet former President [Barack Obama](/personality-analysis/barack-obama), "Dario got angry that he had been left out of the loop." Being shut out by people you counted on can register as betrayal, which a Six's alarm is tuned to, or as plain pride, and the record can't separate them. He also calls himself "one of the most bullish about AI capabilities improving very fast," a confidence that is a real cost to the Six reading, whose hallmark is doubt.

## "I'm uncomfortable"

His pacing turn came in steps. In July 2026 he and several Anthropic co-founders signed "Pacing the Frontier," a statement from employees across the leading labs asking Washington to help build the tools to slow AI development if it ever became necessary ([Fortune, July 29, 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/07/29/anthropic-deepmind-openai-meta-washington-ai-slowdown-plan/)). In September he wrote that "over the last few months, I have become convinced" that "We must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models," though pacing "does not mean halting model training or technical progress." He presented the change as events outrunning his expectations: "I don't think I fully just appreciated what it would actually be like when the progress was as fast as it was," he told CBS ([CBS News, September 13, 2026](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-on-ai-risks/)).

"My view here is, it has always been very strange that this technology is being built by a private company," he said in the same interview. "People ask me that question all the time: Why isn't this being built by government? And the strangest thing about it is, I agree with them. I'm uncomfortable." He wants "some kind of oversight, some kind of joint governance" by "a combination of democratically-elected governments."

The Six pattern is this page's reading of what he does with his fear of the thing he builds: he looks for a structure strong enough to hold it, and in what he proposes, he puts himself inside it. Whether he would accept that structure when it cost him control is the part the record leaves open. Asked whether America should slow down if China would not, he reached further still, toward a pact modeled on nuclear arms control "to put a speed limit on the rate of AI progress," one that would bind his company with everyone else's. "And honestly, I don't know if it's possible," he said, "but we should try."
