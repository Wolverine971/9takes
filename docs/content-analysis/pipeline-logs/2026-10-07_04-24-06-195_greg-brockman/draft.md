In July 2017, Greg Brockman, OpenAI's co-founder and chief technology officer, began a machine learning project of his own, using "behavioral cloning to teach a neural network from human training data" for the lab's Dota bot. He kept finding "small bugs that had been corrupting my results the whole time." "I didn't feel confident in my work, but to make it worse, other people did," he wrote. "I always made sure to correct them by pointing out that I was a newbie."

His code made it into the bot for a while. Then a colleague, Jie Tang, "figured out how to get better results without using my code, and I had nothing to show for my efforts." The next line sits alone as its own paragraph: "I never tried machine learning on the Dota project again." ([Greg Brockman, "How I became a machine learning practitioner," July 30, 2019](https://blog.gregbrockman.com/how-i-became-a-machine-learning-practitioner)) For OpenAI's "first three years," he admitted, he "dreamed of becoming a machine learning expert but made little progress towards that goal." The blocker, he decided, was "a mental barrier": "getting ok with being a beginner again."

Brockman, born in 1987 and raised in North Dakota, was Stripe's fourth employee and first CTO before he co-founded OpenAI in 2015. He is its president. He quit within hours of [Sam Altman](/personality-analysis/sam-altman)'s firing on November 17, 2023, and returned with him days later. Forbes named him the richest newcomer to its 2026 Forbes 400, worth an estimated $25.5 billion as of September 4, 2026 ([Forbes, September 15, 2026](https://www.forbes.com.au/?p=206622)). Interest spiked again after Luca Guadagnino's _Artificial_ premiered at the New York Film Festival on October 5, 2026, with Cooper Hoffman as Brockman and [Andrew Garfield](/personality-analysis/andrew-garfield) as Altman ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_%282026_film%29)).

Read in order, his own essays show a man who gives up titles with unusual ease. The one stretch where he describes himself stuck for years is the one where he had nothing to point to. This profile argues that the pattern fits Enneagram Type 3, with Type 1 as a serious rival.

## What is Greg Brockman's personality type?

Greg Brockman most likely fits Enneagram Type 3, the Achiever, at medium confidence. Across two decades, his costliest choices protect visible, shipped results and how his competence registers with others, more than titles or procedure. Type 1, the Reformer, is the strongest alternative, and Type 9, the Peacemaker, is a credible third.

[Type 3](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-3) is organized around feeling valuable through accomplishment other people can see. Brockman's version runs light on glamour. He rarely fights for titles, and after Altman became CEO in 2019 he took "a less public-facing role," as Fortune put it. The currency in his writing is output, and other people's reaction to it. A [Type 1](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-1) reading explains much of the same record through his moral vocabulary and exacting standards; a [Type 9](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-9) reading fits how he now describes his own indecision. The question that separates the Three from the One is practical: when the right way and the fast way collide, which does he protect?

This is a reading of the public record. It claims no access to his mind and makes no clinical judgment. Several of the most vivid accounts of him come from anonymous former colleagues or from rivals, and they are labeled that way.

## "I felt like I could be best in the world"

Nothing in the record points to a formative wound, and this profile won't supply one. In 2007 the Grand Forks Herald reported that his parents were both physicians, that he had completed every math course his high school offered, and that he had just become North Dakota's first Intel Science Talent Search finalist since 1973 ([Grand Forks Herald, February 3, 2007](https://www.grandforksherald.com/newsmd/greg-brockman-intel-science-talent-search-finalist-grand-forks)). He described a "focused, quiet childhood" to MIT Technology Review, which later noted, in a correction OpenAI requested, that he grew up "on a hobby farm," not a farm: "Brockman considers this distinction important." ([MIT Technology Review, February 17, 2020](https://www.technologyreview.com/2020/02/17/844721/ai-openai-moonshot-elon-musk-sam-altman-greg-brockman-messy-secretive-reality/))

How he explains his choices says more. He told Ben Thompson in September 2026 that he thought he might be "an actor all the way through ninth grade," then decided to "double down on something, I felt like I could be best in the world and really try to move the needle in a field." He chose the hard sciences "because I felt like maybe that was an area where I could most make a difference in the world." ([Stratechery, September 4, 2026](https://stratechery.com/2026/an-interview-with-openai-president-greg-brockman-about-astra-and-alignment/))

His first paid job came from an audition for extras at a Mannheim Steamroller concert in Grand Forks. While the college students chatted during breaks, "I was there standing at attention, just being in character." He wasn't picked, but the organizers told him, "We loved seeing how much you were dedicated to this, so we're going to actually make a new role for you." He became a gingerbread man. North Dakota, he said, made it "possible for me to really excel and be the best in the state."

Gifted kids of every type win contests, and a 2026 podcast story has had decades of polish. What stands out is his logic. He picked a field by where he could be best, and his favorite early story is about dedication that someone noticed and rewarded with a role built for him. He also says he loved acting for "the group aspect of it," a people-first note that recurs across his career.

## From wake until sleep

After a gap year spent writing a chemistry textbook, he learned to code to get it online. His first project with users, a chatbot game, drew 1,500 hits from StumbleUpon in a day: "It was the most glorious feeling." At Harvard, running the computer club as a sophomore, he decided, "I'm not ready. I have so much to learn. I need to be around people who are so much better than me." He moved to MIT, then left in 2010 to join Stripe as its fourth employee ([Business Insider, May 6, 2015](https://www.businessinsider.com/stripes-cto-greg-brockman-is-leaving-the-company-2015-5)).

At Stripe he found "a repeatable pattern for creating software systems from nothing: focus single-mindedly on the software, shutting out all distractions and working from wake until sleep." His next sentence: "These are the times I feel most alive." His rule was to set a soft and a hard launch date: "I've never hit a soft launch date, but have never missed a hard one." ([#define CTO OpenAI, January 9, 2017](https://blog.gregbrockman.com/define-cto-openai))

Management went worse. Stacking every engineer's one-on-one on Tuesdays left him "completely burned out by the end of the day." He hired a VP of Engineering, Marc Hedlund, handed the meetings over, and then took "a vacation (my first ever) to Hawaii, and then holed myself up with a few engineers building CTF3." His first vacation became a build. The same 2014 essay confesses "having trouble distinguishing 'not the way I'd do it' from 'bad'," and it names "sparse micromanagement," defined as "jumping in to some random issue, overturning all the decisions, and then disappearing," as "the worst." It ends: "What my role will look like from here, I have yet to define fully. I'm too busy coding." ([#define CTO, October 27, 2014](https://blog.gregbrockman.com/figuring-out-the-cto-role-at-stripe))

Within months he was in "a strangely luxurious place: I'd successfully removed myself as a critical lynchpin for the company and could craft literally any role I wanted." Then: "I was yearning to create something of my own." He left in May 2015 so that he wouldn't "always wonder what could have been" ([Leaving Stripe, May 6, 2015](https://blog.gregbrockman.com/leaving-stripe)). He went once he was no longer the critical piece. A [Type 2](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-2), whose sense of worth rests on being needed, would be expected to stay where the need was.

## "I volunteered myself as tribute"

After a July 2015 dinner in Menlo Park where the idea for OpenAI took shape, he wrote, "I knew it would only happen if someone was willing to go full-time on figuring out exactly what that would be and who would be a part of it. I volunteered myself as tribute." ([My path to OpenAI, May 3, 2016](https://blog.gregbrockman.com/my-path-to-openai)) When researchers at the first office, his apartment, turned to write on a whiteboard that wasn't there, "I quickly got them a whiteboard and any other office supplies they needed." He "resolved to do whatever was needed to remove all non-research tasks from Ilya's plate," meaning Ilya Sutskever's ([#define CTO OpenAI](https://blog.gregbrockman.com/define-cto-openai)).

This is the strongest evidence against a status-hungry reading. In 2017 he wrote that he had found his role by seeking out "a problem more important to me than my role within it." Yet one idea returns whenever he explains why work mattered: someone else registering it. In 2015 it was "the deep satisfaction of having others actually use my creations." In 2017, after nights spent fixing bugs for researchers including [Dario Amodei](/personality-analysis/dario-amodei), it was "something extremely validating about your work allowing researchers to ask questions that no human ever has before." In 2019, "the team's excitement every time I overcame a new obstacle was deeply validating." The humility about titles is consistent. So is the need to see the work land.

## "Nothing to show for my efforts"

The 2019 essay is specific about what stalled him. He had built the infrastructure that made Dota trainable, but he "felt half blind" beside teammates who had made "the core breakthrough that powered our Dota bot." After the July 2017 project ended with nothing to show, he stayed with infrastructure for more than a year. In November 2018 he took "a gamble with three months of my time," studying through the holidays and building a chatbot, because "I learn best when I have something specific in mind to build." He had help: "I'd begun a relationship with someone who made me feel it was ok if I failed." That appears to be Anna, whom he married at OpenAI's office in November 2019 after about a year of dating, with Sutskever officiating and a robot hand as ring bearer ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2020/02/17/844721/ai-openai-moonshot-elon-musk-sam-altman-greg-brockman-messy-secretive-reality/)).

The turn came when "my frustration overwhelmed my fear" and he made a change that touched an entire model someone else had built. By mid-2019, with Jakub Pachocki and Sutskever advising, the project produced results exciting enough that Pachocki and Szymon Sidor joined it full time: "I feel proud every time I see a commit from them in the machine learning codebase I'd started." He tracked his recovery in hours, reporting that he was "around 75% of the number of coding hours from where I've been historically." His verdict on the lost years: "somehow I'd convinced myself that I was the exception and couldn't learn." Even inside OpenAI, he wrote, "I couldn't make the transition because I was unwilling to become a beginner again."

This essay carries more of the type argument than anything else in the record. Other people's confidence bothered him more than his own doubt, and he kept talking their estimate down. He quit when the visible result vanished. He came back in private, over a holiday, beside someone who made failure feel "ok," and he scored the comeback in commits and hours. That is the Type 3 fear in plain form: failing where people can see it.

A [Type 5](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-5) facing a competence gap tends to withdraw and study until ready; Brockman says he learns by building. A One's block tends to center on doing it wrong; his centered on others' estimate of him and on having a result to point to. The limits are real. He wrote the essay as encouragement, it ends with a hiring pitch ("apply to OpenAI!"), and the word he uses is fear, which a [Type 6](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6) reading would also claim.

## The diary and the mirror

The same year he stalled, OpenAI's founders fought [Elon Musk](/personality-analysis/elon-musk) over control. In September 2017, Sutskever sent Musk and Altman an email signed "Greg & Ilya" that admitted, "we were afraid of harming the relationship, having you think less of us, or losing you as partners" ([court-exhibit emails compiled on LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/5jjk4CDnj9tA7ugxr/openai-email-archives-from-musk-v-altman-and-openai-blog)). It went out from Sutskever's account, so it can't be assigned to Brockman alone.

Brockman's private diary from that year, produced in Musk's lawsuit, is his alone, and its entries pull in different directions. The New Yorker quoted "Happy to not become rich on this, so long as no one else is," and a question, "So what do I *really* want?", answered in part with "Financially what will take me to $1B." ([The New Yorker, April 6, 2026](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/04/13/sam-altman-may-control-our-future-can-he-be-trusted)) In court in May 2026, Musk's lawyer showed a November 2017 entry, rendered by MIT Technology Review as "it'd be wrong to steal the nonprofit from him. to convert to a b-corp without him. that'd be pretty morally bankrupt." Courthouse News's version continues, "and he's really not an idiot" ([Courthouse News, May 4, 2026](https://www.courthousenews.com/openai-president-testifies-in-musk-altman-feud/)). Asked what he meant, Brockman said, "I meant it would actually serve the mission, but it'd be hard to look at yourself in the mirror." He also told the jury, "Solving for the mission has always been my primary motivation." ([MIT Technology Review, May 8, 2026](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/05/08/1137008/musk-v-altman-week-2-openai-fires-back-and-shivon-zilis-reveals-that-musk-tried-to-poach-sam-altman/))

Read as a One, the entries show a conscience at work. Read as a Three, they show a man weighing an act that would "serve the mission" against how it would look, to a rival who is "not an idiot" and to himself in the mirror. The idiom fits either type. What the diary does establish is the tension itself: a personal financial target, a fairness rule measured against other people, and a line he would not cross alone. Musk's lawyers chose the excerpts, and a defendant facing claims of greed has every reason to frame his answers carefully.

## "An ends-over-means kind of person"

The Wall Street Journal, relying largely on unnamed sources, reports that after Musk left, Altman and Amodei "agreed that the lab's employees didn't have faith in Brockman and Sutskever's leadership, given the layoffs." When Brockman sought a role on the early language-model work, co-lead Daniela Amodei "offered to step down as head of the project rather than allow Brockman on it." In March 2020, by the Journal's account, he "wrote a lengthy piece of feedback for Daniela accusing her of abusing her power to create bureaucratic processes to get her way," and when the fight escalated he "at one point offered to withdraw" it ([The Wall Street Journal, syndicated by To Vima, March 28, 2026](https://www.tovima.com/wsj/the-decadelong-feud-shaping-the-future-of-ai/)). The New Yorker says researchers chafed under Brockman, "whom some considered an abrasive operator."

Fortune's 2025 profile, quoting unnamed former employees, describes the pace. When OpenAI had about 200 people, he had Slack notify him of every message on every channel. "He'd go super hard, then go off like a bear and hibernate for a few weeks, and then come back," one said. Another: "He has incredibly high standards and expects results." A third: "If something's not moving fast enough, Greg will take it into his own hands and work around people if necessary. He's very much an ends-over-means kind of person." ([Fortune, November 5, 2025](https://fortune.com/2025/11/05/openai-greg-brockman-ai-infrastructure-data-center-master-builder/))

Before Altman's firing in November 2023, Sutskever sent the board a memo critical of Brockman, which he confirmed under oath on October 1, 2025 ([deposition transcript](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/9mp6vBwxitoZcvDmG/ilya-sutskever-deposition-transcript)). Fortune reports that Keach Hagey's book _The Optimist_ suggested it laid out concerns about Brockman's "alleged bullying." OpenAI told Fortune, "These claims aren't true." The memo is not public. Sutskever also testified that his written claim that Brockman "was essentially fired from Stripe" rested on "Mira told me," meaning [Mira Murati](/personality-analysis/mira-murati), and that he never checked it with Brockman, whose 2015 farewell post describes a voluntary exit.

Most of this comes from anonymous sources or from the Amodeis' side of the split that produced Anthropic, and Brockman's version of most episodes is not on record. Still, separate outlets describe the same tendency: speed over process. A former colleague's "into his own hands" also sits uncomfortably close to the habit Brockman himself called "the worst" in 2014.

## November 17, 2023

At 12:19 p.m., Brockman got a text from Sutskever asking for a quick call. By the account he and Altman posted that night, "Greg was told that he was being removed from the board (but was vital to the company and would retain his role) and that Sam had been fired." ([X, November 17, 2023](https://x.com/gdb/status/1725736242137182594)) Asked in April 2026 what went through his mind, he said, "It just wasn't right." Was it anger? "No. I felt like I understood what had happened." Then: "Right after I hung up the call, I talked to my wife and I said, 'Got to quit.' And she said, 'I agree.'" ([The Knowledge Project, April 22, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6JoUcQ1qmAc))

His note to staff, posted that afternoon, read "but based on today's news, i quit," and closed with "i continue to believe in the mission of creating safe AGI that benefits all of humanity." ([X, November 17, 2023](https://x.com/gdb/status/1725667410387378559)) He returned with Altman days later, and in March 2024, after a review by the law firm WilmerHale, the board expressed "full confidence" in both men ([OpenAI, March 8, 2024](https://openai.com/index/review-completed-altman-brockman-to-continue-to-lead-openai/)).

This was the costliest choice in his record, made in an afternoon, and it is the One reading's best day: a moral verdict, with anger set aside. A Six would read it as loyalty. Nothing in it favors a Three over a One.

## If he is a One

The rival case deserves its strongest form. His moral vocabulary runs a decade: "morally bankrupt" in 2017, "It just wasn't right" in 2023 with anger denied, the 2014 admission about treating "not the way I'd do it" as "bad," and "incredibly high standards" in a colleague's description. In 2019, Karen Hao of MIT Technology Review watched him recite OpenAI's charter "like scripture," then stop to explain: "I guess I know all these lines because I spent a lot of time really poring over them to get them exactly right. It's not like I was reading this before the meeting." ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2020/02/17/844721/ai-openai-moonshot-elon-musk-sam-altman-greg-brockman-messy-secretive-reality/))

Read this way, he is a reformer whose mission is a moral cause, whose impatience is a perfectionist's, and whose nine years without a real break are self-discipline. It may be right. Even the charter aside cuts both ways: the care to get the lines "exactly right" belongs to a One, and the reflex to head off the impression that he had crammed belongs to a Three.

## Where the One reading strains

The test is what he does when the right way and the fast way collide. Ones are usually the people defending procedure, and Brockman keeps turning up on the other side. His launch rule plans for the first deadline to slip. His peer review, by the Journal's account, attacked a colleague's "bureaucratic processes." A former colleague's summary is "ends-over-means." On the stand, he described the line he would not cross in 2017 as one that would "serve the mission" but be hard to face in the mirror.

His latest reversal fits. He and his wife gave $25 million to MAGA Inc., a pro-Trump super PAC, and he committed $50 million to Leading the Future, a "pro-A.I." super PAC that has funded opponents of congressional candidates who favor AI regulation, though OpenAI's official stance is that the company will not contribute to such PACs ([The New Yorker](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/04/13/sam-altman-may-control-our-future-can-he-be-trusted)). In June 2026, according to The New York Times as relayed by Gizmodo, he wrote on Slack, "I also want to say that I feel terrible about the fact that LTF keeps reflecting on the company and become so much of a distraction for people here," adding, "We've donated $25m so far, and have no plans to donate more at this time." ([Gizmodo, October 1, 2026](https://gizmodo.com/openai-president-reportedly-pulls-support-from-controversial-ai-super-pac-2000820292)) The stated reason is the effect on OpenAI and its people, and the message as reported does not call the giving wrong. His fuller rationale, given to WIRED in February 2026, was not available for this profile.

Most of these items carry a weakness: they are anonymous, rival-sourced, secondhand, or sworn testimony from a defendant. The Three reading rests on their agreement with the 2019 essay, written with no legal stakes.

A [Nine](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-9) reading comes from his own recent words. He told Shane Parrish in April 2026 that he has approached OpenAI "through a lens of uncertainty, of feeling like I don't know what the right answer is," that he has worked to "have a stronger sense of self," and that his regrets are times "we just waited too long." His lesson learned more than once: "Make the hard decision. Have the hard conversation." That reading struggles with the rest of the record: pushing onto a project over its leaders' objections, a peer review that set off a fight intense enough that he offered to withdraw it, working "around people," and a teenager standing at attention to win a part.

## Like a bear

The cycle colleagues describe, super hard and then gone, appears in his own account. One of his hardest moments at OpenAI, he says, came when Sutskever left: "maybe the only moment in OpenAI's history where I felt like I didn't want to do it anymore." On August 5, 2024, he announced a sabbatical, his "First time to relax since co-founding OpenAI 9 years ago." ([X, August 5, 2024](https://x.com/gdb/status/1820644694264791459)) Fortune reported that media outlets linked it to concerns that "his demanding leadership style had created tension within teams"; OpenAI said the break was his decision. He spent part of it training language models on DNA for the Arc Institute, work he calls "very personally meaningful" to him and his wife, who he says "has a lot of health conditions." In November he posted, "longest vacation of my life complete. back to building @OpenAI." ([X, November 12, 2024](https://x.com/gdb/status/1856441156281753908))

Giving up hands-on coding, he says, meant letting go of "something that I was used to and valued and really loved. But I've actually replaced it with something I love even more." ([Stratechery](https://stratechery.com/2026/an-interview-with-openai-president-greg-brockman-about-astra-and-alignment/))

Read as a Three, this is a career-long loop: rest only after depletion, and rest that turns into more building. Startup culture rewards the same sprints in any type, including a hard-driving [Eight](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-8) or [Seven](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-7), so the loop supports the reading without proving it.

## Someone bold enough to pronounce a goal

Some of Brockman doesn't fit the Three reading, and it shouldn't be trimmed to fit. The moral vocabulary stays. "So long as no one else is" mixes fairness and comparison. His emotion over loyalty and his long record of unglamorous jobs fit a Two or a Six at least as well.

What the record shows most consistently is a man who measures himself by work that lands. In 2016 he explained why Altman's goal, "To build safe human-level AI," won him over: "sometimes an effort needs only someone bold enough to pronounce a goal, and then the right people will join them." At the September 3, 2026 press briefing for OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra, he said he personally believes OpenAI has reached AGI, left users to decide whether Astra meets the definition, and closed with "Welcome to the AGI era" ([Axios, September 3, 2026](https://www.axios.com/2026/09/03/openai-astra-gpt-6-agi-brockman)). Ten years after admiring the pronouncement, he was the one making it.

Asked in April 2026 about the loyalty he inspired that November, he described himself this way: "I think the way that I operate is I'm very much a in the trenches kind of leader. Try to lead from the front. And sometimes when I do that, I don't always Sorry, I'm getting a little emotional. Um, but I don't always look back to see if everyone's following. Like I just like run right in." No one, he added, left for a rival that weekend: "we actually did not lose a single person." ([The Knowledge Project](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6JoUcQ1qmAc))
