In July 2017, Greg Brockman, OpenAI's co-founder and chief technology officer, started a machine learning project of his own for the lab's bot in Dota 2, a multiplayer video game. He kept finding "small bugs that had been corrupting my results the whole time." "I didn't feel confident in my work, but to make it worse, other people did," he wrote. When colleagues said the problem was hard, "I always made sure to correct them by pointing out that I was a newbie, and this probably said more about my abilities than the problem."

His code made it into the bot for a while. Then a colleague, Jie Tang, "figured out how to get better results without using my code, and I had nothing to show for my efforts." The next line sits alone as its own paragraph: "I never tried machine learning on the Dota project again." ([Greg Brockman, "How I became a machine learning practitioner," July 30, 2019](https://blog.gregbrockman.com/how-i-became-a-machine-learning-practitioner)) His software skills "were always in demand, and I kept procrastinating," he explained, and the biggest blocker was "a mental barrier": "getting ok with being a beginner again."

Brockman, born in 1987 and raised in North Dakota, was Stripe's fourth employee and first CTO before he co-founded OpenAI in 2015. In its early years he "essentially served as the AI lab's CEO," Fortune reported ([Fortune, November 5, 2025](https://fortune.com/2025/11/05/openai-greg-brockman-ai-infrastructure-data-center-master-builder/)), and he chaired its board until November 17, 2023, when [Sam Altman](/personality-analysis/sam-altman) was fired and Brockman quit. Both returned days later. He is OpenAI's president, and since July 2026 he has overseen its product business as well as its infrastructure, "making him the company's second-in-command ahead of a possible IPO," Forbes reported ([Forbes, September 15, 2026](https://www.forbes.com.au/?p=206622)). In Luca Guadagnino's _Artificial_, which premiered on October 5, 2026, Cooper Hoffman plays him opposite [Andrew Garfield](/personality-analysis/andrew-garfield) as Altman ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_%282026_film%29)).

His essays show a man who hands off jobs easily and watches closely whether his work lands. His long machine learning stall came not when he had nothing to show, but in the one field where he would have to start as a beginner.

## What is Greg Brockman's personality type?

Greg Brockman most likely fits Enneagram Type 3, the Achiever, at medium confidence. In his own accounts, he scores his work by being best, being noticed and having a result to point to, more than by doing it right. Type 1, the Reformer, is a close rival, and Type 9, the Peacemaker, a weaker third.

A [Type 3](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-3) tends to measure worth by results that register with other people. Brockman's version does not run on titles. After Altman became CEO in 2019, he took "a less public-facing role," as Fortune put it in 2025, but that followed reported staff doubts about his leadership, and by September 2026 he was the face of OpenAI's Astra launch. A [Type 1](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-1) reading explains much of the record through his moral language and exacting standards; a [Type 9](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-9) reading fits how he described his own indecision in April 2026. What separates Three from One is how he keeps score on his own work: whether it was right, or whether it landed and was seen.

This profile reads the public record; it claims no access to his mind and makes no clinical judgment.

## "I felt like I could be best in the world"

Nothing in the record points to a formative wound, and this profile won't supply one. In 2007 the Grand Forks Herald reported that his parents were both physicians and that he had just become North Dakota's first Intel Science Talent Search finalist since 1973 ([Grand Forks Herald, February 3, 2007](https://www.grandforksherald.com/newsmd/greg-brockman-intel-science-talent-search-finalist-grand-forks)). He described a "focused, quiet childhood" to MIT Technology Review, which later noted, in a correction OpenAI requested, that he grew up "on a hobby farm," not a farm: "Brockman considers this distinction important." ([MIT Technology Review, February 17, 2020](https://www.technologyreview.com/2020/02/17/844721/ai-openai-moonshot-elon-musk-sam-altman-greg-brockman-messy-secretive-reality/))

He told Ben Thompson of Stratechery in September 2026 that he thought he might be "an actor all the way through ninth grade." He set a bar: "I felt like I could be best in the world and really try to move the needle in a field." He picked science over acting "because I felt like maybe that was an area where I could most make a difference in the world." ([Stratechery, September 4, 2026](https://stratechery.com/2026/an-interview-with-openai-president-greg-brockman-about-astra-and-alignment/))

His first paid job came from an audition for extras at a Mannheim Steamroller concert in Grand Forks. While the casting team compared notes and other hopefuls chatted, he reasoned that "this job has one requirement, which is you're going to be six hours at attention." So "I was there standing at attention, just being in character." He wasn't picked, but the organizers told him, "We loved seeing how much you were dedicated to this, so we're going to actually make a new role for you." He became a gingerbread man. North Dakota, he said, let him "be the best in the state." ([Stratechery](https://stratechery.com/2026/an-interview-with-openai-president-greg-brockman-about-astra-and-alignment/))

Gifted kids of every type win contests, and a 2026 interview story has had decades of polish. Still, his bar for committing to a field was being best in the world, though the choice itself turned on impact. At the audition he worked out what the evaluators would reward and showed it unasked. A One could tell that story as conscientiousness; his gloss, "trying to be out of the box in order to try to get the job done," stresses getting the job. He also loved acting for "the group aspect of it."

## From wake until sleep

In a gap year after high school, he read Turing and "started writing a chatbot," he wrote in 2016. It could "talk about the weather very convincingly," but "no one had any techniques that could make my bot really work," so "I soon shelved my chatbot pursuits" and "decided to focus on creating systems that can have a real impact." ([My path to OpenAI, May 3, 2016](https://blog.gregbrockman.com/my-path-to-openai)) In 2026 he recalled a "competitive chatbot game" from that time that drew 1,500 StumbleUpon hits in a day: "It was the most glorious feeling," and the lesson was "I want to help people." Whether these were one chatbot or two, AI was "definitely my life goal," as he told Altman in 2015. This was the first of two times he shelved his own AI work when he could not make it do what he wanted. After Harvard and MIT, he left school in 2010 to join Stripe ([Business Insider, May 6, 2015](https://www.businessinsider.com/stripes-cto-greg-brockman-is-leaving-the-company-2015-5)).

At Stripe he found "a repeatable pattern for creating software systems from nothing: focus single-mindedly on the software, shutting out all distractions and working from wake until sleep." Then: "These are the times I feel most alive: coding feels like magic made real, where anything I can imagine and describe becomes possible." ([#define CTO OpenAI, January 9, 2017](https://blog.gregbrockman.com/define-cto-openai))

Management went worse. Stacking every engineer's one-on-one on Tuesdays left him "completely burned out by the end of the day." He hired a VP of Engineering, Marc Hedlund, and so that "people would take their problems to Marc," took "a vacation (my first ever) to Hawaii, and then holed myself up with a few engineers building CTF3," a security contest. His first vacation was a delegation tactic, and a build followed it. The same 2014 essay confesses "having trouble distinguishing 'not the way I'd do it' from 'bad'," and calls "sparse micromanagement," "jumping in to some random issue, overturning all the decisions, and then disappearing," "the worst." ([#define CTO, October 27, 2014](https://blog.gregbrockman.com/figuring-out-the-cto-role-at-stripe))

By late 2014 he had "removed myself as a critical lynchpin for the company," yet "I was yearning to create something of my own." He left in May 2015 ([Leaving Stripe, May 6, 2015](https://blog.gregbrockman.com/leaving-stripe)). He went once he was no longer the critical piece. A [Type 2](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-2), whose sense of worth rests on being needed, would be expected to stay where the need was.

## "I volunteered myself as tribute"

He came to OpenAI with a plan of his own, telling Altman in June 2015 "my current plan was to start an AI company within the next year." After a dinner the next month, he wrote, "I knew it would only happen if someone was willing to go full-time on figuring out exactly what that would be and who would be a part of it. I volunteered myself as tribute." ([My path to OpenAI](https://blog.gregbrockman.com/my-path-to-openai)) That made him the founder who recruited the team, and it meant errands. When researchers at the first office, his apartment, found no whiteboard, "I quickly got them a whiteboard and any other office supplies they needed." He "resolved to do whatever was needed to remove all non-research tasks from Ilya's plate," meaning Ilya Sutskever's ([#define CTO OpenAI](https://blog.gregbrockman.com/define-cto-openai)).

Those errands are the strongest evidence against a status-hungry reading. Yet when he explains why work mattered, beside the love of making things, one idea returns: someone else registering it. He "got hooked on programming because I loved the act of creating something from nothing," he wrote in 2015, then "soon discovered the deep satisfaction of having others actually use my creations." Enabling researchers such as [Dario Amodei](/personality-analysis/dario-amodei) in 2016 was "extremely validating." In 2019, "the team's excitement every time I overcame a new obstacle was deeply validating." The love of building is real. So is the need to see it land.

## "Nothing to show for my efforts"

By his 2019 account, he had built the environment that made Dota trainable, but he "felt half blind," limited to "looking at all problems through a software lens." He "wanted to be like my teammates Jakub Pachocki and Szymon Sidor, who had made the core breakthrough that powered our Dota bot," and "to be able to make critical contributions like that." After July 2017 he stayed with infrastructure, where he was in demand, for more than a year.

After Dota's 2018 world championship, "the demands on my time had relented," and in November he took "a gamble with three months of my time," studying through the holidays and building a chatbot. Why persist? "I wanted to see the whole picture clearly." He had help: "I'd begun a relationship with someone who made me feel it was ok if I failed." That appears to be Anna, whom he married at OpenAI's office in November 2019 ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2020/02/17/844721/ai-openai-moonshot-elon-musk-sam-altman-greg-brockman-messy-secretive-reality/)).

The turn came when he crossed "a barrier I'd been too timid to do with Dota: make substantive changes to someone else's machine learning code." His "frustration overwhelmed my fear." After three months, "I felt ready to work on an actual project," which went well enough that Pachocki and Sidor joined: "I feel proud every time I see a commit from them in the machine learning codebase I'd started." Even inside OpenAI, he wrote, "I couldn't make the transition because I was unwilling to become a beginner again."

This essay carries more of the type argument than anything else in the record, though it cannot carry it alone. Read as a Three, he stayed where he was in demand and praised, dropped the one attempt that left nothing to point to, and named his obstacle as a status, being a beginner, rather than a mistake. He came back when his day job eased, beside someone who made failure feel "ok." This suggests the Type 3 fear of failing where people can see it.

The essay also pushes back. Offered an excuse, he refused it and blamed his own ability; a Three might lower expectations that way, but a One might simply insist on the truth. His frustrations, bugs and results he "hadn't properly archived," concern correctness, a One's territory. Wanting to "see the whole picture" and studying three months before he "felt ready" fit a [Type 5](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-5), but a Five reading fails on a record full of performing and recruiting. The essay ends with a hiring pitch ("apply to OpenAI!"), and the word he uses is fear, which a [Type 6](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6) reading would also claim.

## The diary and the mirror

That same year OpenAI's founders fought [Elon Musk](/personality-analysis/elon-musk), a co-founder and the lab's main early funder, over a for-profit arm and who would control it; Musk left the board in 2018 ([NPR, May 18, 2026](https://kvcr.org/news/npr-top-stories/2026-05-18/musk-altman-openai-jury-verdict-claims-dismissed)). A September 2017 email signed "Greg & Ilya," sent from Sutskever's account, admitted, "we were afraid of harming the relationship, having you think less of us, or losing you as partners" ([court-exhibit emails compiled on LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/5jjk4CDnj9tA7ugxr/openai-email-archives-from-musk-v-altman-and-openai-blog)).

Brockman's diary from that year, produced in the suit Musk filed in 2024, pulls in different directions. The New Yorker quotes a truthfulness rule, "cannot say that we are committed to the non-profit . . . if three months later we're doing b-corp then it was a lie" (a b-corp being a for-profit benefit corporation), along with "Happy to not become rich on this, so long as no one else is" and "Financially what will take me to $1B." ([The New Yorker, April 6, 2026](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/04/13/sam-altman-may-control-our-future-can-he-be-trusted)) At trial in May 2026, Musk's lawyer showed a November 2017 entry, rendered by MIT Technology Review as "it'd be wrong to steal the nonprofit from him. to convert to a b-corp without him. that'd be pretty morally bankrupt." Courthouse News's version continues, "and he's really not an idiot" ([Courthouse News, May 4, 2026](https://www.courthousenews.com/openai-president-testifies-in-musk-altman-feud/)). Brockman explained, "I meant it would actually serve the mission, but it'd be hard to look at yourself in the mirror." ([MIT Technology Review, May 8, 2026](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/05/08/1137008/musk-v-altman-week-2-openai-fires-back-and-shivon-zilis-reveals-that-musk-tried-to-poach-sam-altman/))

Read as a One, the entries show a conscience, and the mirror answer refuses a step that would have served the goal. Read as a Three, they weigh an act against how it would look, to a rival who is "not an idiot" and to himself. The idiom fits either type. What the diary establishes is the tension: a personal financial target, a fairness rule measured against others, and a line drawn at converting "without him." In 2019, after Musk left, OpenAI created a for-profit subsidiary, which his suit called a "breach of charitable trust." On May 18, 2026, a unanimous advisory jury found the claims filed too late, and the judge dismissed the case without reaching the merits; Musk said he would appeal ([NPR](https://kvcr.org/news/npr-top-stories/2026-05-18/musk-altman-openai-jury-verdict-claims-dismissed)). Musk's lawyers chose the excerpts, and a defendant facing claims of self-enrichment has every reason to frame his answers carefully.

## "An ends-over-means kind of person"

The Wall Street Journal's account of the OpenAI–Anthropic rift, by Keach Hagey and Berber Jin and largely from unnamed sources, reports 2017 layoffs that followed Musk's request that Brockman and Sutskever list "every employee and what important contribution they had made." In 2018 Altman and Dario Amodei, then research director, "agreed that the lab's employees didn't have faith in Brockman and Sutskever's leadership, given the layoffs." When Brockman sought a role on early language-model work, co-lead Daniela Amodei, Dario's sister, "offered to step down as head of the project rather than allow Brockman on it," and he and Altman "reluctantly agreed" to keep him off. In 2020 Altman had executives write peer reviews. Brockman's accused Daniela "of abusing her power to create bureaucratic processes to get her way"; Altman had called it "tough but fair." She rebutted it "point by point," and Brockman "at one point offered to withdraw his" ([The Wall Street Journal, syndicated by To Vima, March 28, 2026](https://www.tovima.com/wsj/the-decadelong-feud-shaping-the-future-of-ai/)). The Amodeis later founded Anthropic, a rival. The New Yorker says some considered Brockman "an abrasive operator."

Fortune's 2025 profile mixes praise and complaint. AMD's Lisa Su, a business partner, praised his "just maniacal focus." Unnamed former employees describe the pace: "He'd go super hard, then go off like a bear and hibernate for a few weeks, and then come back." Another: "If something's not moving fast enough, Greg will take it into his own hands and work around people if necessary. He's very much an ends-over-means kind of person." ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/11/05/openai-greg-brockman-ai-infrastructure-data-center-master-builder/))

Before Altman's firing, Sutskever sent the board a memo critical of Brockman, which he confirmed under oath on October 1, 2025 ([deposition transcript](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/9mp6vBwxitoZcvDmG/ilya-sutskever-deposition-transcript)). Fortune reports that Keach Hagey's book _The Optimist_ suggested it laid out concerns about Brockman's "alleged bullying." OpenAI told Fortune, "These claims aren't true. Ilya signed the petition asking for Greg and Sam to be reinstated, and the board's independent review further concluded that he and Sam are the right leaders for OpenAI." Sutskever also testified that his written claim that Brockman "was essentially fired from Stripe" rested on "Mira told me," meaning [Mira Murati](/personality-analysis/mira-murati), and that he never checked it with Brockman, whose 2015 farewell post describes a voluntary exit.

Most of this is anonymous or from the Amodeis' side, his version is mostly not on record, and some of it shows him backing down. The accounts still share a tendency: pace over process. On one unnamed source, "into his own hands" sits uncomfortably close to the habit Brockman called "the worst" in 2014, though it describes working around slow work, not overturning decisions.

## November 17, 2023

At 12:19 p.m., Brockman got a text from Sutskever, then a board member, asking for a quick call. On it, Brockman recalled in April 2026, was "the board minus Sam." By the account he and Altman posted that night, "Greg was told that he was being removed from the board (but was vital to the company and would retain his role) and that Sam had been fired." ([X, November 17, 2023](https://x.com/gdb/status/1725736242137182594)) The board said Altman "was not consistently candid" and that Brockman would step down "as chairman of the board" but stay ([OpenAI, November 17, 2023](https://openai.com/index/openai-announces-leadership-transition/)). Brockman says he asked for "any reasons, any feedback" and "Was told no." What went through his mind? "It just wasn't right." Was it anger? "No. I felt like I understood what had happened." Then: "Right after I hung up the call, I talked to my wife and I said, 'Got to quit.' And she said, 'I agree.'" ([The Knowledge Project, April 22, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6JoUcQ1qmAc))

That afternoon he posted his note to staff: "but based on today's news, i quit." ([X, November 17, 2023](https://x.com/gdb/status/1725667410387378559)) That day, he says, he, Altman and a few colleagues who had also quit "started to chart out what a new company could look like," putting the odds of getting OpenAI back at 10 percent. A staff petition for their return "crashed Google Docs," and Sutskever signed it. They returned days later; in March 2024, after a WilmerHale review, the board expressed "full confidence" in both men ([OpenAI, March 8, 2024](https://openai.com/index/review-completed-altman-brockman-to-continue-to-lead-openai/)).

This was the costliest choice in his record, made in an afternoon, and it is the One reading's best day: a moral verdict, with anger set aside. A Six would read it as loyalty. Nothing in it favors a Three over a One. The later details don't change that. Asking for reasons is a due-process complaint, which helps the One reading; planning a new company is a reflex any founder might share; and he quit before he knew who would follow. The support, he says, "was a real honest surprise."

## If he is a One

The rival case deserves its strongest form. His moral vocabulary runs a decade: the 2017 rule that a reversed commitment "was a lie," "morally bankrupt" months later, "It just wasn't right" in 2023 with anger denied, and the 2014 admission about treating "not the way I'd do it" as "bad." In 2014 he traced his Stripe culture work to "a very strong vision of the environment I wanted to be a part of." In April 2026: "I also really care about the environment in which these things are done." In 2019, Karen Hao of MIT Technology Review watched him recite OpenAI's charter "like scripture," then stop to explain: "I guess I know all these lines because I spent a lot of time really poring over them to get them exactly right. It's not like I was reading this before the meeting." ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2020/02/17/844721/ai-openai-moonshot-elon-musk-sam-altman-greg-brockman-messy-secretive-reality/))

Read this way, he is a reformer whose mission is a moral cause, whose impatience is a perfectionist's, whose refusal of an easy excuse in 2017 is a One's honesty, and whose nine years without a real break are self-discipline. It may be right. Even the charter aside cuts both ways: the care to get the lines "exactly right" belongs to a One, and the reflex to head off the impression that he had crammed belongs to a Three.

## Where the One reading strains

His moral vocabulary turns up when he judges conduct, his own included. In the private 2017 diary, a b-corp move he and his co-founders were weighing would be "a lie" if it reversed a public commitment and "morally bankrupt" if made "without him." That is the One reading's best private evidence, though most people would judge both acts the same way. What marks a One is turning that standard on everything, the work included, so the better test is how he scores his own work. Brockman's accounts of it circle being best, being noticed and having something to show.

His ninth-grade bar for any field was being "best in the world." Recalling his first deep learning practice, in 2015, he added, "(I was even number 1 for a while on my first contest!)" ([My path to OpenAI](https://blog.gregbrockman.com/my-path-to-openai)) His reading of Ian Goodfellow's textbook in January 2016 "doubled as a recruiting strategy, since he was impressed that my pages of comments were more thorough than those from his official reviewers." ([#define CTO OpenAI](https://blog.gregbrockman.com/define-cto-openai)) Even his talk of environment bends toward output: in 2026 he described his career as helping "build and shape environments and culture that are actually able to deliver great results" ([Stratechery](https://stratechery.com/2026/an-interview-with-openai-president-greg-brockman-about-astra-and-alignment/)). Self-reports are self-presentation too, and a One can enjoy a ranking. But the Three reading rests on these first-person accounts, most written with no legal stakes, more than on anonymous, rival-sourced or litigated items.

"Ends-over-means" comes from one unnamed former employee. The 2020 peer review is a fairness charge, as much a One's protest as a Three's impatience. His trial gloss described refusing a step that "would actually serve the mission," a One's answer.

His political giving does not settle it either. In September 2025 he and his wife gave $25 million to MAGA Inc., a pro-Trump super PAC. They also promised $50 million to Leading the Future, a super PAC that opposes candidates who favor AI regulation. He told WIRED, "maybe supporting the team is bigger than the people that I happen to be employed with. It's really team humanity." ([WIRED, February 12, 2026](https://www.wired.com/story/openai-president-greg-brockman-political-donations-trump-humanity/)) After a backlash, he wrote on Slack in June 2026, according to The New York Times as relayed by Gizmodo, "I feel terrible about the fact that LTF keeps reflecting on the company and become so much of a distraction for people here. I've been thinking hard about how to turn the ship on it. We've donated $25m so far, and have no plans to donate more at this time." ([Gizmodo, October 1, 2026](https://gizmodo.com/openai-president-reportedly-pulls-support-from-controversial-ai-super-pac-2000820292)) Any executive would write much the same after a backlash, so the message cannot separate a One from a Three.

So the Three reading leads on how he scores his work and the One on how he judges conduct: a medium-confidence call that would flip if new first-person evidence showed him scoring his own work mainly on whether it was right.

A [Nine](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-9) reading draws on his own words in April 2026: approaching OpenAI "through a lens of uncertainty," working to "have a stronger sense of self," and a lesson learned more than once: "Make the hard decision. Have the hard conversation." He also accepted exclusion from the language project and offered to withdraw his review. But a Nine reading struggles with his first-person record: the "best in the world" bar, the young extra standing in character to win a part, the founder who volunteered to run everything.

## Like a bear

Fortune's "bear" is an anonymous colleague's image; his own account of his 2024 break differs. Sutskever's departure in May 2024 ([TechCrunch, May 14, 2024](https://techcrunch.com/2024/05/14/ilya-sutskever-openai-co-founder-and-longtime-chief-scientist-departs/)) was, he told Parrish, "maybe the only moment in OpenAI's history where I felt like I didn't want to do it anymore," and he "needed some time to kind of find my way back to remembering like why I was doing this." ([The Knowledge Project](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6JoUcQ1qmAc)) On August 5, 2024, he announced a sabbatical, his "First time to relax since co-founding OpenAI 9 years ago." ([X, August 5, 2024](https://x.com/gdb/status/1820644694264791459)) Fortune reported that media outlets linked it to concerns that "his demanding leadership style had created tension within teams"; OpenAI said the break was his decision. He spent part of it training language models on DNA for the Arc Institute and returned in November: "longest vacation of my life complete. back to building @OpenAI." ([X, November 12, 2024](https://x.com/gdb/status/1856441156281753908))

Read as a Three, this is a career-long loop: long stretches without rest, and holidays that turn into builds, from Stripe's credit card vault "built in my two weeks home for the holidays in 2010" to machine learning over the 2018 holidays. His 2024 account adds a loss of meaning after a partner left, which a Nine or Six reading could claim as easily. Startup culture rewards the same sprints in any type, including a hard-driving [Eight](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-8) or [Seven](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-7), so the loop supports the reading without proving it.

## Someone bold enough to pronounce a goal

Some of Brockman doesn't fit the Three reading, and it shouldn't be trimmed to fit. The moral vocabulary stays. "So long as no one else is" mixes fairness and comparison. His emotion over loyalty and his long record of unglamorous jobs fit a Two or a Six at least as well.

In 2016 he explained why Altman's goal, "To build safe human-level AI," won him over: "sometimes an effort needs only someone bold enough to pronounce a goal, and then the right people will join them." At GPT-4's launch livestream on March 14, 2023, he demoed his own sketch becoming a website: "Going from hand-drawn beautiful art, if I do say so myself, to working website." ([OpenAI, March 14, 2023](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=outcGtbnMuQ)) At the September 3, 2026 briefing for GPT-6 Astra, he said he personally believes OpenAI has reached AGI, or artificial general intelligence, and closed with "Welcome to the AGI era" ([Axios, September 3, 2026](https://www.axios.com/2026/09/03/openai-astra-gpt-6-agi-brockman)). That was launch messaging, hedged in an interview recorded beforehand: "maybe it was the previous model, maybe it's Astra, maybe it's the next model." Ten years after admiring the pronouncement, he was the one making it.

Asked in April 2026 about the loyalty he inspired that November, he described himself this way: "I think the way that I operate is I'm very much a in the trenches kind of leader. Try to lead from the front. And sometimes when I do that, I don't always—" He broke off: "I'm getting a little emotional. Um, but I don't always look back to see if everyone's following ... just like run right in." With competitors "circling," he added, "No one accepted a competing offer." ([The Knowledge Project](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6JoUcQ1qmAc))
