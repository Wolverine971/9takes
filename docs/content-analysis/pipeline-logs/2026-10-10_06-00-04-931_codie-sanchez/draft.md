In an interview published on September 21, 2026, Codie Sanchez told Lewis Howes about the day she walked into a bank to borrow against one of her businesses. She believed the account held about a million dollars. The banker, as she tells it, said there was no money in it. "Excuse me, can you please turn that thing around so I can see what's on that screen?" she recalls asking. The account "was basically to zero." Her head of operations, she says, had hidden from her that the business was almost out of money.

That night she sat on her couch with the lights off and called her father, sure she would lose the business and "have to go back and get a job." His answer, in her telling: "If you haven't sat in the dark in the middle of the night with no idea of what to do next, you're not really in the game." Then she named what had hurt: "it was so painful. Like just my identity and that I was a failure because I had let an entire business fail." Of the manager who kept the shortfall from her, she said, "it was my fault for not tracking him, obviously." She does not name or date the business, and nobody else has told the story. ([The School of Greatness, September 21, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a_5-iAEMRpE))

About twenty minutes later, Howes asked what her real dream is. Her first answer was about a different injury: "I don't ever want anybody to have control over me or my life again."

Those two answers are the question of this profile. The second is the one her company is built on. The pledge on Contrarian Thinking's site ends, "I will not do what I am told, I will do what is required." ([Contrarian Thinking](https://www.contrarianthinking.co/about)) The first is the one she reaches for when something goes wrong, and she has been giving versions of it since 2017.

Sanchez is the founder and CEO of Contrarian Thinking, a media, advisory and investment company that teaches people to buy and run small businesses such as laundromats, car washes and roofing companies. She grew up in Phoenix, studied journalism at Arizona State, and worked in finance at Vanguard, Goldman Sachs, State Street and First Trust. Her site describes her parents as "a special education teacher and a self-made blue-collar builder." She wrote _Main Street Millionaire_ and _Own or Be Owned: Build a Business So Good It Doesn't Need You_, which she released on September 18, 2026 through her own imprint ([press release, September 18, 2026](https://codiesanchez.com/own-or-be-owned-press-release/)). Forbes estimated her 2026 creator earnings at $31 million as of June 23, 2026 ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/profile/codie-sanchez/)). In an interview published on August 20, 2026 she said, "I'm turning 40 this month" ([The Mother Daze](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3A4m9q9fkcQ)). She and her husband, Chris Petkas, announced in June 2026 that they expect their first child in December 2026 ([PEOPLE via AOL, June 22, 2026](https://www.aol.com/articles/entrepreneur-codie-sanchez-pregnant-expecting-140000000.html)).

## What is Codie Sanchez's personality type?

Codie Sanchez most likely fits Enneagram Type 3, the Achiever, at medium confidence. Her slogans are about control. In her own accounts of setbacks, the injury is failing or being seen to fail, and her response is to adjust and outperform. Type 8, the Challenger, is a close alternative. No statement of her own type has been found.

On a [Type 3](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-3) reading, she steers by results other people can see, and what she most wants to avoid is being the one who failed. On a [Type 8](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-8) reading, she steers by autonomy, and what she most wants to avoid is living under someone else's power. Both fit a person who quit jobs in order to own things.

The test used here is narrower. When a setback or a hostile judgment landed, what does she say it damaged, and what did she do next? Every answer in this record is her own telling, and that limit applies to everything below.

## Why she left journalism for money

As a journalism student at Arizona State, Sanchez was one of 17 students whose class project on families separated by the U.S.-Mexico border, "Divided Families," won a 2009 Robert F. Kennedy Journalism Award in the college print category ([Cronkite School, April 12, 2009](https://cronkite.asu.edu/news/2009/cronkite-students-win-international-rfk-award)). Her own site says the coverage "earned her" the award ([codiesanchez.com](https://codiesanchez.com/about/)). The university's record shows a team award for a class project.

In 2017, before Contrarian Thinking existed, an interviewer from Hispanic Executive raised the award. "Right, but I thought we were going to change the world," she answered. She was twenty, she said, and "in about fifteen minutes, nobody remembered what we wrote about." A mentor supplied the next move: "you find the centers of power. And that's money." ([Hispanic Executive, June 15, 2017](https://hispanicexecutive.com/codie-sanchez/))

By 2026 the story had hardened. "Money wasn't just about houses or vacations," she told HOLA! of her border reporting. "It decided who had protection, who had options, and who could end up dead in the desert and no one cared." Then: "I got jaded. So I went where money was understood." ([HOLA!, September 1, 2026](https://www.hola.com/us/lifestyle/20260901920847/codie-sanchez-money-wealth-entrepreneurship/)) To Entrepreneur that month: "If you don't have it, the world really asserts its will on you." ([Entrepreneur, September 16, 2026](https://www.entrepreneur.com/entrepreneurs/she-taught-people-to-become-millionaires-then-she-started-giving-the-advice-no-business-book-dares-to-admit))

The Type 8 reading starts here, with money as the thing that keeps other people's will off you. It is also a brand story. Its central scene has changed with retelling, from an old folks' home on the border in [a 2016 video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2_8oqoirNHM) to cartel violence in the 2026 versions, and only the class project is independently documented. Neither type explains the first career well. A twenty-year-old who expected a story about separated families to change the world was after something other than a score or a shield.

## "Maybe he was right"

Sanchez tells two stories about being turned down early in finance, where, she told HOLA!, "I was the only Latina in my group so many times, I can barely count." In the 2026 version, set at Vanguard, her first employer, she expected a promotion and was "told that I wasn't a team player because I never joined the happy hours." Her vow, as she gave it to Entrepreneur: "I am never going to let somebody hold me back because of my bank account."

The 2017 version is stranger and more useful. At a firm she does not name, she noticed there was no international arm, wrote a proposal, and heard from her boss that he might hire her to run it. "Hours later, he told me he had to offer the job to someone else." The reason: "I was told that everyone thought I would leave dead bodies behind me to get whatever I wanted."

The interviewer asked whether the boss had really said that. "Yep. And maybe he was right. It was a big realization for me. I thought that I was driven, but I was putting a different message out." She says she got the idea approved somewhere else, and then: "I became more aware of how people perceive me. I adapted." She added a postscript: "now, the woman they gave the job to instead of me is a member of my team." Asked for the takeaway, she said, "I think it's about personal brand."

The same interview holds the opposite note. "I just never accept the word 'no.' I hate that word," she said. It also holds her theory of advancement, "There's not a faster way to the top than to be known and wanted," and a habit: "I have a journal I've kept for fifteen years." In it, she said, she lists each year's accomplishments. By that arithmetic it began in her mid-teens.

Both strands were present before she had a brand to protect. The dead-bodies answer is the cleaner test. Told that colleagues saw her as ruthless, she did not dispute the judges. She decided she had been sending the wrong signal, changed how she came across, and kept a tally that ends with the woman who got the job on her team. That suggests a person who treats a bad reputation as a fault in her own presentation. Someone organized mainly around not being controlled would have more reason to reject the verdict than to study it. The limit is plain: this is one telling, from 2017, of an unnamed boss, and no one else in the room has spoken.

## Leaving finance like a "scaredy-cat"

Her account of leaving First Trust, where she says she built a Latin America business, is a dispute over who set the rules. "I think I saw the writing on the wall that it wasn't my casino. They weren't my chips," she told [Steven Bartlett](/personality-analysis/steven-bartlett) in 2023. In the next breath she conceded the point: "they were writing the rules, as is fair. It was their business." ([The Diary of a CEO, June 22, 2023](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XkxZCJ2pYqs)) Two months later, on her own channel, she said the people who ran the company told her, "We don't like the way you're building it," and wanted a friend of theirs to run it ([her YouTube channel, August 26, 2023](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1PeQ_dXKy80)). In 2026 she compressed her corporate exits into "I got told I was unemployable," and judged the people who said it: "That's all actually fair." First Trust has given no account in any source found here.

The exit was not a walkout. She told Bartlett, "I'm like a little bit of a scaredy-cat," and said she "needed something where I could have like a soft landing." She bought small businesses on the side for years first, and said on her channel, "So I tried to figure out a way to derisk my life." To HOLA! in 2026: "I might have been more brave at twenty-one than I was at thirty."

What she says she felt inside finance was success by someone else's measure. "I think I was probably what every Hispanic mother and father wants for their kid," she told Hispanic Executive in 2026. Of the career itself she said, "The problem was it felt empty." ([Hispanic Executive, September 28, 2026](https://hispanicexecutive.com/codie-sanchez-own-or-be-owned/)) In the 2023 video she described people who imitate what others want until they catch it and "realize they were chasing somebody else the whole time. And that's what happened to me." Entrepreneur quotes her on the reaction at home: "My parents didn't understand how I could leave a job like finance, a job they thought was so lucky, the best I could ever get."

## The mountain she turned back on

At the end of the 2023 interview, Bartlett put a closing question to her: when had she been most afraid? She chose a three-day glacier climb on Mount Baker with a group of women. "I like kind of thought I was tougher than them," she said. Below the final ice wall she decided to stop.

She asked herself, "what am I trying to prove?" Then she named the fear: "it wasn't even the heights that was so scary or the cold. It was like, am I like a failure by not doing this tough thing that everybody else is going to do?" What she told her friends goes well past mountaineering: "I just realized up here that most of my life I've been trying to like please a bunch of people to goals I don't care about." She went down. "I felt like it took more courage for me to not do the thing than do the thing."

Asked for her worst fear, a person who says she comes off as tough described being the one who taps out in front of her peers, and the lesson she drew was about pleasing people. Neither is the usual language of someone whose central concern is power.

Earlier in the same conversation she said that ending her first marriage was her decision, and that she could only do it by setting a date: "I was such a wuss I couldn't do it unless I put it on a calendar." Her father, she said, gave the cost a name: "there's always an exit tax to freedom." In a 2022 podcast clip she described the months before it: "being in the marriage was part of my identity and I didn't know how to let go of it and I felt like I was failing and there was massive shame and guilt around it" ([The Brave Table, March 22, 2022](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4bJSVDGpV0k)). That is her account of her own experience, and this profile goes no further into the marriage than that.

## The case for Type 8

The Eight reading deserves its strongest form, because her most direct statement of motive belongs to it. After "I don't ever want anybody to have control over me or my life again," she described the attitude that went with it: "I'll become every single thing that you said I wouldn't," she told Howes, and summed up the mood: "I was angry." When he offered, "You were trying to prove yourself," she said, "Oh, yeah."

The theme is older than the book it now sells. In 2023 she defined wealth to Bartlett as "having no master. That's what rich looks like for me." Back on his show in 2024, she said she stayed at most jobs under two years until First Trust, where she lasted five "because I had ownership," and called herself "really intense" and "kind of hard to work for sometimes" ([The Diary of a CEO, August 12, 2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IYu_PDPqKFc)). In 2026 she said, "I always jokingly call myself the business bully," because people need someone to push them into hard things. Of her Myers-Briggs result, a different system from the Enneagram, she joked that it was "something close to a dictator sounding and I thought well that sounds right."

She disclaims the need to be liked. Helping others get rich is "not because I'm nice I'm really not that nice," she told [Chris Williamson](/personality-analysis/chris-williamson) in 2023, and of her marriage she said, "our thing is it's us against the world" ([Modern Wisdom, October 19, 2023](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cG8ih5DwOlM)). A fan's praise, she said in 2026, is "totally uninteresting to me." She even calls the visible part of her job a chore: "Do I really want to be on social media every day? No. But it serves my goal," she told Entrepreneur.

Anger as fuel, autonomy as the definition of rich, toughness as a self-image, a marriage framed as an alliance: that is a real Eight case. One word in it stays unexplained. She says nobody will control her "again," and she has not said who did. Nothing in this record supports a guess.

## Where the Eight reading strains

It strains first on how she says she gets her way. "I try in my conversations with my team to almost say nothing about what my highest value is," she told Williamson in 2026. Owners, she said, "try to be a dictator to our employees" or "try to be a doormat." Her alternative: "I think we don't sell enough inside of our companies." The goal she described is to be "so persuasive to the people around you in business that you can get them to do the things they want to do without them even realizing that it was your idea, not theirs." ([Modern Wisdom, September 3, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2d4u80LmRQ8)) That is a seller's method, described by the person using it.

It strains again on what she says hurts. Across five tellings from 2017 to 2026, she names failure or how she was seen: the dead-bodies verdict, the marriage, the mountain, the empty account, and the infertility described below. In none of them does she describe confronting the person who crossed her. She grants the verdicts instead: "maybe he was right," "as is fair," "it was my fault."

A third detail is small and revealing. Explaining in 2023 why she makes content, she said, "I don't ever want to be the person standing in line at the club. I want to own the club." Trying to talk her way into a party, she added, "feels gross, and it feels thirsty. I don't want to be thirsty." Owning the club is an Eight's sentence. Worrying about looking thirsty is a concern with how wanting appears to other people.

The weighting is an inference and should be read as one. Her control statements match the product she sells. "I think ownership = freedom," she told HOLA!, and she would have reason to say so whatever her deepest motive. Her failure statements cut against the image of a self-made operator, which is why this profile gives them more weight. The reading should flip if people who have worked for or against her describe habitual open confrontation and indifference to how she is seen.

## The thing she could not brute-force

_Own or Be Owned_ argues that a business which cannot run without its owner is a trap. Sanchez says she is the case study. "I mean that literally, because I had built the opposite and I didn't have a name for it at the time," she told HOLA! of the subtitle. She told Williamson that she once turned down an invitation to Richard Branson's island: "I told myself that I didn't have time. If I left the business, it would fail." Her verdict now: "It was just a lie I told myself." She calls the pattern "the hero complex in business."

The identity under it is one she names often. "I am a workhorse," she told Hispanic Executive in 2026. On The Mother Daze: "I'm a workhorse. Like I get off on working all the time. I used to think that was my identity." And then: "I feel like the book kind of came around cuz I needed it." Workhorse is also one of the owner archetypes in her book.

On June 22, 2026 she announced through PEOPLE that she was pregnant after IVF. "My whole life, I've been the one who handles it, who doesn't need help," she said. "Getting pregnant was the thing I could not brute-force. I had to learn how to ask, and how to want something I might not get." She added, "You don't see the part where I have to ask a Navy SEAL to please just hold me because I cannot do it by myself." Three days later she posted her own account, addressed to women who have struggled to conceive: "I'm pregnant and thrilled. AND YET I've been failing at it for years." She wrote that she had been "blaming myself every seven seconds." ([X, June 25, 2026](https://x.com/Codie_Sanchez/status/2070213306883920099))

She has given two durations. "It took three years," she told PEOPLE; her post says she was "5 years into not getting pregnant." Both figures are hers and may measure different things. These were composed statements, written partly to comfort other women. Even so, she reached for the word she used for the bank account, the mountain and the marriage: failing.

Her husband, Chris Petkas, is a former Navy SEAL. "He runs our investment and holdco," she said in August 2026, while she runs the media and advisory business. "When I come home, Chris is actually kind of the alpha," she said. At the office the roles reverse: "I'm a crazy person. I have all the opinions non-stop." ([Valeria Lipovetsky, August 26, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=h1vf7vhGrQ0))

In that interview the host suggested motherhood would test whether Sanchez owns her business or is owned by it. She answered that most of what she calls her "33 companies" already run without her: "I am not a hero. I am a character in a play." The count is her own. Contrarian Thinking is a different case, since its media is built on her name, face and voice, which is this profile's reading of its marketing and not a reported fact. Whether its output holds during her leave, expected from December 2026, will be the first public test of her book's claim.

## What the record cannot show yet

The largest hole is testimony. The outside descriptions that exist come from writers and hosts producing friendly coverage; Entrepreneur's Stephanie Schomer calls her "direct, honest, and supportive, with a cut-through-the-bullshit energy." Nobody who has worked for her, competed with her or paid for her programs has been found on record describing how she behaves when crossed. That is the observation most likely to change the type.

Her business claims are contested, and the most specific criticism concerns the laundromats at the center of her origin story. By her own account in July 2023 she bought her first for about $100,000, added two more, "and we sold that for close to a million bucks." She called laundromats "the gateway drug," a good business to learn on, and told the host, who she said could find a higher return, "For you, I'd say don't buy a laundromat." ([Young and Profiting, July 3, 2023](https://youngandprofiting.com/codie-sanchez-7-boring-businesses-you-can-buy-right-now-to-replace-your-income-e231/)) A YouTube critic put it this way in July 2026: "She's never stopped selling that laundromat story. As far as I can tell, she just stopped owning them." ([Hard Hat Economics, July 17, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OuL0KNJijz0)) She disclosed the sale herself. The figures are hers alone, and no regulator action or news investigation alleging that she misled customers was found as of October 10, 2026.

What the record does show is a person who keeps score and has started to say so. "I don't optimize for fun," she told Howes. Her life's motto, she said, is a borrowed line about reaching the end able to say, "I used everything you gave me. I have not one drop left." The owner of that motto has now written a book telling owners to make themselves unnecessary, and says she wrote it because she needed it.

When Lipovetsky asked which part of her identity she feared motherhood might change, Sanchez did not pick the company. The scary part, she said, "is what will I want afterwards." Then: "And that I have no idea." She explained: "I have always kind of gone after exactly what I wanted. But the scary part is what if you want something totally different?"
