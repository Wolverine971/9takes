In an interview published on September 21, 2026, three days after her second book came out, Lewis Howes quoted its claim that most owners are owned by what they build and asked Codie Sanchez when that had been true of her. She answered with the day she walked into a bank to borrow against one of her businesses. The banker, as she tells it, said the account she thought held about a million dollars had no money in it. "Excuse me, can you please turn that thing around so I can see what's on that screen?" she recalls asking. The account "was basically to zero." She went to her head of operations, who by her account told her, "I did tell you this one time that it might be tight this summer," and had hidden from her that the business was almost out of money.

She remembers sitting in the dark and calling her father in the middle of the night, sure she would lose the business. His answer, in her telling: "If you haven't sat in the dark in the middle of the night with no idea of what to do next, you're not really in the game." Then she named what had hurt: "it was so painful. Like just my identity and that I was a failure because I had let an entire business fail." Of the manager, she said, "it was my fault for not tracking him, obviously."

She tells the story as a lesson: track performance consistently, or "expect this to happen to you." Her site carries a differently worded version of her father's line. She does not name or date the business, and nobody else has told the story. ([The School of Greatness, September 21, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a_5-iAEMRpE))

A little over twenty minutes later, Howes asked what her real dream is. For "this current stage," she said, it is ownership for more people: "the world is better when more of us own a little piece of something" ([at about 1:11:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a_5-iAEMRpE&t=4250s)). But she began with where the dream started. "At first, early on for me," it was this: "I don't ever want anybody to have control over me or my life again."

That early motive is the one her company sells. The pledge on Contrarian Thinking's site ends, "I will not do what I am told, I will do what is required." ([Contrarian Thinking](https://www.contrarianthinking.co/about)) The injury in the bank story is a different one, being a failure, and she described setbacks that way before the book existed. Which of the two she is organized around is the question of this profile.

Sanchez is the founder and CEO of Contrarian Thinking, a media, advisory and investment company that teaches people to buy and run small businesses such as laundromats, car washes and roofing companies. She grew up in Phoenix, where, Entrepreneur reports, her father owned a company selling granite and her mother taught special education ([Entrepreneur, September 16, 2026](https://www.entrepreneur.com/entrepreneurs/she-taught-people-to-become-millionaires-then-she-started-giving-the-advice-no-business-book-dares-to-admit)). She studied journalism at Arizona State and worked in finance at Vanguard, Goldman Sachs, State Street and First Trust; Entrepreneur adds Entourage Effect Capital. She wrote _Main Street Millionaire_ and _Own or Be Owned: Build a Business So Good It Doesn't Need You_, released on September 18, 2026 ([press release](https://codiesanchez.com/own-or-be-owned-press-release/)). In June 2026 she and her husband, Chris Petkas, a former Navy SEAL, announced that they expect their first child in December 2026 ([PEOPLE via AOL](https://www.aol.com/articles/entrepreneur-codie-sanchez-pregnant-expecting-140000000.html)).

## What is Codie Sanchez's personality type?

Codie Sanchez most likely fits Enneagram Type 3, the Achiever, at medium confidence. Her slogans are about control, and she meets a boss's "no" by going around it or leaving. But in several of her own setback stories, the injury she names is failing, shame or how she came across. Type 8, the Challenger, is a close alternative.

On a [Type 3](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-3) reading she steers by results other people can see and most wants to avoid being the one who failed. On a [Type 8](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-8) reading she steers by autonomy and most wants to avoid living under someone else's power. Both fit a person who quit jobs in order to own things. As of October 2026, no statement by her of her own type has been found.

The test used here is narrower. When a setback or a hostile judgment landed, what does she say it damaged, and what did she do next? Every answer in this record is her own telling, and that limit applies to everything below.

## Why she left journalism for money

As a journalism student at Arizona State, Sanchez was one of 17 students whose class project on families separated by the U.S.-Mexico border, "Divided Families," won a 2009 Robert F. Kennedy Journalism Award in the college print category ([Cronkite School, April 12, 2009](https://cronkite.asu.edu/news/2009/cronkite-students-win-international-rfk-award)). Her own site, as read in October 2026, says the coverage "earned her" the award ([codiesanchez.com](https://codiesanchez.com/about/)). In her own tellings from 2016 and 2017 she says "we." The university's record shows a team award for a class project.

In 2017, before Contrarian Thinking existed, an interviewer from Hispanic Executive raised the award. "Right, but I thought we were going to change the world," she answered. She was twenty then, she said. A mentor supplied the next move: "you find the centers of power. And that's money." ([Hispanic Executive, June 15, 2017](https://hispanicexecutive.com/codie-sanchez/))

In 2026 she drew the lesson more sharply. Money, she told HOLA! of her border reporting, "decided who had protection, who had options, and who could end up dead in the desert and no one cared." ([HOLA!, September 1, 2026](https://www.hola.com/us/lifestyle/20260901920847/codie-sanchez-money-wealth-entrepreneurship/))

The Type 8 reading starts here, with money as the thing that keeps other people's will off you. It is also a story she tells for a living, and its emphasis has moved. Her scripted [2016 video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2_8oqoirNHM) turns on a bedridden woman in a border old folks' home asking whether the coverage meant help was coming; the 2026 versions lead with violence. Both elements are already in the 2016 and 2017 tellings, so the versions are compatible. Only the class project is independently documented.

Neither type explains the first career well. A twenty-year-old who expected a story about separated families to change the world was after something other than a score or a shield. That motive is known only from her later telling, and she says it survived the move: "I still want to change the world," she said in 2017, by "helping put Latinos, women, and other minorities in positions of power." Like her 2026 answer to Howes, that purpose fits either reading, as autonomy for others or as impact others can see, so this profile does not use it to tell the types apart.

## "Maybe he was right"

Sanchez has told two stories about being passed over early in finance. The 2017 one names no firm, but its details resemble her accounts of Vanguard, so the two may be one episode. That is this profile's inference. If it is right, the stated reason and the moral both changed between tellings.

The one she told Entrepreneur in 2026 is set at Vanguard, her first finance job. "People weren't that kind to people who were broke and didn't come from anything," she said. She expected a promotion and was "told that I wasn't a team player because I never joined the happy hours." Her vow: "I am never going to let somebody hold me back because of my bank account."

The one she told Hispanic Executive in 2017 is stranger and more useful. She introduced it as a story about refusing: "I just never accept the word 'no.' I hate that word." She proposed an international arm, and her boss said he might hire her to run it. "Hours later, he told me he had to offer the job to someone else." The reason: "I was told that everyone thought I would leave dead bodies behind me to get whatever I wanted."

The interviewer asked whether the boss had really said that. Her whole answer: "Yep. And maybe he was right. It was a big realization for me. I thought that I was driven, but I was putting a different message out. But when they told me 'you can't,' I went and got the green light somewhere else. I became more aware of how people perceive me. I adapted. And then I just worked hard. I refused to let up. And now, the woman they gave the job to instead of me is a member of my team."

Asked for the takeaway, she said, "I think it's about personal brand," and tied it to being Latino in those rooms: "Don't hide it, but be aware." The same interview holds her theory of advancement, "There's not a faster way to the top than to be known and wanted," and a habit, a journal "I've kept for fifteen years" in which she lists each year's accomplishments.

The interview predates Contrarian Thinking but not her brand. Hispanic Executive captioned her "CEO, Keynote Speaker, & Investor, CS Inc.," and she had posted the scripted origin video six months earlier. A finance employee already building a public persona is what a Three reading expects.

The episode shows two things. She refused the decision: she took the idea elsewhere and did not let up. She accepted the perception: she changed how she came across. Circumstance explains part of that. She told HOLA! in 2026, "I was the only Latina in my group so many times, I can barely count," and a junior employee in that position has career reasons to manage how she is read whatever her type. What circumstance does not explain is that years later, unprompted, she still granted the verdict and had made a method of it. That suggests a person who treats a bad reputation as a fault in her own presentation. A pragmatic Eight could adapt for tactical reasons too, so the episode leans toward Three without settling it. The limit is plain: this is one telling, from 2017, of an unnamed boss, and no one else in the room has spoken.

## Leaving finance like a "scaredy-cat"

Her account of leaving First Trust is a dispute over who set the rules. She told [Steven Bartlett](/personality-analysis/steven-bartlett) in 2023 that she was "fighting a little bit with the CEO," and that when managing directors told her to do it their way, her answer was "So no, I'm going to run the business this way." Then: "I think I saw the writing on the wall that it wasn't my casino. They weren't my chips." She conceded the point almost at once: "they were writing the rules, as is fair. It was their business." She started buying businesses on the side "because I wasn't sure I wanted to work for somebody ever again." ([The Diary of a CEO, June 22, 2023](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XkxZCJ2pYqs))

She has told the departure both ways: a video on her channel two months later is titled "I Quit My $500,000 Job After I Learned THIS" ([YouTube, August 26, 2023](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1PeQ_dXKy80)), and in 2026 she told HOLA! she "got kicked out of the corporate club." First Trust has given no account in any source found here.

The exit was not a walkout, and it did not end her finance career. "I'm like a little bit of a scaredy-cat," she told Bartlett, and said she "needed something where I could have like a soft landing." She told HOLA! that her paycheck funded the down payment on her first laundromat. Entrepreneur's 2026 profile dates that purchase to more than ten years earlier and says she left finance only when the small businesses let her "close the door." Contrarian Thinking followed, in 2019 by her press release and in 2020 by her own site.

That caution, and a father she calls in a crisis, would also fit a security-minded [Type 6](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6). The next section says why this profile does not lead with it.

What she says she felt inside finance was success by someone else's measure. "I think I was probably what every Hispanic mother and father wants for their kid," she told Hispanic Executive in 2026. ([Hispanic Executive, September 28, 2026](https://hispanicexecutive.com/codie-sanchez-own-or-be-owned/)) In the 2023 channel video she said people who imitate what others want "realize they were chasing somebody else the whole time. And that's what happened to me." Her site says her parents "inspired her to take risks"; Entrepreneur quotes her on that last exit: "My parents didn't understand how I could leave a job like finance."

## The mountain she turned back on

The 2023 Bartlett interview ended with the show's closing ritual, a question left by the previous guest: "When was the moment you were most afraid?" Her answer was a three-day glacier climb on Mount Baker with a group of women. "I like kind of thought I was tougher than them," she said. Below the final ice wall she decided to stop.

She asked herself, "what am I trying to prove?" Then she named the fear: "it wasn't even the heights that was so scary or the cold. It was like, am I like a failure by not doing this tough thing that everybody else is going to do?" Her friends tried to talk her back up. "I'm like, no," she said, and what she told them goes well past mountaineering: "I just realized up here that most of my life I've been trying to like please a bunch of people to goals I don't care about." She went down.

Asked when she had been most afraid, a person who says she comes off as tough described being the one who taps out in front of her peers. An Eight also hates to look weak, so that fear fits either reading. The lesson is what separates them, in this profile's judgment: a life spent pleasing people is not how someone centered on power usually accounts for herself. The story also weakens a Six reading. She says she was scared of falling, then that the heights were not the worst of it. Quitting in front of the others was.

Earlier in the same conversation she said that ending her first marriage was her decision, and that she could only do it by setting a date: "I was such a wuss I couldn't do it unless I put it on a calendar." In a 2022 podcast clip she described what she called "the ramp up to it": "being in the marriage was part of my identity and I didn't know how to let go of it and I felt like I was failing and there was massive shame and guilt around it" ([The Brave Table, March 22, 2022](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4bJSVDGpV0k)). That is her account of her own experience, and this profile goes no further into the marriage than that.

## The case for Type 8

The Eight reading deserves its strongest form, because her most direct statement of motive belongs to it. After "I don't ever want anybody to have control over me or my life again," she said what she meant: "I don't ever want to have to work for somebody I don't like or to spend a moment of my day in somebody else's life architecting their dream instead of mine." The word "again" points at employers, as her First Trust account does, and this profile reads no more into it than that. Then came the attitude: "I'll become every single thing that you said I wouldn't," she told Howes, and summed up the mood: "I was angry." When he offered, "You were trying to prove yourself," she said, "Oh, yeah."

The theme is older than the book it now sells. In 2023 she defined wealth to Bartlett as "having no master. That's what rich looks like for me." Back on his show in 2024, she said she stayed at most jobs under two years until First Trust, where she lasted five "because I had ownership," and called herself "really intense" and "kind of hard to work for sometimes" ([The Diary of a CEO, August 12, 2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IYu_PDPqKFc)). Of her marriage she told [Chris Williamson](/personality-analysis/chris-williamson) in 2023, "our thing is it's us against the world" ([Modern Wisdom, October 19, 2023](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cG8ih5DwOlM)).

Anger as fuel, autonomy as the definition of rich, toughness as a self-image, a marriage framed as an alliance: that is a real Eight case. What she does when a superior says no adds to it.

## Where each reading strains

Take the two halves of the test separately. What she did next, when a person stood in her way, was mostly refuse. She got the vetoed idea approved elsewhere. When a Goldman Sachs boss "wouldn't allow me to travel for a job interview," she said in the same 2017 interview, "I paid my own way." Overruled at First Trust, she argued, then left to own things. Shown an empty account, she went to the manager. This half does not separate the types. If anything it leans Eight.

What she says was damaged depends on the setback. Held back by a gatekeeper, at Vanguard in the 2026 telling and at First Trust, she names control. When the judgment was on her, or nobody stood in the way, she names how she came across or failing: the dead-bodies verdict, the marriage, the mountain, the empty account. Three tellings involve a person who judged or failed her, and in each she contested the outcome but granted the judgment: of the 2017 boss, "maybe he was right"; of First Trust's owners in 2023, "as is fair"; of the manager in the 2026 telling, "it was my fault." The mountain had no adversary, and this profile does not treat the marriage as having one, so neither can test whether she confronts anyone.

The weighting is an inference, and one rule has to cover both sides. Her control statements match the product she sells: "I think ownership = freedom," she told HOLA!. Her 2026 failure statements match it too: _Own or Be Owned_ is about having built the wrong thing, and she tells the empty-account story as its cautionary tale. Whether that year's confessional register is hers or her market's is an open question. Some of the words are ordinary besides: "failing" is what people say of a divorce and "my fault" is what owners are taught to say, so those weigh less than the mountain and the dead-bodies answer.

What predates the campaign is thinner and counts for more. On the control side: never accepting "no" (2017), "having no master" and the casino (2023). On the other: "personal brand" and "maybe he was right" (2017), a marriage that was "part of my identity" (2022), the mountain and "chasing somebody else" (2023). Both strands are old. Type 3 leads on what the second holds that an Eight seldom volunteers: a verdict on her image accepted and made into a method, a self-description as fearful, and the statement that she spent most of her life pleasing people. That is a judgment about how the types tend to talk, which is why confidence stays at medium.

One 2023 remark to Bartlett holds both readings: "I don't ever want to be the person standing in line at the club. I want to own the club." Trying to talk her way into a party, she added, "feels gross, and it feels thirsty. I don't want to be thirsty." Owning the club is an Eight's sentence. Worrying about looking thirsty is a concern with how wanting appears to other people. It is also distaste for having to ask, which an Eight shares, so the remark shows the question without answering it.

The Three reading strains on behavior. A person steering by visible success left a prestige track to build her name on laundromats and car washes. On [The Mother Daze](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3A4m9q9fkcQ) in 2026 she pictured a tombstone reading "managing director" and said, "That doesn't interest me at all." She calls visibility a chore: "Do I really want to be on social media every day? No. But it serves my goal," she told Entrepreneur. A fan calling her cool is "totally uninteresting to me," she said on the same podcast; one who bought a business because of her is different: "That means you did something." The Three answer is that she traded titles for another scoreboard, results she caused, and that "boring" is itself a brand. That answer is convenient. An Eight needs no such move to explain someone who walked off other people's ladders.

The reading should flip if people who have worked for or against her describe habitual open confrontation and indifference to how she is seen.

## The thing she could not brute-force

_Own or Be Owned_ argues that a business which cannot run without its owner is a trap. "I mean that literally, because I had built the opposite and I didn't have a name for it at the time," she told HOLA! of the subtitle. She told Williamson in 2026 that she once declined an invitation to Richard Branson's island because "If I left the business, it would fail," and now calls that "just a lie I told myself" ([Modern Wisdom, September 3, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2d4u80LmRQ8)).

The identity under it is one she names often. On The Mother Daze: "I'm a workhorse. Like I get off on working all the time. I used to think that was my identity." And then: "I feel like the book kind of came around cuz I needed it."

On June 22, 2026 she announced through PEOPLE that she was pregnant after IVF. "My whole life, I've been the one who handles it, who doesn't need help," she said. "Getting pregnant was the thing I could not brute-force. I had to learn how to ask, and how to want something I might not get."

Three days later she posted her own account, written for "every woman who has struggled with or been shamed trying to get pregnant" and partly in answer to strangers who had told her "you never prioritized family." "I'm pregnant and thrilled. AND YET I've been failing at it for years," she wrote. When one such comment arrived, she said, she was "blaming myself every seven seconds." Then she turned the word around for her readers: "You are not lazy. You are not late. You are not failing." ([X, June 25, 2026](https://x.com/Codie_Sanchez/status/2070213306883920099))

These were composed statements, and "failing" is a common word for infertility, so this profile does not count the post as evidence for a type. What it shows is narrower: a verdict she passed on herself and would not let anyone pass on other women.

In August 2026 the YouTube host Valeria Lipovetsky suggested motherhood would test whether Sanchez owns her business or is owned by it ([interview, August 26, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=h1vf7vhGrQ0)). Most of her "33 companies," she answered, already run without her: "I am not a hero. I am a character in a play." The count is hers. Contrarian Thinking is a different case: its media is built on her name, face and voice, which is this profile's reading of its marketing, not a reported fact. As of October 2026 no announcement of a leave had been found, and whether that company can run without her had not been tested in public.

## What the record cannot show yet

The largest hole, as of October 2026, is testimony. The outside descriptions that exist come from writers and hosts producing friendly coverage. Nobody who has worked for her, competed with her or paid for her programs has been found on record describing how she behaves when crossed. That is the observation most likely to change the type.

The most specific public criticism is about laundromats, the business she is best known for teaching: that she went on promoting them after selling her own. By her account in July 2023 she bought her first for about $100,000, added two more, "and we sold that for close to a million bucks." In the same interview she told the host, "For you, I'd say don't buy a laundromat." ([Young and Profiting, July 3, 2023](https://youngandprofiting.com/codie-sanchez-7-boring-businesses-you-can-buy-right-now-to-replace-your-income-e231/))
