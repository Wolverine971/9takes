On November 14, 2020, Mark Fischbach and his friend Ethan Nestor sat in front of a computer in black and white suits, confirmed that they wanted to permanently delete a YouTube channel, and deleted it. The channel was called Unus Annus. It had posted a video every day for a year, and its premise from the first upload was that it would be destroyed on the last. More than 1.5 million people watched it go, Polygon reported two days later, and the man who "made a name for himself with exuberant gaming videos" was "quiet and contemplative" while it happened. ([Polygon, November 16, 2020](https://www.polygon.com/2020/11/16/21569701/unus-annus-markiplier-crankgameplays-channel-delete-reuploads-youtube-mark-fischbach-ethan-nestor))

Fischbach is the YouTuber known as Markiplier. He became popular with playthroughs of horror games such as _Five Nights at Freddy's_, and his main channel had more than 38 million subscribers as of August 7, 2026. Unus Annus was a second channel, started with Nestor on November 15, 2019, and it had 4.56 million subscribers by its last minutes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Markiplier&oldid=1379210842)). Deleting it erased a year of daily work while the main channel carried on.

The ending was also what the channel sold, and the final stream was its biggest event. He warned viewers on a livestream against hosting copies of the videos, Polygon reported. His own account of its purpose is about endings. On a panel in June 2026 he called it "an exercise in letting go," about "knowing that something was going to go and there's nothing you could do about it, but making the most of the time that you had." ([Colin and Samir, June 11, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W5Irjxu1FVI&t=1535s))

His feature film _Iron Lung_, released on January 30, 2026, was built on a rule too. He financed, wrote, directed and starred in it ([BBC Newsbeat, February 16, 2026](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c2lg5r414wpo)), and he set it inside a submarine that the story never leaves, because the short horror game it adapts never leaves one. "I would have loved to be able to cut away to another scene," he told the creator-hosted podcast Lemonade Stand. A moment later: "but that's not the game. So, it can't be the movie." And: "I cannot break the rules." ([Lemonade Stand, February 25, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cB4dtw8xwMI&t=905s))

None of this sounds like the man on the channel, who is loud, absurd and quick to undercut himself: the fundraiser that brought the Cancer Research Institute $500,000 was a calendar he called "Tasteful Nudes" ([CRI](https://www.cancerresearch.org/stories/supporters/mark-fischbach-markiplier)). He says of himself, "I don't really quit," but that he goes "from idea to idea all the time. It's part of having ADHD." ([Slate, February 10, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=k29YAAX5u3w&t=455s)) This profile follows one question: why someone who works that way keeps finishing things inside rules he says he cannot break, and who he thinks is checking.

## What is Mark Fischbach's personality type?

The public record does not settle Mark Fischbach's type. Type 1, the Reformer, leads narrowly: where what he wants and what he thinks is right come apart, he reports following the rule. Type 7 explains his appetite for the next thing, and Type 3 his wish for respect. Nearly all the evidence is his own account.

As used here, a [Type 1](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-1) runs on an inner standard: a sense of how a thing ought to be done, a critic that notices the gap, and anger that gets turned into effort instead of being shown. A [Type 7](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-7) keeps options open and moves toward what is next, away from confinement and pain. A Type 3 measures worth by success and by how it is seen. All three describe parts of him. The question is which one explains his choices when they pull apart. This is a reading of public statements and observable choices, not a diagnosis.

## The standard he says he inherited

Most of what is known about his early life comes from him, chiefly a 2013 video called "Draw My Life." By that account he was born on a military base in Honolulu to a career Army father, and his parents later divorced. His father had met his mother while stationed in Korea, and the family settled in Cincinnati, where he grew up with a brother, Tom. He wanted to make video games. "My dad didn't agree with me," he says, and he went into engineering.

His father died of cancer on July 4, 2008, he told the [Cancer Research Institute](https://www.cancerresearch.org/stories/supporters/mark-fischbach-markiplier) in 2013. In the video he says, "the last words that he said to me were I love you so much, and I held my dad's hand as he died." ([Draw My Life, May 4, 2013](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6Sl-1X58ObY&t=462s)) Afterward, by the same account, he was stuck in "mind-numbing" engineering jobs: "I didn't have time for games, I didn't have an escape."

On May 20, 2022, Anthony Padilla, a fellow YouTuber who hosts an interview series, asked how his father had shaped his sense of right and wrong. "My dad was very much the kind of guy that never did anything half-ass," Fischbach said. "Always do the right thing, admit if you're wrong, own your mistakes." When Padilla said it sounded like a self-description, he answered that he would "like to think I embody that." ([Anthony Padilla](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pIF9p2aZdeo&t=553s))

In 2026 he used the phrase he had used about his father to describe what his audience can count on: "I'm not going to half-ass anything and I'm going to try it to the very end. If it fails, it fails and I'll crash and burn with it." Moments later he punctured it: "God, I sound good in these answers." ([Lemonade Stand](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cB4dtw8xwMI&t=5253s))

These are a son's words about a dead parent, drawn out by a leading question, and "half-ass" is a common phrase. They show the standard he claims and where he says he got it. They do not show that his father's death produced it.

The same 2022 interview has him describing what he did with anger. He agreed that he had been a very angry teenager and said: "That was one of the first things that I really started to try to take control of myself, is like my anger, because I really did not like where that led me." By his telling anger is something he judged and set out to manage. This profile found no one who knew him then on record.

## An escape story

The channel's origin, as he tells it, is a story about getting out. In 2013 he described a stretch around 2012 in which, by his telling, he lost a job and a relationship and ended up in a hospital, where surgeons took out his appendix and, later, what he called "a tumor the size of a fist in my adrenal gland." Recovering, he says, "it seemed like everything in my life was completely out of my control and I had no choice in the matter, so finally I decided that enough was enough and I was going to do what I wanted." Asked in 2022 what drove him to start, he said, "Just complete lack of control in my life."

That is a man leaving the path his father preferred, and jobs he called mind-numbing, to do what he wanted. It shares a word, control, with what he says about anger, but getting free and holding yourself back are different motives. On its face the origin story favors the Seven reading.

The start was trial and error. He told the Slate podcast in 2026 that YouTube "was like the fifth thing that I tried to do," and that he picked horror games because "people wanted to see it. I actually didn't really like playing horror games. I was scared." On NBC's TODAY he named the first hook: "having people pay any sort of attention to me. I was the quiet kid in school." ([TODAY, November 19, 2019](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=X7D5v1tGG1c&t=58s))

## The streak and the audience

In a late-night vlog in November 2014 he described what he thought he owed his viewers. He was, he said, in a position where "so many people are looking at me and so many people are judging what I do and so many people are looking to me for guidance and I have an obligation to fulfill that." ([An Important Message, November 23, 2014](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YG-vC3i0_Dw&t=125s))

Ten months later his housemate and collaborator Daniel Kyre died after a suicide attempt ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Markiplier&oldid=1379210842)). Fischbach opened the video announcing the death by apologizing for his silence, "considering I haven't missed a day's uploads since I started the YouTube channel." Most of the video is about a friend, "such a positive influence on a lot of people," whose life he wanted to celebrate. Among the things he praised was a standard. Kyre "inspired us to expect better from ourselves," he said, and with the small details of editing "he always took the time to make sure they were perfect." ([Lost a Friend, September 21, 2015](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J_cxoZLPyR0))

He was away about two weeks, by his count. In the video marking his return he said, "I wanted to make sure that I confronted it directly." He also reported what the break had felt like: "it's been eating away at the back of my mind, just like, you should really probably be recording something right now, people are probably losing their mind." He went straight on to the fear that viewers would have "abandoned me," called it "an irrational fear," and described it: "if I don't upload videos somehow I think that everything's going to come collapsing down." ([Markiplier Returns, October 5, 2015](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iWQ34ixgx-k&t=58s))

The thought he reports is about the audience: people losing their minds, then leaving. A daily schedule was also good business for a gaming channel, and fear of being dropped may explain all of it. The only signs of something more are his own word, irrational, and an apology for silence at the top of a death announcement. Reading those as an inner standard is this profile's inference, and a weak one. In these years the judge he names is outside him.

## The night he felt lost

On March 24, 2017, with his channel close to 17 million subscribers, he posted a tearful late-night vlog titled "I Feel Lost." It is the strongest evidence for the other reading of him, so it gets its own section.

He says at the start that he is "not depressed," and near the end, "I'm happier than I've ever been" and "I'm not quitting." "I'm actively turning down movies and TV roles and book deals," he says, and then: "I feel like I don't deserve it, you know, in a way, like I haven't earned it yet." The trouble is choice: "there's so many things that I could focus my time on that you end up focusing on nothing and you end up just kind of drifting."

He misses how the channel felt at the start, when he did not care about "the rules" or "how people thought YouTube videos should be done," and he fears he has lost "even the possibility of feeling that again," which he calls "my biggest fear." A little later: "I'm not afraid of failure. I'm afraid of disappointing you guys." What he resolves to do: "I have to take the right steps and I have to make the right choices and I have to make a conscious effort every day to be better." ([I Feel Lost, March 24, 2017](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fd-VFBOOL5c&t=205s))

The next morning he posted a second part. He said, "I just can't wait to fail," and closed on a rule for his life: "I'm never going to stop moving, not until the day I die." ([I Feel Lost pt. 2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QiJCBFArQcg&t=155s))

Paralysis among too many options, a swing from tears to appetite within hours, and a life described as motion: this is what a Seven sounds like when the horizon runs out. A One usually knows what ought to be done and suffers over not doing it. On this night Fischbach did not know. Two details pull the other way: he was refusing rewards he considered unearned, and his resolution was about right steps and right choices. Against them stand the fear he calls his biggest, which is lost novelty, and a founding indifference to rules, though those were other people's conventions and not rules he had set himself. The night belongs more to the Seven reading.

What followed was a spread of formats: a comedy tour, two interactive YouTube Originals he wrote and directed (2019 and 2022), Unus Annus, and from May 2021 a podcast with Wade Barnes and Bob Muyskens, friends since sixth grade and his first year of college ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Markiplier&oldid=1379210842)). That range fits a Seven. The long friendships fit less well, and so does an aim he says never changed: he told Slate he had been "slowly building my skills there over the past 14 years."

## Three years in one room

_Iron Lung_ began quickly, though not on a whim. David Szymanski, who made the game, said it came down to "him DMing me on Twitter out of the blue" after a few friendly messages, that Amy Nelson, Fischbach's partner, had told him not to "cold open with that," and that he was already thinking about a feature. ([Quad Damage Podcast, September 2, 2025](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MtKke2Pli9E&t=2985s))

The rule came with the game, and he gave Slate a plain reason for liking it: "I was naive and thought, 'Oh, one room will be an easier movie.' Much harder." Asked by YouTube's blog what a studio would have tried to fix, he named the slow opening and defended it: "The point is the monotony, the loneliness, the boredom." ([YouTube Official Blog, February 23, 2026](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/markiplier-iron-lung-interview/)) And he kept making YouTube videos the whole time, he says, because streaming is fun.

By his account he paid for it himself to keep the final say. On Lemonade Stand he said, "I'm a bit of a control freak in that regard and I don't like being beholden to anybody else's opinion besides the creative team." He also said that "no one was really bashing down my door," so the independence was partly forced.

The price, by his account: "it was 16-hour days for about 3 years." He said, "I had to postpone even my wedding to work on these things." ([Lemonade Stand](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cB4dtw8xwMI&t=4915s)) He married Amy Nelson on September 20, 2025 ([Dexerto, October 7, 2025](https://www.dexerto.com/youtube/markiplier-marries-amy-nelson-after-viral-engagement-rumors-3263392/)). On the night he judged the film finished, as he retold it on May 17, 2026, he drove home, told Nelson, "I think it's done," and then: "I break down for 30 minutes straight. I cried. Just curled up in a ball and cried. Not out of sadness." ([his channel](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U5Xpd8ja9bk&t=270s))

All of that is self-report, told after the film had succeeded, when hardship reads as heroism. No account from Nelson was found.

It took in $18.19 million in the United States and Canada on its opening weekend in 3,015 theaters, a close second to Sam Raimi's _Send Help_, according to Comscore figures reported by [NBC News on February 2, 2026](https://www.nbcnews.com/pop-culture/viral/markiplier-youtuber-iron-lung-film-box-office-hit-rcna257110). Its worldwide total was about $51.2 million as of May 2, 2026 ([Wikipedia's box-office summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Iron_Lung_%28film%29&oldid=1377060554)). The budget was widely reported as $3 million; he says, "It's actually a little over four." The reviews were mixed: the BBC reported a 61% score on Rotten Tomatoes and two-star ratings from Empire and The Guardian.

On February 3, 2026, he opened a stream titled "Iron Lung Weekend Results (Number... 1?)" by mock-solemnly celebrating a number-one daily ranking for that Monday. About ten minutes in: "the win condition isn't being number one. That's not the win condition. That's a fun little haha achievement. The win condition is that this actually came out at all." Later: "I'm proud of what I made," and, "this isn't the best I can do." ([stream](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eFaihZ0f58w&t=600s)) And he is easy on the result: "I recognize Iron Lung has plenty of flaws," he told Lemonade Stand, but he was "happy where it is and I'm not regretful of any of the decisions that I made."

Slate asked whether it mattered if critics did not love it. Not at all, he said: strangers do not know him, and while making it "I already hated myself more and this movie more than anyone" else could. Asked what does drive him, he said, "I am horribly addicted to the cycle of making things and pushing myself," and then: "Some people call me a masochist on that one. And I'm not a masochist. I just like seeing how far I can go." ([Slate](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=k29YAAX5u3w&t=1405s))

Szymanski is the one outside witness to the work that this profile found. He said Fischbach had spent so long building out the game's world that it was "definitely as much his as it is mine now. Probably even a little more his." Fischbach says Szymanski shares in the film's profits, and Szymanski called himself "the least unbiased source you could hear from."

## Where the judge sits

Since 2022 he has described the judge as internal. On a live panel of creators hosted by Colin and Samir in June 2026, talking about the judgment that comes with going on camera, he said, "no one's going to judge me harsher than I judge myself. So if I can do that and overcome my own doubt then nothing else would really matter to me." What he called his "guiding star" was the next clause: "I believe in myself enough to make up for the shortcomings that I know are there." ([Colin and Samir](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W5Irjxu1FVI&t=565s))

He had said it four years earlier in different words. "Yes, I have a standard I hold myself to, and yes, my self-worth is pretty much nailed to my ability to put my ideas into the world," he told Padilla, whose question had supplied the frame, and of his work: "it's also never good enough." He added, "I don't look back critically on my past projects," as terrible, "but it wasn't good enough."

The 2026 statements answer questions about outside judgment, after mixed reviews: strangers' praise and criticism, he told Slate, do not count because "you don't know me, so it doesn't matter." The earlier record puts the judge outside him altogether: the people "judging what I do" in 2014, the viewers who might forget him in 2015, the fear of "disappointing you guys" in 2017. Single comments from strangers may not reach him. The audience as a body has, for twelve years. And a driven Three, Four or Six will also say nobody judges them harder than they do. Where the judge sits cannot carry the type. What the judge measures might.

The three limits this profile opened with do not separate a One from a Seven. The upload streak is his own claim, and it answers to the audience. A channel announced with a one-year expiry is a commitment with the exit built in, and he ties it to mortality as well as to letting go: he told Padilla that during the biopsy of that tumor he learned "my life was finite." Urgency about limited time would explain his pace with no inner critic at all. The one-room film followed the game's own rule and looked easier to him at the start.

What may separate the readings is narrower. At a few points the easier or more rewarding option was open and he reports taking the other, for a reason he states as right or owed. In 2017 he had offers in hand and turned them down as unearned. In 2022 he called his income unfair and said his answer was to give it away. In 2026 he "would have loved" to open the film up and kept it shut, and he kept the slow opening he says a studio would have fixed. A Seven following appetite would more likely take the open door, and a Three the choice that plays better. That is this profile's inference from four moments in twelve years, and the reasons are all his own telling. It is enough to put Type 1 first. It is not enough to settle the question.

Two things count against it. He takes comfort quickly: learning that he had already had most of his possible time with his father was, he told Padilla, "the most comforting thing I've ever heard in my life," the consoling turn the Seven reading predicts. And a One's critic does not usually let a flawed film go as easily as he let this one go.

Only three things here rest on more than his word: the deletion, the self-financed one-room film, and one $500,000 donation confirmed by its recipient. Everything about motive is his own account, which is why the reading is held loosely.

## Where Type 7 and Type 3 still fit

The Seven reading has his own answer to what drives him. It has the origin story, the fear he called his biggest, the spread of formats, and the loud comic performer the audience subscribed to, which this profile cannot assign to temperament or to craft. It has his account of happiness. "I want a direction to climb," he told fans in 2026, because what makes him happy is "so long as I'm moving in a direction." On Lemonade Stand he put it as, "if I'm not getting better, then I'm not happy." He said that while describing "a new mountain to climb with movies." The record cannot fully separate "better" from "next." If they are the same hunger, the Seven reading is stronger than this profile allows. He attributes the idea-jumping to ADHD, which describes attention and says nothing about motive either way.

Against it stand one channel, one partner and the same friends for more than a decade, and the few moments where the open option was there and he took the bound one. The Seven reading explains more of what he says drives him. The One reading explains those refusals. That is a narrow lead.

[Type 3](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-3), a motive built on success and image, is closer than it first looks: his worth is tied to what he produces. He told Lemonade Stand that he made a feature because his online work lacked prestige: "to win and to gain some respect, I knew that I had to do a project." He says, "it's selfish a lot of it. I want to succeed," and that he wants "a reputation of quality." In 2017 he could not wait to "prove people wrong." Attention was the first hook, and he enjoyed being number one.

Where Type 3 strains is what he gives up. He told Padilla in 2022, "people don't understand just how much money falls out of the YouTube bucket. Like, it's unfair." His answer, he said, is, "I donate to charity." Of offers to put his name on products he said, "I say no thank you," because "if I'm making something it's because I really love it." Each has a Three reading. Charity builds a creator's standing, and no total for his giving has been verified. Refusing endorsements protects a reputation, and he sells his own clothing line. The deleted channel was the event. This profile found no clean test between One and Three, so Type 3 stays live.

One thing fits none of the three. He cries easily on camera, and what sets it off is being backed. In 2015 he thanked viewers "for continuing to believe in me." Asked on Slate about "tearing up about being number one," he said, "it was the fact that people believed in me." That twelve-year thread reads more like the need for support that marks Type 2 or Type 6 than like a One's standard. This profile leaves it unexplained, and it limits the answer to who is checking: partly, the audience.

## What the record cannot show

Nearly everything above is Fischbach describing himself, usually to friendly interviewers and mostly after a success. The outside voices are few and warm. That is what this profile's search had found as of October 11, 2026. Padilla, the Lemonade Stand hosts and Colin and Samir are fellow creators, and YouTube's blog belongs to the platform; Slate, the BBC, NBC and Polygon are outside press. Besides Szymanski there is Nestor, who told him on camera, in a show they made together, that "seeing how much you're willing to give up is extremely inspiring." As of the same date that clip survived only as a fan re-upload, the kind of copy he asked viewers not to host, so it is described here and not linked.

The search found no on-record account from Nelson, from Barnes or Muyskens, or from anyone who has disagreed with him at work. The nearest thing is a dispute this profile could not check at the source. According to a January 2, 2025 report by [Deltia's Gaming](https://web.archive.org/web/2025/https://deltiasgaming.com/the-entire-controversy-surrounding-markipliers-official-subreddit-explained/), he removed several moderators of his official subreddit in December 2024; a former head moderator complained that he had not reached out, and he replied that he had seen no attempt to contact him, only an inactive marker beside their names. The Reddit posts could not be retrieved, so neither account was checked.

What would test the reading is not final cut on his next film. As of [Gizmodo's June 29, 2026 report](https://gizmodo.com/iron-lung-creator-markiplier-on-how-his-place-in-hollywood-has-evolved-2000779184) he had not chosen one, and a Seven, a Three or an Eight would want the final say too, so that outcome tests only the control strand. The sharper signals are whether collaborators, once on record, describe him redoing work that misses a standard or moving on to the next idea; what he does when the right way and the well-received way diverge; and whether he describes finishing as a verdict on the work or as clearing the way for the next thing.

When he finally let the film go, the verdict he gave it was not praise. "Okay, I don't hate it anymore. Go," he recalled on Slate. His mother, he told YouTube's blog, saw it with his cousin from Korea, loved it, and had one note: "Don't do that again. No sequels." He had already told Slate what came next: "Can't wait for the next one."
