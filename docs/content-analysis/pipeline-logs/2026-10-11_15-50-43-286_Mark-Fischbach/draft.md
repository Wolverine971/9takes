On November 14, 2020, Mark Fischbach and his friend Ethan Nestor sat in front of a computer in black and white suits, confirmed that they wanted to permanently delete a YouTube channel, and deleted it. The channel was called Unus Annus. It had posted a video every day for a year, and its premise from the first upload was that it would be destroyed on the last. More than 1.5 million people watched it go, Polygon reported two days later, and the man who "made a name for himself with exuberant gaming videos" was "quiet and contemplative" while it happened. ([Polygon, November 16, 2020](https://www.polygon.com/2020/11/16/21569701/unus-annus-markiplier-crankgameplays-channel-delete-reuploads-youtube-mark-fischbach-ethan-nestor))

He also policed the ending. Polygon quoted him warning viewers on a livestream against hosting copies of the videos: "We're not joking around when it comes to that sort of thing." Looking back on the project, he said, "I firmly believe that the entirety of what something is cannot be truly appreciated until it ends." The channel had gained 4.56 million subscribers by its last minutes, according to [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markiplier), which also gives the date. Nothing but the premise required the deletion.

Five years later he made the same kind of choice with his own money on the table. He financed a feature film, set it inside a submarine that the story never leaves, and explained the confinement as fidelity to the short horror game it adapts: "I have to bend the rules as much as necessary to be able to tell the story as well as I can, but I cannot break the rules." ([Lemonade Stand, February 25, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cB4dtw8xwMI&t=905s))

This is also a man who says of himself, "I don't really quit," but that he goes "from idea to idea all the time. It's part of having ADHD." ([Slate, February 10, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=k29YAAX5u3w&t=455s)) His channel is loud and absurd, he calls himself "an impatient guy" ([his channel, May 17, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U5Xpd8ja9bk)), and the film began with a direct message to a stranger. This profile follows one question: why someone who starts things that way keeps finishing them inside rules he will not let himself break, and who he thinks is checking.

Fischbach, known online as Markiplier, was born on June 28, 1989, in Honolulu, and his channel had more than 38 million subscribers as of August 7, 2026 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markiplier)). He financed, wrote, directed and starred in _Iron Lung_ ([BBC Newsbeat, February 16, 2026](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c2lg5r414wpo)), which he released himself on January 30, 2026. It took in $18.19 million in the United States and Canada on its opening weekend in 3,015 theaters, a close second to Sam Raimi's _Send Help_, according to Comscore figures reported by [NBC News on February 2, 2026](https://www.nbcnews.com/pop-culture/viral/markiplier-youtuber-iron-lung-film-box-office-hit-rcna257110). Its worldwide total was about $51.2 million as of May 2, 2026 ([Wikipedia's box-office summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Lung_%28film%29)). The budget was widely reported as $3 million; he says, "It's actually a little over four." He married Amy Nelson on September 20, 2025 ([Dexerto, October 7, 2025](https://www.dexerto.com/youtube/markiplier-marries-amy-nelson-after-viral-engagement-rumors-3263392/)).

## What is Mark Fischbach's personality type?

Mark Fischbach, known as Markiplier, most likely fits Enneagram Type 1, the Reformer, at medium confidence. His costliest choices follow rules he set for himself, and he says nobody judges him more harshly than he does. Type 7, the Enthusiast, is the strongest alternative and explains how he starts things. Nearly all the evidence is his own account.

As used here, a [Type 1](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-1) is organized around an inner standard: a sense of how a thing ought to be done, a critic that notices the gap, and anger that gets converted into effort instead of being shown. A [Type 7](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-7) is organized around keeping options open and moving toward what is next, away from confinement and pain. Both describe parts of him. The question is which one explains the choices that cost him the most. This is a reading of public statements and observable choices, not a diagnosis.

## The standard he says he inherited

Most of what is known about his early life comes from him, chiefly a 2013 video called "Draw My Life." By that account he was born on a military base in Honolulu to a career Army father, and his parents later divorced. His father died of cancer on July 4, 2008, he told the [Cancer Research Institute](https://www.cancerresearch.org/stories/supporters/mark-fischbach-markiplier) in 2013. In the video he says, "the last words that he said to me were I love you so much, and I held my dad's hand as he died." ([Draw My Life, May 4, 2013](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6Sl-1X58ObY&t=462s))

Nine years later Anthony Padilla asked how his father had shaped his sense of right and wrong. "My dad was very much the kind of guy that never did anything half-ass," Fischbach said. "Always do the right thing, admit if you're wrong, own your mistakes." He said his father never sat him down to teach any of it; he watched him live that way. When Padilla said it sounded like a self-description, he answered that he would "like to think I embody that." ([Anthony Padilla, May 20, 2022](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pIF9p2aZdeo&t=553s))

In 2026 he reached for his father's phrase to describe what his audience can count on: "I'm not going to half-ass anything and I'm going to try it to the very end. If it fails, it fails and I'll crash and burn with it." ([Lemonade Stand](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cB4dtw8xwMI&t=5253s))

These are a son's words about a dead parent, drawn out by a leading question, and "half-ass" is a common phrase. They show the standard he claims and where he says he got it. They do not show that his father's death produced it.

The same 2022 interview has him describing what he did with anger. He agreed that he had been a very angry teenager and said: "That was one of the first things that I really started to try to take control of myself, is like my anger, because I really did not like where that led me." In the 2013 video, describing a layoff, he says it "made me more mad but I didn't yell at my boss, professionalism and all that." In both tellings anger is something he judged and set out to manage. No one who knew him then is on record.

The channel's origin story uses the same vocabulary. In 2013 he described a stretch around 2012 in which, by his telling, he lost a job and a relationship and ended up in a hospital recovering from surgery: "it seemed like everything in my life was completely out of my control and I had no choice in the matter, so finally I decided that enough was enough and I was going to do what I wanted." Asked in 2022 what drove him to start, he said, "Just complete lack of control in my life."

His later tellings are messier, and more believable for it. He told Slate in 2026 that he picked horror games because "people wanted to see it. I actually didn't really like playing horror games. I was scared." On NBC's TODAY he named the first hook plainly: "I was completely obsessed with this new thing I'd never experienced, having people pay any sort of attention to me. I was the quiet kid in school." ([TODAY, November 19, 2019](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=X7D5v1tGG1c&t=58s)) So the beginning was trial and error, audience demand and the pleasure of being noticed.

## The streak and the voice

In a late-night vlog in November 2014 he described what he thought he owed his viewers. "I recognize that I have this weird responsibility to you guys," he said, describing a position in which "so many people are looking to me for guidance and I have an obligation to fulfill that." ([An Important Message, November 23, 2014](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YG-vC3i0_Dw&t=125s))

Ten months later his housemate and collaborator Daniel Kyre died after a suicide attempt ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markiplier)). Fischbach opened the video announcing the death by apologizing for his silence, "considering I haven't missed a day's uploads since I started the YouTube channel." What he chose to praise in his friend was a standard. Kyre "inspired us to expect better from ourselves," he said, and with the small details of editing "he always took the time to make sure they were perfect." The loss, he said, "just means that we have to try harder when we come back from this." ([Lost a Friend, September 21, 2015](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J_cxoZLPyR0))

He was away about two weeks, by his count. In the video marking his return he said, "I wanted to make sure that I confronted it directly," and that he would see a therapist. He also reported what the break had felt like: "it's been eating away at the back of my mind, just like, you should really probably be recording something right now." And he named a fear he called irrational: "if I don't upload videos somehow I think that everything's going to come collapsing down." ([Markiplier Returns, October 5, 2015](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iWQ34ixgx-k&t=58s))

A daily schedule was also good business for a gaming channel, and the fear he names is of being forgotten, so part of this is economics. In the same video he calls those two weeks "very stressful" and also "very beautiful," a quick turn toward the good that fits Type 7. What neither business nor optimism explains is the voice he describes. Something in him scored a break taken for a death as time he should have been working.

## The night he felt lost

On March 24, 2017, with his channel close to 17 million subscribers, he posted a tearful late-night vlog titled "I Feel Lost." It is the strongest evidence for the other reading of him, so it gets its own section.

The problem, as he states it, is not failure. "I'm actively turning down movies and TV roles and book deals," he says, and then: "I feel like I don't deserve it, you know, in a way, like I haven't earned it yet." The trouble is choice: "there's so many things that I could focus my time on that you end up focusing on nothing and you end up just kind of drifting." "I'm not afraid of failure," he says. "I'm afraid of disappointing you guys." His answer to the crisis is a resolution: "I have to take the right steps and I have to make the right choices and I have to make a conscious effort every day to be better." ([I Feel Lost, March 24, 2017](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fd-VFBOOL5c&t=205s))

The next morning he posted a second part. He said, "I just can't wait to fail," and a few sentences later, "I love failing because it just gives me an idea of how much farther I need to go." He closed on a rule for his life: "I'm never going to stop moving, not until the day I die." ([I Feel Lost pt. 2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QiJCBFArQcg&t=155s))

Paralysis among too many options, a swing from tears to appetite within hours, and a life described as motion: this is what a Seven sounds like when the horizon runs out. A One usually knows what ought to be done and suffers over not doing it. On this night Fischbach did not know. Two details pull the other way. He was refusing rewards he considered unearned, and when he recast failure overnight he recast it as a measuring stick.

## Three years in one room

_Iron Lung_ started the way a Seven starts things. David Szymanski, who made the game, said the project began with "him DMing me on Twitter out of the blue." ([Quad Damage Podcast, September 2, 2025](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MtKke2Pli9E&t=2985s))

What he did next is the part a Seven does not do. He told YouTube's blog, "our first rule was staying in the submarine. It's a restrictive box, but creativity lives in those restrictions." Asked what a studio would have tried to fix, he named the slow opening and defended it: "The point is the monotony, the loneliness, the boredom." ([YouTube Official Blog, February 23, 2026](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/markiplier-iron-lung-interview/)) That interview was published by the platform his career is built on, so its framing is friendly.

By his account he paid for it himself to keep the final say. On Lemonade Stand he said, "I'm a bit of a control freak in that regard and I don't like being beholden to anybody else's opinion besides the creative team." He also said that "no one was really bashing down my door," so the independence was partly forced. By June 2026 he had told IndieWire, as relayed by Gizmodo, that he "did too much myself" and would give up some control on a next feature ([Gizmodo, June 29, 2026](https://gizmodo.com/iron-lung-creator-markiplier-on-how-his-place-in-hollywood-has-evolved-2000779184)).

The price, by his account: "it was 16-hour days for about 3 years." He said, "I lost time with some people that were very important to me that I will never get back again. And I had to postpone even my wedding to work on these things." ([Lemonade Stand](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cB4dtw8xwMI&t=4915s)) Of the years spent making it, he told Slate, "I already hated myself more and this movie more than anyone" else could. On the night he judged it finished he drove home, told Nelson, "I think it's done," and then: "I break down for 30 minutes straight. I cried. Just curled up in a ball and cried. Not out of sadness." He called it "this immense pressure falling off." ([his channel, May 17, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U5Xpd8ja9bk&t=270s))

All of that is self-report, told after the film had succeeded, when hardship reads as heroism. No account from Nelson was found.

Then the result came in, and he ranked it below the making. On a stream four days after the opening he told fans, "the win condition isn't being number one," and, "The win condition is that this actually came out at all." Minutes later: "this isn't the best I can do." ([Iron Lung Weekend Results, February 3, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eFaihZ0f58w&t=600s)) The stance was not invented after the hit. On the eve of release he told IGN, "not everything's about making a profit" ([IGN, January 29, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1eNORrwTFBo)), and he says he had decided before shooting that "if I didn't make my money back I'd be okay with that."

Szymanski is the one outside witness to the work itself. He said Fischbach had spent so long building out the game's world that it was "definitely as much his as it is mine now. Probably even a little more his." Fischbach says Szymanski shares in the film's profits, and Szymanski called himself "the least unbiased source you could hear from."

## The judge he answers to

Type 1 is the read here because of where he places the judge. Speaking to an audience of creators in June 2026 he said, "no one's going to judge me harsher than I judge myself. So if I can do that and overcome my own doubt then nothing else would really matter to me." He called that his "guiding star." ([Colin and Samir, June 11, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W5Irjxu1FVI&t=565s))

He had said it four years earlier in different words. "Yes, I have a standard I hold myself to, and yes, my self-worth is pretty much nailed to my ability to put my ideas into the world," he told Padilla, whose question had supplied the frame, and of his finished work: "it's also never good enough." On Lemonade Stand: "I won't release a project until I'm happy with it and until I know that it was the best I could do at the time, and I will be very hard on myself if I know that it could be better and I have not put in all the effort that I possibly could."

Strangers do not reach this judge. Of online praise he told Slate, "Well, you don't know me, so it doesn't matter." A critic he knew would be different: "it would devastate me," he said, and his response would be, "I got to be better so I can make them proud of me again." So he is not indifferent to approval. The circle whose approval counts is small, and its verdict arrives in the language of being better.

Here is the distinction this profile rests on. A Seven and a One can both work hard, and both can put a bright face on pain. The difference is what the limit is for. A Seven treats rules, monotony and long discomfort as things to get out of, and reframes pain in order to leave it. Fischbach keeps choosing the limit and staying in it. He kept a daily streak for more than three years by his account, deleted a channel because he had said he would, and held a film to one room. When he reframes pain, it is to justify staying. Describing a biopsy he was awake for, he told Padilla, "this pain is necessary, this pain is actually essential to me getting better." And finishing brings him release more than satisfaction, followed within days by the judgment that it was not his best.

This rests on his own descriptions plus four things that happened in public: the streak, the deletion, the film and the giving. That is why the confidence is medium.

## Where Type 7 and Type 3 still fit

The Seven reading explains things the One reading does not. By his own account he starts on impulse, jumps between ideas, plans lightly and is impatient. The 2017 vlog is a Seven's dilemma. And his own description of happiness is about motion: "I want a direction to climb," he told fans in 2026, because what makes him happy is "so long as I'm moving in a direction." On Lemonade Stand he put it as, "if I'm not getting better, then I'm not happy." The record cannot fully separate "better" from "next." If they are the same hunger, the Seven reading is stronger than this profile allows. He attributes the idea-jumping to ADHD, which describes attention and says nothing about motive either way.

[Type 3](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-3), a motive built on success and image, is closer than it first looks: his worth is tied to what he produces. He told Lemonade Stand that he made a feature because his online work lacked prestige: "to win and to gain some respect, I knew that I had to do a project." And attention, he has said, was the first hook.

Where Type 3 strains is the record of what he gives up. He told Padilla in 2022, "people don't understand just how much money falls out of the YouTube bucket. Like, it's unfair." He said, "I've wrestled with this a lot over the years," and that his answer is, "I donate to charity." Of offers to put his name on products he said, "I say no thank you." The Cancer Research Institute says he donated $500,000 in proceeds from a 2018 calendar, and the proceeds of the OnlyFans account he opened in 2022 were to be split between Cincinnati Children's Hospital and the World Food Programme ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markiplier)). No total for his giving has been verified, and charity also builds a creator's standing. But add the deleted channel, a film he says he entered prepared to lose money on, and his claim that the verdict that counts is his own, and a motive built on winning explains less than it should.

One thing fits neither. He cries easily on camera, and what sets it off is being backed. Asked on Slate about tearing up after the opening weekend, he said, "it was the fact that people believed in me." That reads more like a need for support and belonging, the territory of Type 2 or Type 6, than like a One's standard. This profile leaves it unexplained.

## What the record cannot show

Nearly everything above is Fischbach describing himself, usually to friendly interviewers and mostly after a success. The outside voices are few and warm. Besides Szymanski there is Nestor, who told him on camera, in a show they made together, that "seeing how much you're willing to give up is extremely inspiring." The clip survives only as an undated fan re-upload ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yEOda4EI8lA)).

No one who has disagreed with him at work is on record. The nearest thing is a dispute this profile could not check at the source. According to a January 2, 2025 report by [Deltia's Gaming](https://web.archive.org/web/2025/https://deltiasgaming.com/the-entire-controversy-surrounding-markipliers-official-subreddit-explained/), he removed several moderators of his official subreddit on December 25, 2024, and a former head moderator wrote that he was disappointed "that you didn't care enough to reach out." The original posts could not be retrieved, so the episode is noted here and not interpreted.

Two of his own claims have no outside confirmation. He says he doubled the film crew's pay as a bonus once costs were repaid, and that he bought majority control of his clothing company to fix its quality and sold almost nothing for a year and a half while he rebuilt it (both on Lemonade Stand).

The real test is ahead. If his next film is made with a studio's money and someone else's final say, and he is at ease with it, the control described here was about circumstance. If he keeps the final cut and the long hours, the pattern holds.

When he finally let the film go, the verdict he gave it was not praise. "Okay, I don't hate it anymore. Go," he recalled on Slate. His mother, he told YouTube's blog, saw it with his cousin from Korea, loved it, and had one note: "Don't do that again. No sequels." He had already told Slate what came next: "Can't wait for the next one."
