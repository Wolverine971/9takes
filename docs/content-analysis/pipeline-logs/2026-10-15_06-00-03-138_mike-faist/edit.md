In May 2021, seven months before Steven Spielberg's _West Side Story_ reached theaters, Mike Faist sat down in Park Slope with [The New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2021/05/27/arts/television/mike-faist-panic-west-side-story.html) and tried to describe what making it had felt like. He called it "transcendental: either I didn't feel like I was myself, or I was the most authentic version of myself. I can't really tell which one."

The paper predicted the film would put him "on Hollywood's speed dial." He was turning down offers anyway, and selling his Brooklyn apartment to head back to Ohio, where his father had fallen ill.

"Having gone from having no money, wanting to just be a working actor — I don't want to just be a working actor anymore," he said. "I just don't want to follow the trajectory of what the industry wants me to do." Asked what that trajectory was, he said, "Put on a cape and wear a mask."

Faist, born in 1992 and raised in Gahanna, Ohio, originated Connor Murphy in _Dear Evan Hansen_ and played Riff, leader of the Jets, for Spielberg, earning a BAFTA nomination. He was Art Donaldson in _Challengers_ (2024), opposite [Zendaya](/personality-analysis/zendaya) and Josh O'Connor, and plays Charles Trask, a quiet farmer who is dangerous when provoked, in Netflix's _East of Eden_, from John Steinbeck's novel. After shooting it, around 2024, he worked on nothing new for about two years. The series premiered on October 1, 2026.

Since at least 2017 he has said that being himself in public is harder than being someone else. Explaining why he dislikes interviews to his friend Jonathan Anderson, the designer who costumed _Challengers_, he put it this way in a conversation [Interview](https://www.interviewmagazine.com/film/a-reluctant-interview-between-mike-faist-and-jonathan-anderson) published on October 6, 2026:

> "I actually feel a lot more comfortable being a character than I do being myself in the public eye. I feel a lot braver, quite frankly, to be someone else."

This analysis asks what he is guarding, and what it has cost him.

## What is Mike Faist's personality type?

Mike Faist's personality type is Enneagram Type 4, the Individualist, read at medium confidence. Fours sit in the heart triad, whose core emotion is shame. Since 2017 he has guarded his plain self from public view, and he has turned down work that didn't feel like his own. Type 6 and Type 9 remain close alternatives.

A [Type 4](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-4) wants to be himself and be seen that way, and fears that something essential in him is missing or will be found wanting. That fear of being found deficient is the shame. Faist shows both halves: embarrassment at being himself in front of people, and a rule for choosing work, repeated since 2021, that it has to come from "a place of passion and love."

The feeling he reports most often is anxiety, which points to [Type 6](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6), so the case turns on what the anxiety is about: being found wanting and exposed, or safety and trust. His best experiences at work are a kind of self-forgetting, which points to [Type 9](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-9).

## "I keep fooling everyone"

As a small child Faist watched _Singin' in the Rain_ on repeat. "I'm going to be Gene Kelly one day," he told his mother, Julie, [Another Man](https://www.anothermag.com/another-man/15568/mike-faist-challengers-the-bikeriders-interview-profile) reported in 2024.

He graduated from high school a year early, and at 17 his father drove him to New York. He dropped out of the American Musical and Dramatic Academy after two semesters and at times lived on food stamps. Another Man reports that he ran into the actor Ben Platt while delivering for Postmates and felt "so ashamed that he pretended he was doing something else."

Then fear froze him. After quitting school, he said at a [BUILD Series](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1SyXL1F43Qg) interview in March 2017, he "went maybe six, eight months without auditioning for anything 'cause I felt too incapable." What ended it was another person: "a friend of mine at the time dragged me kicking and screaming to an audition," he told [Interview](https://www.interviewmagazine.com/culture/discovery-mike-faist).

Self-doubt runs through his interviews, often as a joke. Asked at BUILD how he kept landing musicals when he did not consider himself a strong singer, he said, "You know, I keep fooling everyone. I really have no idea, it works out." Of the _West Side Story_ dance call, he told [Screen International](https://www.screendaily.com/features/do-i-have-to-dance-mike-faist-on-moving-from-stage-to-screen-for-west-side-story/5168275.article) in 2022, "I'm not an ensemble dancer," then: "I'm not good enough. I had no business being in that room, quite frankly. These were some of the best dancers in the world." In October 2026, remembering the 17-year-old who was "really excited to get better," he said on [PBS's American Masters](https://www.pbs.org/wnet/americanmasters/podcast/mike-faist-interview/), "I still feel that way. Maybe I'll be good one day. That'd be cool."

A joke, a dancer's comparison and a hope. None is a confession, but each marks him down.

## "Who I actually am"

Faist made his Broadway debut at 20, in the original cast of _Newsies_ in 2012. In 2015 he joined _Dear Evan Hansen_ at Washington's Arena Stage as Connor Murphy, the troubled teenager whose suicide sets the plot moving, and stayed with it to Broadway, where it opened in December 2016. The role earned him a 2017 Tony nomination. Ben Platt played the title character, Evan Hansen.

What he guarded hardest was the gap between Connor and himself. He rarely did the stage-door autograph line, he told [MTV News](https://web.archive.org/web/2017/http://www.mtv.com/news/3002027/mike-faist-dear-evan-hansen-interview/) in April 2017, signing only "maybe twice a week" for repeat customers. Fans "feel like they know us, in a way," he said, and "I don't want to take that away from them, by having them meet who I actually am."

> "I just would hate for that illusion to be broken by having interaction with me, that, maybe, [fans] were expecting something else."

That spring he described Evan and Connor to Interview, and the pronoun slid from "they" to "we": "they don't want people to see them because if we allow ourselves to be seen, actually seen, and allow [ourselves] to be vulnerable, then we lose the game." His next sentence made the "we" generational: "Teens don't want to let people know how they are actually feeling." It was not a confession, but he would later say much the same of himself.

He left in the summer of 2018, [The Washington Post](https://www.stripes.com/living/entertainment/movies/2021-12-13/faist-actor-west-side-story-profile-entertainment-3955668.html) reported, and kept moving: an audition tape for Riff, then Steven Levenson's off-Broadway play _Days of Rage_. Asked by the Times in 2021 why he was not in the _Dear Evan Hansen_ film, he said, "I feel like I couldn't do it." Eight shows a week, he explained, "very much turns into relying on your technique and the job of it." He did not say whether a film role was offered.

## "Like being used as a vessel"

Faist went into _West Side Story_ fearing Spielberg would be "a dictator," he told [Gold Derby](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=R1377wu21GE) in December 2021, and the shoot "Hollywood overproduced," with the actor reduced to a prop. Instead, he told [Esquire](https://www.esquire.com/uk/culture/film/a61081999/mike-faist-the-bikeriders-interview/) in 2024, "Steven never made me feel I needed to ask permission to do anything."

As Riff he made the Jets a gang off camera too. "I wanted to actually create a family, a brotherhood, a tribe," he said, and had each Jet pick an activity the whole gang had to do. On camera, he had the unscripted idea of Riff pressing his temple to the barrel of a bartender's gun. "What Mike came up with in that moment," the screenwriter Tony Kushner told the Post, "is the whole character in this one little gesture."

Kushner's favorite memory of the shoot was quieter. After the Jets splashed paint over a mural of the Puerto Rican flag, crew members set about scrubbing it off. "One of the people washing it was Mike," he said. "And it really wasn't in any way done for display. It was something that he needed to do because he felt terribly about what his character had just done, and he needed to do something to make himself feel okay about it." The motive is Kushner's reading. The scrubbing is something he watched.

Faist's own description of that shoot is the most revealing thing in his record. "It felt like: I'm not me right now, I'm being used to tell the story in the best way possible," he told Another Man in 2024.

> "It was like being used as a vessel, and that was the best feeling ever."

In 2021 he could not decide whether that state was losing himself or finding himself. That question is where the Four and Nine readings part, and he has never settled it.

## Saying no at the top

After the film came out, Kushner told [GQ](https://www.gq.com/story/mike-faist-gq-hype) in 2026, friends making movies called him to ask whether Faist would take this part or that part. "And I think in both cases he turned them down. And he was very nice about it, but the stuff that they were asking just didn't feel right to him at the moment, and it had nothing to do with size of the role or anything." Speaking of Faist's choices in general, not any one project, Kushner added a caution: "That's hard with actors, because you can also become an actor who says no to a lot." Then: "But I'm intrigued by what he's decided to do and what he has decided not to do and what he's fought hard to get out of."

Circumstance pressed too. After the pandemic shutdown he spent much of two years driving the country with his dog, Austin, and writing a screenplay. His father, Kurt, had fallen ill with pulmonary fibrosis, and in 2021 Faist moved home to help care for him, buying a house in Columbus that he renovated, partly with his father's help.

He kept working on his own terms: _Challengers_ with Luca Guadagnino, then _The Bikeriders_, as the photographer Danny Lyon, alongside [Austin Butler](/personality-analysis/austin-butler) and [Tom Hardy](/personality-analysis/tom-hardy). He took that one "because all I had to do was just be the observer," he said on PBS. To Another Man he described the work he wants:

> "Like, it's coming from me. Like not necessarily for love, but from love. It may not be good [laughs] but it's coming from that place of 'I'm free, I'm liberated, I'm the most authentic I can be.'"

In May 2023, with his father still ill, he opened in London's West End as Jack Twist in a stage _Brokeback Mountain_, two years after saying eight shows a week had worn _Dear Evan Hansen_ down to technique. "We're always nervous," he told the [Associated Press](https://www.clickondetroit.com/entertainment/2023/05/22/brokeback-mountain-brings-two-rising-acting-stars-to-the-london-stage/).

His father died on December 12, 2023, and his grandfather weeks later. "All the men have passed," he told GQ. _Challengers_ opened in April 2024. He dreaded the press tour, Another Man reported, but did it, and walked the Met Gala carpet in a custom look from Anderson.

Then came _East of Eden_. Zoe Kazan, its writer and showrunner, told GQ they met twice before he agreed: "I don't need you to convince me. I just need to know that this is right for me." His own accounts credit other people: "honestly Zoe and Florence," he told [Cultured](https://culturedmag.com/article/2026/09/21/film-mike-faist-east-of-eden-interview/) in September 2026, meaning Kazan and Florence Pugh, a producer on the series. Kazan had "a really specific point of view," he said on PBS, and a clear leader "helps any sort of anxieties because it's like, well, someone's steering the ship." The part is against type, GQ notes, and after the first footage came in, Kazan says, Netflix offered to "pay for another episode," and Kazan wrote him into a later one.

## Two years out of view

After the _East of Eden_ shoot he worked on no new production until August 2026, and some of that was not his call. _Sgt. Rock_, a DC film he was reportedly attached to with Guadagnino, fell apart "more or less in prep," its screenwriter Justin Kuritzkes told GQ. A DC film sits oddly with 2021's "cape" line, though Kuritzkes calls it a superhero movie "about everyday heroes, humans, normal people." In June 2025 Faist was announced opposite Michelle Williams in an off-Broadway [_Anna Christie_](https://www.russh.com/anna-christie-mike-faist-michelle-williams). By August 26 he had [withdrawn](https://www.newyorktheatreguide.com/theatre-news/news/tom-sturridge-brian-darcy-james-join-michelle-williams-in-anna-christie-off-broadway). As of October 2026, no reason had been made public.

His own reasons point several ways. In GQ he described "needing to recalibrate" amid "so much noise" and said, "I don't think that I have that kind of stamina." Asked whether promotion was the problem, he said, "It's the public gaze": before fame, "you could kind of be whoever you wanted to be." And some of it was outside him: "There's things that pop up and for whatever reason, they don't happen because of the industry and financing."

To Cultured, his first answer was, "You know, there really isn't a lot of thought." Then he spoke of needing to "hear myself think, and feel what is right for me," and of "trying to tune everything out." "The truth is that Challengers was a lot. It was very overwhelming," he said. And: "I don't really know how much control I actually have over any of this."

In the same interview, a year after leaving _Anna Christie_, he said, "I would love to go back to the theater at some point," and "I remain open and optimistic that I'll be able to find my way back." By then GQ had found him about to start rehearsals for his first production since _East of Eden_. He would not say what it was.

Of these episodes, only one is a clear trade: in 2021, at peak demand, he turned down parts that didn't feel right and set aside the "working actor" life he had chased with no money. The rest is mixed. The Brooklyn sale was a move home to a sick father. The quiet years followed two deaths, an overwhelming summer and a collapsed film, and his reasons run from stamina to fame to financing. Circumstance explains part of every episode: the pandemic, his father's illness, a studio film collapsing. And he has never said why he left _Anna Christie_.

## Why not Type 6?

The best argument against the Four reading is the feeling Faist reports more than any other. He told BAFTA in March 2022:

> "I tend to get anxious or have a bit of anxiety before every project, no matter what it is."

Each time, he went on, he asks himself "what did I sign myself up for? I can't do this. Why did they hire me?"

Anxiety is the Six's signature emotion, and that run of questions is a Six's worst-case worry about a commitment already made. He manages it the way a Six does: research, which he told Another Man is his way "to feel more secure"; a showrunner "steering the ship"; and, he told [BBC Radio 1](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gMOhYHCmN9w) in October 2026, people "a lot smarter than you." He builds groups and stays loyal to them: the Jets, the family he moved home to help, a high-school friend he lived with in 2024, and the [Ohio Artists Gathering](https://www.columbusunderground.com/gahanna-native-and-tony-nominee-mike-faist-brings-inaugural-ohio-artists-gathering-to-short-north-stage-rs1/), a festival he organized in Columbus in August 2018.

Belonging runs through his odder choices too. He is adopted, and at 17 he met his birth mother's family, "a family of pilots," he told Interview in 2017. After a flight with one of them he thought, "That's how we can bond!" and got his pilot's license. His first paid job came from a friend's push.

In 2026 he described fear as something he chases. "The other tiny voice inside of you goes, Oh, don't do that. Don't jump into the volcano. Stay up here where it's safe," he told Cultured. "But, that's the thrill of what we get to do." A counterphobic Six moves toward what frightens him, though he gave versions of that answer all through one press cycle.

Even the _East of Eden_ yes splits: Kazan remembers a test of fit, while he credits her clear lead. It does not separate the types.

So why does the Four reading still lead? Not because of the self-doubt: "Why did they hire me?" and "I'm not good enough" are doubts about his craft, and a Six voices them as readily as a Four. Not because of his vocabulary either. Actors on press tours talk about authenticity and hiding inside characters all the time; in the same Interview conversation, Anderson said of himself, "I do feel there is me on and there's me off."

It leads on what he guards. From 2017 to 2026 the fear that recurs is of his plain self being seen: fans kept from meeting "who I actually am," a delivery job hidden from Ben Platt out of shame, "I'm embarrassed to do this interview." Asked about the grind of promotion, he named not safety but the public gaze, and the lost freedom to be "whoever you wanted to be." A Six's anxiety more often fixes on safety, backing, or whether people can be trusted. Apart from the Spielberg worry, which dissolved on contact, Faist almost never voices suspicion of particular people.

It also leads on what he gave up. A security-first reading predicts that an actor who once had no money takes the safe offers after a hit. In 2021 Faist named that goal, "wanting to just be a working actor," and set it aside, and Kushner independently confirms the refusals had "nothing to do with size of the role." This suggests the Four's trade: security given up to keep the work his own.

The Six case still explains some things better: the anxiety before every job, the reliance on trusted leaders, the instinct to build a community, the yes to _East of Eden_. The record also lacks the scene that would separate them most clearly, Faist in open conflict or let down by a collaborator. A Six would look for allies or a trusted authority to settle it, and grow wary of the person who failed him. A Four would hold to his own sense of what is true, even at a cost to his standing. Until that account exists, the Four reading holds at medium confidence.

## The quiet, and the case for Type 9

A Nine reading starts from the vessel, a self-forgetting he calls the best feeling of his working life. He talks about "noise" and lacking "stamina," wanted to "allow things to be quiet," and after the _Challengers_ shoot planned a road trip to "allow myself to be bored," Esquire reported, until Jeff Nichols called. People describe easy company. His co-star on the 2021 Amazon series _Panic_, Jessica Sula, told the Times he is "so fabulously ridiculous and wonderful." Anderson told him, "You're just a modest person, Mike." The record holds almost no conflict.

What tilts the read toward the Four is how he frames the quiet. A Nine's retreat usually protects peace. Faist's protects a self: quiet lets him "hear myself think," and what fame took was the chance to be "whoever you wanted to be." But a Nine tired of noise could say the same. The Nine is a close alternative, and the two readings are not yet separated.

A [Type 5](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-5) reading, built on his observer's role in _The Bikeriders_, fits poorly with a man who turned the Jets into a tribe and learned to fly partly to bond with newfound relatives.

## What the type doesn't explain

Some of what is on record sits awkwardly with any Four reading, and it belongs in the portrait.

He wants to be useful. "I would like to ultimately be of service and of use; that's when I feel at my best," he told the Times in 2021. "So acting is just constantly giving," he said on PBS in 2026.

Florence Pugh saw that from across a scene. For a later _East of Eden_ moment with only a few lines, which an assistant director would usually read, "he insisted on coming back early to be in the room and reading with me," she told [TV Insider](https://www.tvinsider.com/1294095/east-of-eden-cathy-charles-florence-pugh-mike-faist-interview/). "And what that created was a whole relationship." Kushner's mural story belongs here too.

He is funny. At the _Challengers_ press conference he announced that he and O'Connor "hate each other." Asked by GQ about playing Mary Todd in _Oh, Mary!_, he said, "I would rock Mary Todd."

And his account of the job won't hold still. In 2021, laughing off being merely a working actor, he told the Times, "I don't care enough," and moments later, "I can't tell if I hate acting or if I love it too much." To Another Man in 2024: "I fall in and out of love with it on a pretty regular basis." To Cultured in 2026: "I think, unfortunately, I'm doomed to do this." And to PBS: "I don't know why I keep going, 'cause I'm an addict."

## "Just watch my movies"

One pattern fits the Four reading closely: he shows himself most fully as someone else. For a Four, the wish to be seen and the fear of being found wanting pull against each other, and a character can be the safest place to be seen.

During _Dear Evan Hansen_ in 2017 he kept his distance from the stage door. He is not invisible: Another Man reported he was reticent about a Loewe campaign until Anderson "wasn't going to take no for an answer," and he did it. But in August 2026 he told GQ, "it's a lot easier for me to put on a character and be in front of the world than be me," and called a walk with a reporter "a big step."

Kazan, who told GQ she is "very careful not to push on" his privacy, reached for an animal: "There's something about Mike that reads to me like a hermit crab or something. There's this shell on the outside and you can't tell what's inside."

"I'm always saying," Faist told GQ, "if you want to get to know me, just watch my movies."

Asked by PBS what keeps him going, he said he loves the work and keeps a loose grip on it.

> "I love this thing very, very much, but I don't want to hold it so tightly because I think if you hold it too tightly, maybe it'll die."
