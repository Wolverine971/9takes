In June 2022, the Guardian's Simon Hattenstone sat down with Paapa Essiedu in a café and opened with a compliment: you're a very good actor.

"Do you really think so?" Essiedu asked.

Hattenstone assumed he was fishing. He wasn't. He had started a new job the day before, he explained, and "on the day before the first day on every job, I have a nervous breakdown, thinking: this is the one where people will find me out, see that what's underneath the car bonnet doesn't work."

Hattenstone asked if he meant it. "Genuine!" he said, fiercely.

"Nervous breakdown" is his everyday phrase for the dread, not a diagnosis. "I need a lot of support from the people who are close to me to drag me into the car to go to work on that first day," he told [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/culture/2022/jun/11/paapa-essiedu-grief-boris-johnson-i-may-destroy-you-michaela-coel).

In January 2014, fresh out of drama school, Essiedu was understudying Edmund in Sam Mendes's _King Lear_ at the National Theatre. Partway through a preview, Sam Troughton, who played Edmund, lost his voice, and Essiedu went on for the rest of the show. The critic [Mark Lawson](https://www.theguardian.com/stage/2014/jan/27/king-lear-national-theatre-understudy) was in the audience, and he admits he checked Essiedu's knees for knocking.

> "There was, though, neither a tremble in limb or voice."

Born in London in 1990, Essiedu became the Royal Shakespeare Company's first Black Hamlet in 2016, broke through on screen as Kwame in Michaela Coel's _I May Destroy You_ in 2020, and won an [Olivier Award](https://www.nyt.org.uk/news/new-news-article-375) in April 2026 for _All My Sons_. In April 2025, HBO cast him as Severus Snape in its _Harry Potter_ series, and the casting brought him death threats.

Put the café and the King Lear night side by side and a pattern shows. His fear arrives early, before anything has gone wrong. When something really does go wrong, he is the steady one. And when his friend Paloma Faith asked him in 2025 whether he was preoccupied with being good, his answer ended on safety.

## What is Paapa Essiedu's personality type?

Paapa Essiedu's personality type is Enneagram Type 6, the Loyalist, read at medium confidence. Sixes sit in the head triad, whose core emotion is fear. His fear runs ahead of events: for years he has described dreading being found out before each new job and leaning on people close to him to get there. Then he goes on anyway.

In a [Type 6](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6), the alarm goes off before the danger arrives, and the answer is backup. Some Sixes back away from what scares them. Others go straight at it, the style called counterphobic. Essiedu does both, in sequence. What would count against the read is a record of him getting through the run-up alone, or tracing his self-criticism to his own standard rather than to exposure.

The strongest rival is [Type 1](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-1), a gut-triad type whose anger is held down by a strict inner critic, because Essiedu is diligent, hard on himself and openly principled. [Type 3](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-3) needs testing too, since so much of his fear is about how he is seen. This is a read of his public record from 2014 to 2026, at medium confidence.

## "This is the one where people will find me out"

He described the same doubt, in nearly the same shape, in 2020, 2022 and 2024.

On a 2020 [livestream](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jAcEe_qOVAE) hosted by his friend Calum Finlay, a viewer asked whether he had ever struggled with stage fright.

> "I'm always certain I'm gonna be terrible. It's gonna be the end of my career. I'm always like, why did I take this job? It was going so well. I've tricked so many people."

He added that a little humility "pushes you into a position where you want to get better," a thought a One could have had.

In 2024 he told [the Observer](https://www.theguardian.com/culture/article/2024/jul/28/paapa-essiedu-is-this-part-harder-than-hamlet-yeah-its-different-gravy-mate-death-of-england-delroy-soho-theatre): "Every time I get any job, I go: 'Jesus, what? Really? Me?'"

In the café, he went further.

> "I read, 'What are you doing here?' on everybody's face."

He knows he is doing it. "You know that thing when you project what your brain wants you to see on somebody who is probably just having a cup of tea?" Then, after a stop: "Maybe they are thinking it."

The verdict he fears belongs to other people, and he supplies it himself. He also reads "Oh my God I've made a huge mistake in inviting you to be in this" in their body language. The fear is of losing his place among people who chose him.

There are fair objections. First-night nerves are close to universal among actors, and British interviews reward self-deprecation. But Hattenstone pressed and he held to it, and the one glimpse from off stage fits. On the livestream, Finlay recalled being begged not to come and see _Pass Over_, Essiedu's 2020 play at London's Kiln Theatre, before press night.

Is it easing? In August 2023 he told [The Times](https://www.thetimes.co.uk/article/paapa-essiedu-interview-the-effect-lyttelton-national-theatre-london-92vdn0nxb) that "right now I'm thinking less about what people might think about me." Dominic Maxwell of The Times remembered "a kind of Tiggerish energy, a physical restlessness" when they met in 2016. By 2023, Maxwell wrote, "he is all containment." He was in weekly therapy in 2022, and in 2026 he told [The Sunday Times](https://www.thetimes.com/culture/tv-radio/article/paapa-essiedu-harry-potter-snape-interview-3hqkzzmlk): "If I didn't, I'd be incredibly anxious and unhappy." The alarm may be quieter, with support kept in place, which still fits a Six. The dread gone without that support would not.

## "If it all gets taken away"

A week after the café, he and Hattenstone caught up again. Essiedu had been thinking about why he is so dismissive of his own success. Hattenstone lists the reasons he gave: class, insecurity, impostor syndrome, self-preservation. On the last, he said:

> "If it all gets taken away, you don't feel that loss so keenly."

On a trip shortly before that interview, he had told a stranger he'd been in "just a few little things," then gushed over her job in mezcal. "And I thought: what is wrong with me!" He named the habit: "Perennially living in the expectation that things are going to fuck up is just mad."

On taking time off, he told [Mr Porter](https://www.mrporter.com/en-gb/journal/fashion/interview-paapa-essiedu-25088214) in 2024: "The fear of things being taken away from you at any moment really kicks in and you think you need to do something again before it's too late." Then: "But you need to respect your body, respect your talent, you can take time off and then the other thing will come."

He has reasons to expect loss. His father, Tony, a lawyer, went back to Ghana when Essiedu was very young and died when he was 14. His mother, Selina, who taught fashion and design at adult-education colleges, raised him alone in Walthamstow, east London.

She had cancer for seven years and died in his first year at the Guildhall School of Music and Drama, when he was 20.

He went back to drama school the next day, "an emergency act of self-preservation in order to stop myself thinking or feeling or engaging." He locked their belongings in the loft of the family home and walked away "for about 10 years."

This is where the type argument has to be careful. Losing both parents by 20, on little money, would teach anyone to brace for loss, and public evidence can't separate temperament from that history. Class is on his own list, and in 2024 he traced the impostor feeling back to how he was brought up. What the record does show is how he handles the bracing. He calls it "mad," and he keeps walking into the jobs he dreads.

## The leap and the net

Acting itself was a leap. He had a place to study medicine at University College London and dropped out, Hattenstone wrote, "at the last minute, and much to everybody's surprise, including his own."

He still wanted his mother's blessing. "She knew how important her consent was to me," he said. She gave it with a condition:

> "Look, if you're going to do this thing, you've got to do it properly. You've got to treat it in the same way you would if you were a doctor, the same level of professionalism."

"It was just what I needed to hear," he said. That condition is a standard, and a One would hear it the same way. On [The Going Rogue Club podcast](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gskkRklT-9Y&t=745s) in 2024, he said his half-sister had only just told him that his mother used to ring her after each drama-school rejection, still hoping he would end up at medical school.

At Guildhall he co-founded a theatre company, Invertigo. Fear was part of it, he told Finlay in 2020:

> "I was really terrified about the feeling of having been in drama school and having a certain amount of stability within that structure"

Asked by [the Observer](https://www.theguardian.com/stage/2016/mar/13/paapa-essiedu-hamlet-royal-shakespeare-theatre-stratford-interview) in 2016 what an actor needs to survive, he said "genuine openness and vulnerability, a willingness to share yourself," then added: "for anyone to survive, you cannot live with your heart on your sleeve all the time because people will take advantage." He had reasons. During a Guildhall improvisation, a teacher playing a prison warden shouted a racial slur, and "I didn't know if she was talking to me or Michaela," he said in 2022. The school [apologised "unreservedly"](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2022/jun/10/top-drama-school-apologises-paapa-essiedu-michaela-coel-appalling-racism-guildhall). That history would make anyone guarded, so the wariness is weak type evidence.

Michaela Coel, his friend since Guildhall, told Hattenstone he approaches characters "with great sensitivity and thoughtfulness," "trying to discover things almost with fear and trembling." She called him "funny and very dependable," and said "he's always there in times of crisis."

The man who needs people close to him to get into the car on day one is the friend Coel counts on in a crisis. In Six terms, that is one system: loyalty needed and loyalty given. Dependability alone would fit a One, Two or Nine.

## Going on anyway

On the [Dish podcast](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hqph-vko_lY&t=2145s) in May 2026, he explained how he knew Edmund. Bored with his own two or three lines, he had been turning up to Troughton's rehearsals, "Like some kind of stalker," and taking notes. "I'm a nerd."

Inside, he told the Observer in 2016, it was "an out-of-body experience" with "internal banshees." Outside, Lawson saw no tremble. He had been "desperately trying to recall Edmund's lines." Edmund, he said, is "in too deep to say anything other than fuck it. That is where I was, too."

A [Guardian feature](https://www.theguardian.com/stage/2021/mar/29/teacher-kenneth-rea-the-outstanding-actor) in 2021 credited "grit and enthusiasm": he had "devotedly over-prepared." Nothing in his accounts ties that to dread. The preparation covered the lines and the rest was a leap, which a keen young actor or a One holding himself to a standard could also have made. The night is texture for the Six reading, not proof.

Hamlet is clearer. "It was the first time I was confronted by something where I thought, 'I don't know if I can do this. I don't know if I'm ready,'" he told The Times in 2023. He did it anyway. That is fear named first and then faced, the counterphobic move. The press made it harder. He was the RSC's first Black Hamlet, and he told [The Big Issue](https://www.bigissue.com/culture/papa-essiedu-its-hard-for-me-not-to-be-political-in-the-work-that-i-do/) in 2023: "There was almost more focus on that than the actual production and I found that so difficult."

## Snape, threats and a letter

After his casting, critics argued that his skin color should rule him out of playing a man written as white. Some went further.

"I've been told, 'Quit or I'll murder you,'" he told The Sunday Times in March 2026.

"I'd be lying if I said it doesn't affect me emotionally," he said. Then he turned it around.

> "But the abuse fuels me. And makes me more passionate about making this character my own, because I think of how I felt as a kid."

The kid is the point: "a kid like me can see themselves represented in that world."

As of March 2026, he had not reported the threats. "I don't think some 17-year-old boy being put in jail for two weeks for threatening to murder me would actually make me feel any better."

Weeks after his casting, he was among more than 1,500 people in film and TV who signed an open letter condemning the UK Supreme Court's ruling that "woman" and "sex" in the Equality Act refer only to biological sex, [The Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/tv-and-radio/2025/may/06/i-dont-have-the-power-jk-rowling-wont-sack-paapa-essiedu-from-harry-potter-tv-show-over-trans-rights). Many saw his signature as a rebuke to [J.K. Rowling](/personality-analysis/j.k.-rowling), The Sunday Times noted, and some of her fans called for him to be sacked. Rowling posted that she did not have the power to sack him "and I wouldn't exercise it if I did."

He signed without seeking advice, he told The Sunday Times, because trans artists "should be able to work without being intimidated." On the calls to sack him: "I got this job on the merit of my talent." If he lost it over the letter, "then it wouldn't be a project I'd want to work on anyway." And when the interviewer suggested a job for stability and cash would have been easier to get elsewhere, he said: "Yes, but the themes that run through Harry Potter are of love triumphing over hate — of acceptance. And that's why I'm doing it."

These answers complicate the portrait. A man who says fear of loss keeps him from resting shrugged, on the record, at losing a ten-year role. A Six reading can absorb that, since his dread is about whether he is good enough, but a One reading fits more simply: principle, acted on without asking anyone. Only the threats answer, hurt named and then turned toward the work, reads as a Six facing fear. The letter and the police decision read as principle and proportion, which a One fits at least as well.

## Why not Type 1?

The case for Type 1 is strong, and his friend Paloma Faith made it to his face. On her podcast [_Mad, Sad and Bad_](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=adqzaOktoQU) in March 2025, she asked whether he was concerned with being a good person, then told him: "I think you're very preoccupied about being good and doing the right thing all the time."

"Yeah, totally," he said.

The record backs her up. Rehearsal, he told The Big Issue in 2023, is "the pain of being confronted by inadequacy," and then: "Or by the reality that you might be bad. It's that sadomasochistic self-flagellation, you know?" Asked for three words to describe himself in a [Guardian questionnaire](https://www.theguardian.com/culture/2023/nov/18/paapa-essiedu-interview) that year, he chose "Tries his best." He treats his choices as moral ones: "I am not the kind of person who puts his head in the sand and will say yes to anything without thinking about the impact."

His anger has a One's shape too. He told Faith that "all the fights that I've been in as an adult have been trying to break up fights," and that "it probably is Christian guilt." Then: "I don't know if the anger is suppressed. I don't know if it's repressed." He tries, he said, to turn anger into action. Of Boris Johnson's lockdown parties, he said in 2022: "Johnson can get away with breaking the rules he made." Held-down anger, and anger at people who break their own rules, are One territory.

What keeps Six narrowly ahead is what he says the goodness is for. Right after "Yeah, totally," he turned to people-pleasing, and said what he thinks it really is.

> "it's really like an attempt to exert control, right? It's trying to control like what other people think of you, trying to control like how you are perceived in the world, trying to create safeness for yourself, safety for yourself"

A One's goodness answers to an inner standard: is this right? His answer points outward, to how he is perceived, and to safety.

Outward is also where a Three looks, and two of his three glosses are about image. But a Three manages the image, and he keeps handing interviewers the flaws. He told a stranger he'd been in "just a few little things." When Coel asked him who should play Kwame in her own show, Hattenstone reports, "He never put himself forward." "My aim isn't to be that famous," he told [The Times](https://www.thetimes.com/culture/theatre-dance/article/paapa-essiedu-on-growing-up-in-london-interviews-and-his-new-play-pass-over-m08bw3089) in 2020. The fear he names is of being uninvited, not outshone. In the Faith answer, perception is the means and safety the stated end.

His fear in rehearsal "that you might be bad" is a One's line, and it stays on the One side of the ledger.

None of this is decisive. The Faith answer is one podcast exchange, spoken in therapy vocabulary and in the second person. People-pleasing is also a [Type 2](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-2) theme, though Twos find it hard to ask for help and he asks openly. A One can fear exposure too. The scene that would settle it is not on record: a director's account of how he behaves when a production is in trouble. Does he gather allies and over-prepare, or quietly hold the line on standards? The King Lear night is the nearest thing, and it cuts both ways.

## Grief, peace and the other readings

The [Type 4](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-4) case starts with grief, which by 2025 he called a companion. Sadness, he told Faith, "creates the edges, the corners, the imperfections, the roughnesses of our character, of our personalities that allow us to be real, authentic, unique individuals."

That is Four language, and it counts. What keeps Four second is how he handles grief. "I really, passionately do not believe in acting as therapy," he told the actor Lennie James for [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/lifeandstyle/2020/dec/19/lennie-james-paapa-essiedu-walking-dead-i-may-destroy-you-tv) in 2020, though a loss that big shapes you unconsciously, he added, and he wants to "allow that to flow through the work that you do." By 2023, Maxwell reported, he would rather use his imagination than draw on grief. A Four tends to make the feeling the identity. He keeps it at arm's length from the job.

[Type 9](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-9) fits the man who breaks up fights, and Coel calls him "a peaceful, lovely, strong, sensitive man." The grief shutdown fits a Nine too, but it was limited to grief. A Nine tends to tune out threat, and he narrates anticipated threat in vivid detail.

## What the type doesn't explain

Some of the record sits awkwardly with a Six reading.

He turns down big work. In 2024 he told Mr Porter he had been "getting offered a lot of slave roles, a lot of period dramas as a butler or servant, which I think speaks to how I was perceived in the industry," though he wouldn't entirely rule such a role out if it had a point. He told The Big Issue he has tried "to be savvy about the propensity of the industry to try and clip my wings. But that involves saying no to a lot of things," including projects that became "very, very, very big successes." Wariness of being boxed in suits a Six, and holding to a standard suits a One. Neither predicts a man who fears loss turning down jobs that became hits.

His competitive streak overrides caution. He told Faith, as a comic story, about a half marathon he tried to run "way too fast." About 16 kilometers in he was "cooked," yet "I continued running for another three kilometres, even though my body was basically shut down." He woke in the medical tent unable to find words.

He is restless. "I find it difficult to sit still," he wrote in the 2023 questionnaire, naming the trait he most deplores in himself. That is closer to [Type 7](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-7) than to a cautious Six.

And the fear in this portrait is not what colleagues meet first. Jessie Buckley, his co-star in the 2022 film _Men_, called him "funny and naughty," but "with an ocean underneath."

## What the next decade can test

Of the ten-year Snape role, he told The Sunday Times: "I'll be 45 by the time I finish and I know my life is going to change in a big way, but I have to just surrender to that."
