In June 2022, the Guardian's Simon Hattenstone sat down with Paapa Essiedu in a café and opened with a compliment: you're a very good actor.

"Do you really think so?" Essiedu asked.

Hattenstone assumed he was fishing. He wasn't. He had started a new job the day before, he explained, and "on the day before the first day on every job, I have a nervous breakdown, thinking: this is the one where people will find me out, see that what's underneath the car bonnet doesn't work."

Hattenstone asked if he meant it. "Genuine!" he said, fiercely.

"Nervous breakdown" is his everyday phrase for the dread, not a diagnosis. But the cost is real. "I need a lot of support from the people who are close to me to drag me into the car to go to work on that first day," he told [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/culture/2022/jun/11/paapa-essiedu-grief-boris-johnson-i-may-destroy-you-michaela-coel). "It's generally only the first day or the first week."

Now the other scene. In January 2014, fresh out of drama school, Essiedu was understudying Edmund in Sam Mendes's _King Lear_ at the National Theatre. Partway through a preview, Sam Troughton, who played Edmund, lost his voice, and Essiedu went on for the rest of the show. The critic [Mark Lawson](https://www.theguardian.com/stage/2014/jan/27/king-lear-national-theatre-understudy) was in the audience, and he admits he checked Essiedu's knees for knocking.

> "There was, though, neither a tremble in limb or voice."

Born in London in 1990, Essiedu became the Royal Shakespeare Company's first Black Hamlet in 2016, broke through on screen as Kwame in Michaela Coel's _I May Destroy You_ in 2020, and won an Olivier Award in April 2026 for _All My Sons_. In April 2025, HBO cast him as Severus Snape in its _Harry Potter_ series, and the casting brought him death threats.

Put the café and the King Lear night side by side and a pattern shows. His fear arrives early, before anything has gone wrong. When something really does go wrong, he is the steady one. And when he explains why he tries so hard to be good, he talks about safety.

This analysis follows that pattern, and why it reads as Type 6 more than the principled Type 1 it closely resembles.

## What is Paapa Essiedu's personality type?

Paapa Essiedu's personality type is Enneagram Type 6, the Loyalist. Sixes sit in the head triad, whose core emotion is fear. His fear runs ahead of events: before almost every job he expects to be found out, and he leans on people close to him to get through it. When danger is real, he steadies.

A [Type 6](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6) lives a step ahead of trouble. It runs the worst case, looks for people and structures that will hold, and tests the ground before trusting it. Some Sixes back away from what scares them. Others go straight at it, the style Enneagram writers call counterphobic.

Essiedu does both, in sequence. The dread comes before the job. The steadiness arrives once the danger is in the room.

The strongest rival is [Type 1](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-1), a gut-triad type whose core emotion is anger held down by a strict inner critic, because Essiedu is diligent, hard on himself and openly principled. This is a read of his public record from 2014 to 2026, at medium confidence. The One case, [Type 4](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-4) and two others get a full hearing below.

## "This is the one where people will find me out"

The café confession was not a one-off. He has described the same doubt, in nearly the same shape, for a decade.

In March 2016, aged 25 and about to open as Hamlet, he told [the Observer](https://www.theguardian.com/stage/2016/mar/13/paapa-essiedu-hamlet-royal-shakespeare-theatre-stratford-interview): "If I never work again after this, Hamlet will be the role that ended my career." It read as a joke.

The casting had been harder to laugh off. On a 2020 [livestream](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jAcEe_qOVAE) with his old friend Calum Finlay, he recalled four months of auditions spent "very, very stressed and anxious and nervous," about ten seconds of elation when his agent called, then "overwhelming dread," like "a bucket of ice cold water."

Later in the stream, a viewer asked whether he had ever struggled with stage fright.

> "I'm always certain I'm gonna be terrible. It's gonna be the end of my career. I'm always like, why did I take this job? It was going so well. I've tricked so many people."

He added that the worry was not a useful place to put his energy. Finlay, for his part, recalled being begged not to come and see _Pass Over_ before press night.

The bigger the parts got, the more the line repeated. In 2022 he told [Soho House](https://www.sohohouse.com/house-notes/issue-006/film-and-entertainment/paapa-essiedu): "I often find myself thinking: When are they going to realise that they really wanted the other guy?" The interviewer, Jonathan Dean, added: "It does not sound like he is joking, either." In 2024 he told [the Observer](https://www.theguardian.com/culture/article/2024/jul/28/paapa-essiedu-is-this-part-harder-than-hamlet-yeah-its-different-gravy-mate-death-of-england-delroy-soho-theatre): "Every time I get any job, I go: 'Jesus, what? Really? Me?'"

The most telling detail is back in the café, where he described the dread from the inside.

> "I read, 'What are you doing here?' on everybody's face."

He knows he is doing it. "You know that thing when you project what your brain wants you to see on somebody who is probably just having a cup of tea?" Then, after a stop: "Maybe they are thinking it."

The verdict he fears belongs to other people, and he supplies it himself. That is how a Six's fear tends to work. Attention goes outward, scanning for signs that the people around you are about to withdraw their backing.

There are fair objections. First-night nerves are close to universal among actors, and British interviews reward self-deprecation. But Hattenstone pressed and he held to it, and he tells friends the same story off the press circuit. He also says it is easing. In August 2023 he told [The Times](https://www.thetimes.co.uk/article/paapa-essiedu-interview-the-effect-lyttelton-national-theatre-london-92vdn0nxb) that "right now I'm thinking less about what people might think about me."

## "If it all gets taken away"

A week after the café, Hattenstone called him back. Essiedu had been thinking about why he is so dismissive of his own success, and his explanation works like insurance.

> "If it all gets taken away, you don't feel that loss so keenly."

On a recent trip, he had told a stranger he'd been in "just a few little things," then gushed over her job in mezcal. "And I thought: what is wrong with me!" He named the habit: "Perennially living in the expectation that things are going to fuck up is just mad. Even if it makes it feel quite sweet when they don't."

Two years later, admitting to [Mr Porter](https://www.mrporter.com/en-gb/journal/fashion/interview-paapa-essiedu-25088214) that he is bad at taking time off, he said: "The fear of things being taken away from you at any moment really kicks in and you think you need to do something again before it's too late."

He has reasons to expect loss. His father, Tony, a lawyer, went back to Ghana when Essiedu was very young and died when he was 14. His mother, Selina, who taught fashion and design at adult-education colleges, raised him alone in Walthamstow, east London. "My mum was my best mate," he told Hattenstone.

She had cancer for seven years and died in his first year at the Guildhall School of Music and Drama, when he was 20. They never discussed that she might die. Asked whether she knew, he said: "Maybe. Probably. But she definitely protected me from that."

He went back to drama school the next day, "an emergency act of self-preservation in order to stop myself thinking or feeling or engaging." He locked their belongings in the loft of the family home and walked away "for about 10 years." After the funeral he didn't cry again for five or six years, he said on [Paloma Faith's podcast](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=adqzaOktoQU) in 2025.

It caught up with him at 25. Playing Romeo on his birthday, he was "in floods of tears the whole day," he told [Bustle](https://www.bustle.com/entertainment/paapa-essiedu-interview-the-effect). Therapy took hold "maybe only on the third attempt," and he still goes weekly. "If I didn't, I'd be incredibly anxious and unhappy," he told [The Sunday Times](https://www.thetimes.com/culture/tv-radio/article/paapa-essiedu-harry-potter-snape-interview-3hqkzzmlk) in 2026.

This is where the type argument has to be careful. Losing both parents by 20, on little money, would teach anyone to brace for loss, and public evidence can't separate temperament from that history. What it does show is how he handles the bracing. He calls it "mad," and he keeps walking into the jobs he dreads.

## The safety he builds

The riskiest decision of his youth came with a safety net attached.

He had a place to study medicine at University College London. For "the son of a West African woman," he told [The Untitled Magazine](https://untitled-magazine.com/?p=136725) in 2020, "you can be a doctor or you can be an accountant." Then he played Othello in the sixth form, and acting felt "renegade," he told [Glass](https://theglassmagazine.com/?p=108224), "a choice that I was making, as opposed to a choice that was being made for me."

He still wanted his mother's blessing. "She knew how important her consent was to me," he told The Guardian. She gave it with a condition:

> "Look, if you're going to do this thing, you've got to do it properly. You've got to treat it in the same way you would if you were a doctor, the same level of professionalism."

"It was just what I needed to hear," he said. In 2016 he credited her with "that first-generation immigrant commitment to safety and success."

It took two years of drama-school auditions, he said on [The Going Rogue Club podcast](https://sphinx.acast.com/p/open/s/643d3ebb4c61dd001119e92f/e/65faca784876ab001699896c/media.mp3) in 2024, before Guildhall, the last school to hold final recalls, took him. Only that year did his half-sister tell him that his mother used to ring her after each rejection, still hoping he would end up at medical school.

In his final year at Guildhall he set up a theatre company, Invertigo, with three classmates. He told Finlay it was about agency, and about fear:

> "I was really terrified about the feeling of having been in drama school and having a certain amount of stability within that structure"

Out in the industry, "it felt like all the agency, all the power existed outside of me." So, he said, "I wanted to take control of my destiny."

He has been wary of exploitation from the start. "For anyone to survive, you cannot live with your heart on your sleeve all the time because people will take advantage," he told the Observer in 2016.

His advice to others is about trust. Talking with Lennie James for [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/lifeandstyle/2020/dec/19/lennie-james-paapa-essiedu-walking-dead-i-may-destroy-you-tv) in 2020 about the sexual-assault storylines in _I May Destroy You_, he said: "You had to make sure that you were always in safe hands." The same month, he told drama students at [Mountview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=M0TPpp2GvQE) to stick together in rooms that feel unsafe, because there is strength in numbers. In this industry "you can feel very alone," he told [The Big Issue](https://www.bigissue.com/culture/papa-essiedu-its-hard-for-me-not-to-be-political-in-the-work-that-i-do/) in 2023. "But I've found community and strength, especially among people that have come from the same place as me."

The trust runs both ways. Michaela Coel, his friend since Guildhall, told Hattenstone he approaches characters "almost with fear and trembling." She called him "very dependable," and said "he's always there in times of crisis."

The man who needs friends to get him into the car on day one is the friend Coel counts on in an emergency. In Six terms, that is one system: loyalty needed and loyalty given.

## Steady when it counts

Back to that night at the National. By his own account on the [Dish podcast](https://pdst.fm/e/chtbl.com/track/43774/traffic.megaphone.fm/CGPL9251216488.mp3) in May 2026, the production was still in previews and understudies hadn't been rehearsed. He knew Edmund anyway. Bored with his own two or three lines, he had been turning up to Troughton's rehearsals, "Like some kind of stalker," and taking notes. "I'm a nerd."

When Mendes asked whether he wanted the script in hand, he said he knew the lines: "I'm not going to go on with the book."

Inside, he told the Observer in 2016, it was "an out-of-body experience" with "internal banshees." Outside, Lawson saw no tremble, and at the curtain call Simon Russell Beale, the production's Lear, pulled him out of the line to take a bow of his own. A [Guardian feature](https://www.theguardian.com/stage/2021/mar/29/teacher-kenneth-rea-the-outstanding-actor) later said he had "devotedly over-prepared."

That is fear doing its work in advance. A Six often prepares for disaster so thoroughly that, when it comes, the work is already done.

Hamlet followed the same arc. "It was the first time I was confronted by something where I thought, 'I don't know if I can do this. I don't know if I'm ready,'" he told The Times in 2023. Doing it anyway "taught me something about myself and what was possible for me." In 2024 he took on _Death of England: Delroy_, alone on stage for a 100-minute monologue.

Then came Snape. After his casting was announced, critics argued that his skin color should rule him out of playing a man written as white. Some went much further.

"I've been told, 'Quit or I'll murder you,'" he told The Sunday Times in March 2026. On Instagram, he said, he would see someone threatening to come to his house and kill him. "So while I'm pretty sure I'm not going to be murdered…" He laughed nervously. "That could age badly!"

"I'd be lying if I said it doesn't affect me emotionally," he said. Then he turned it around.

> "But the abuse fuels me. And makes me more passionate about making this character my own, because I think of how I felt as a kid."

He has not reported the threats. "I don't think some 17-year-old boy being put in jail for two weeks for threatening to murder me would actually make me feel any better."

Weeks after his casting, he was among more than 1,500 people in film and TV who signed an open letter condemning the UK Supreme Court's ruling that "woman" and "sex" in the Equality Act refer only to biological sex, [The Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/tv-and-radio/2025/may/06/i-dont-have-the-power-jk-rowling-wont-sack-paapa-essiedu-from-harry-potter-tv-show-over-trans-rights). Some fans of [J.K. Rowling](/personality-analysis/j.k.-rowling) called for him to be sacked. Rowling posted: "I don't have the power to sack an actor from the series and I wouldn't exercise it if I did."

Asked whether he sought advice before signing, he said, "No." Then: "My points of view are mine and will continue to be so." He said he signed because trans artists "should be able to work without being intimidated," and added: "I have been really supported by the production team and I'd sign that letter again today."

Here he looks least like a cautious Six. He signed without asking anyone, weeks into the biggest job of his career, and he has passed on the protection of a police report. The counterphobic Six explains it: once a threat is concrete and named, he moves toward it. Still, look at what he reached for in the same breath. He named the production team behind him, and the kid he used to be.

## Why not Type 1?

The case for Type 1 is strong, and his friend Paloma Faith made it to his face. On her podcast _Mad, Sad and Bad_ in March 2025, she asked whether he was concerned with being a good person, then told him: "I think you're very preoccupied about being good and doing the right thing all the time."

"Yeah, totally," he said.

The record backs her up. Rehearsal, he told The Big Issue in 2023, is "the pain of being confronted by inadequacy," and then: "Or by the reality that you might be bad. It's that sadomasochistic self-flagellation, you know?" Asked for three words to describe himself in a [Guardian questionnaire](https://www.theguardian.com/culture/2023/nov/18/paapa-essiedu-interview) that year, he chose "Tries his best." He calls himself "more hard-working than clever" and "definitely diligent."

He is choosy on principle. He has turned down "a lot of slave roles, a lot of period dramas as a butler or servant," and projects that became "very, very, very big successes." He told The Big Issue: "I am not the kind of person who puts his head in the sand and will say yes to anything without thinking about the impact."

He is rarely angry. He told Faith that "all the fights that I've been in as an adult have been trying to break up fights," and credited churchgoing until his late teens: "it probably is Christian guilt."

A One lives in the gut triad, with anger held down by an inner critic that insists on doing things right. Much of that fits Essiedu.

What tips the read to Six is what he says the goodness is for. Right after "Yeah, totally," he explained that the question made him laugh because it was "very front cortex at the moment for me." He tied it to people-pleasing, and said what he thinks people-pleasing really is.

> "it's really like an attempt to exert control, right? It's trying to control like what other people think of you, trying to control like how you are perceived in the world, trying to create safeness for yourself, safety for yourself"

He went on to ask whether anyone can live that way and stay truthful with themselves. "Probably not," he said. In the end, he suggested, you have to decide whether that matters more than letting your true self come out.

A One's goodness answers to an inner standard: is this right? His answer points outward, to how he is perceived, and to safety. The critic he describes elsewhere talks in other people's voices: "What are you doing here?", "they really wanted the other guy," "if it all gets taken away." His fear in rehearsal "that you might be bad" is a One's line, and it stays on the One side of the ledger.

His anger points the same way. A One's anger fixes on error. His public anger fixes on people with power who fail those in their charge.

In 2022 he described a Guildhall improvisation in which a teacher, playing a prison warden, shouted a racial slur at him and Coel. The school [apologised "unreservedly"](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2022/jun/10/top-drama-school-apologises-paapa-essiedu-michaela-coel-appalling-racism-guildhall). What still outraged him was the breach, a teacher choosing "to treat someone who's meant to be in your care like that." He grew up feeling the police in his area were not "there to protect you; they are there to see what you've done," he told [The Times](https://www.thetimes.com/culture/theatre-dance/article/paapa-essiedu-on-growing-up-in-london-interviews-and-his-new-play-pass-over-m08bw3089) in 2020. And of Boris Johnson's lockdown parties, after losing an aunt and uncle to Covid, he said in 2022: "Johnson can get away with breaking the rules he made."

None of this is decisive. The Faith answer is one podcast exchange, spoken in therapy vocabulary and in the second person. People-pleasing is also a [Type 2](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-2) theme, and a One can fear exposure too. The scene that would settle it is not on record: a director's account of how he behaves when a production is in trouble. Does he gather allies and over-prepare, or quietly hold the line on standards?

## Grief, peace and the other readings

The Type 4 case starts with grief, which he now calls a companion. He told The Times in 2023 that "long-term grief is a way of keeping those we have lost alive." Sadness, he told Faith, "creates the edges, the corners, the imperfections, the roughnesses of our character, of our personalities that allow us to be real, authentic, unique individuals."

That is Four language. But for years he locked his grief in a loft rather than living in it, and he keeps it out of the work. "I really, passionately do not believe in acting as therapy," he told Lennie James. He puts on his costume "two minutes before a play starts," he told Mr Porter.

[Type 9](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-9) fits the man who breaks up fights, and Coel calls him "a peaceful, lovely, strong, sensitive man." But Nines struggle to say no, and he says it often, in public, knowing the cost.

Type 2 has a foothold. He was drawn to medicine for "being able to meet people, speak to people, try and work people out, and help people," he told [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/culture/2018/sep/08/bbc-press-star-paapa-essiedu-the-word-diversity-doesnt-mean-anything) in 2018. Yet Twos find it hard to ask for help, and he asks openly, down to the ride to work on day one.

## What the type doesn't explain

Some of the record sits awkwardly with a Six reading.

He has a competitive streak that overrides caution. He told Faith, as a comic story, about a half marathon he tried to run "way too fast." About 16 kilometers in he was "cooked," yet "I continued running for another three kilometres, even though my body was basically shut down." He woke in the medical tent unable to find words. "I'm mad when I'm actually in competition myself," he said, laughing.

He is restless. "I find it difficult to sit still," he wrote, naming the trait he most deplores in himself. Dominic Maxwell of The Times remembered "a kind of Tiggerish energy, a physical restlessness" when they met in 2016. By 2023, Maxwell wrote, "he is all containment."

He is funny, and colleagues tend to mention it first. Jessie Buckley called him "funny and naughty," but "with an ocean underneath." Lucy Prebble called him "playful, brave and also technically brilliant," adding, "I never feel unsafe when he's on stage. Unless he wants me to." [Saoirse Ronan](/personality-analysis/saoirse-ronan) said "he doesn't take himself too seriously" ([Dazed](https://www.dazeddigital.com/film-tv/article/64727/1/saoirse-ronan-paapa-essiedu-the-outrun-interview-addiction-2024), 2024).

And he hates being watched while he eats. "I hate eating in front of people, so this is an act of love," he told the Bustle reporter over lunch in 2024.

## Ten years of Snape

The role is meant to last about a decade. "I'll be 45 by the time I finish and I know my life is going to change in a big way, but I have to just surrender to that," he told The Sunday Times. HBO has scheduled the first season to premiere on December 25, 2026, [TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/streaming/hbo-max/hbo-max-dropped-the-harry-potter-tv-show-trailer-after-its-uk-launch-and-the-shows-magical-release-date-will-delight-potterheads) reported in March 2026.

He took it partly for the boy in the library, where his mother took him in the holidays because she couldn't afford a babysitter. "I loved Harry Potter. I never saw the films but the books were escapism when other things were less easy for me."

Accepting the Olivier in April 2026, he urged "everyone at governmental level, to continue funding these organisations to make sure that the next generation of theatre makers had the opportunities that I had" ([National Youth Theatre](https://www.nyt.org.uk/news/new-news-article-375)). He wants the structures that held him kept standing.

The decade ahead will test this read. A Six would describe Snape with relief at a trusted team and a stable home, and aim the dread at how audiences receive it. A One would talk about getting the role right and bristle at compromises in the work.

For now, the best guide is something he told The Times in 2023, about living with rejection "as a constant companion."

> "The luck won't always go your way, so when it does you've got to celebrate it."
