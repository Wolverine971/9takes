In the early 2000s, Josh Hartnett was asked to play Superman, and he had an idea for the part. He described it to [the Guardian's Ryan Gilbey](https://www.theguardian.com/film/2020/oct/23/josh-hartnett-target-number-one-pearl-harbor-the-virgin-suicides) in October 2020:

> "I had this idea that because he lives in this world where he can't touch anything without it flying across the room, he has become almost afraid of himself and his own power. He doesn't know how to be Superman any more."

The studio "didn't really want a fear-based character at the centre of their movie," he said. He walked away.

Offered the most invulnerable hero in fiction, Hartnett went looking for the fear in him. Gilbey read the pitch as a metaphor for the actor's own life at the time, and Hartnett's later accounts fit that reading.

Hartnett was born on July 21, 1978, in Saint Paul, Minnesota. Between 1997 and 2002 he went from the TV series _Cracker_ to _The Virgin Suicides_, _Pearl Harbor_ and _Black Hawk Down_, and the press billed him as "the next [Tom Cruise](/personality-analysis/tom-cruise)." Then, in 2002, he stepped back and spent about a year and a half away from the business in Minnesota.

Studio films followed through _30 Days of Night_ (2007). From 2008 until the TV series _Penny Dreadful_ (2014–2016) he worked mostly in small independent films, several released straight to DVD. By his forties he was back in big studio films, including Christopher Nolan's _Oppenheimer_ (2023) and M. Night Shyamalan's _Trap_ (2024). In October 2026 he had two releases in one week: the thriller _Verity_, opposite [Anne Hathaway](/personality-analysis/anne-hathaway) and [Dakota Johnson](/personality-analysis/dakota-johnson), and Netflix's _Below_, which he also executive produced.

What was he protecting each time he said no?

## What is Josh Hartnett's personality type?

Josh Hartnett is most likely an Enneagram [Type 6](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6), the Loyalist, held at medium confidence. Sixes belong to the head triad, the types built around fear, and they look for people they can rely on. Hartnett braced for attack before fame peaked and later chose work by whom he trusts. Type 9 runs close.

The strongest rival is [Type 9](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-9), the Peacemaker. A man who leaves fame for an English village looks like a Nine, and 9takes reads other actors with walk-away stories, such as [Keanu Reeves](/personality-analysis/keanu-reeves), as Nines.

## "Then it defined me"

Hartnett's parents split when he was small, and he grew up mainly with his father, a former musician, and his stepmother, an artist who gave him his love of painting, the Observer reported in 2024. A knee injury as a teenager pushed him from football toward youth theater and the part of Huck Finn, who was, he told a 2003 press round table [reported by the Philippine Star](https://www.philstar.com/entertainment/2003/09/19/220926/heart-josh), "the person I've always wanted to be": someone "not always accepted by society but who finds his own ideals."

His first films, _Halloween H20_ and _The Faculty_ (both 1998), were made for Dimension, the Weinstein brothers' horror label. The contract bound him to "like, five more or something," he told Gilbey, and once he was popular Dimension used it to become co-producer of films he was already making, such as _O_.

Then came the offer of a lead in Michael Bay's _Pearl Harbor_ (2001), playing the best friend of [Ben Affleck](/personality-analysis/ben-affleck)'s character. Its producers, [the Observer's Rachel Cooke reported](https://www.theguardian.com/film/2002/apr/21/features.magazine) in April 2002, kept telling him the film would "change his life." He told her: "Yeah, I nearly turned it down." Then: "It was a role a lot of people wanted but... I didn't want to be famous." Cooke added, "Still, someone obviously persuaded him to think again." His only explanation then: "Well, we're all on a journey."

Later secondhand accounts name the persuader as his father. [AnOther magazine wrote in 2019](https://www.anothermag.com/design-living/12562/what-happened-to-josh-hartnett-actor-pearl-harbour) that he consulted him, and [El País said in 2021](https://english.elpais.com/usa/2021-05-26/why-josh-hartnett-left-hollywood-and-why-hes-back-i-decided-to-have-a-life.html) that his father convinced him, though neither cites a primary source.

Eighteen years later he gave Gilbey a longer account, and both halves of it matter. "I didn't necessarily want things to change that much," he said. "I was happy with the amount of fame I had and the types of roles I was getting." Then he questioned that instinct:

> "At the same time, I asked myself: 'Am I just afraid that by doing Pearl Harbor, I'm going to enter a new category of film-making that I might not be ready for?' I ultimately chose to do it because turning it down would've been based on fear. Then it defined me, which means I was right to fear it."

The first half is a contented young man. The second half is the move that matters for typing. He suspected his own reluctance, named it as fear and refused to let fear make the call. Then, when the film did change his life, he counted the outcome as proof that the fear had been right.

That is a 2020 retelling, and it leaves out whoever pushed him, so it shows how he later explained the decision, not how he reasoned at the time. Leaning on a trusted adviser fits a Six as well as a self-audit does.

He also felt the backlash coming. A splashy 2001 Vanity Fair piece written on the set of _Black Hawk Down_ was, he told Gilbey, "an awful piece." "People got a chip on their shoulder about me after that. They genuinely thought I'd been thrust on them."

Rereading it years later, he decided it wasn't so bad. He remembered the feeling, though: "Oh my God! I'm not the tallest poppy yet – don't cut me down!" He called it "a set-up-to-fail moment."

He was bracing for the cut before he had finished rising.

## Who gets to define him

Cooke's profile, published in April 2002, has him walking in "so gauche and anxious," though she doubted his anti-fame line: "The way he tells it - though I am not entirely sure I believe him - fame is agony." He said he planned time off: "I've been doing publicity for so long and before that I worked for four years straight."

"After Pearl Harbor came out and Black Hawk Down was coming out, it felt oddly stifling," he told [Tom Power on CBC's Q](https://www.cbc.ca/arts/q/josh-hartnett-on-escaping-the-heartthrob-label-to-explore-more-interesting-roles-1.7282192) in 2024. He was on magazine covers and chased by paparazzi, he told [NPR's Juana Summers](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/02/nx-s1-5788798/josh-hartnett-reflects-on-verity-and-living-a-much-less-hollywood-existence) in October 2026, "and I didn't want to feel like I wasn't in any control of my own life."

"I never really lived in L.A.," he told [Julia Stiles](https://www.interviewmagazine.com/film/josh-hartnett-tells-julia-stiles-how-he-escaped-the-hollywood-trap) in 2024, so he went home to Minnesota. In 2004 he told [Newcastle's Evening Chronicle](https://www.chroniclelive.co.uk/whats-on/film-tv/hell-stay-out-of-limelight-1630334) he had taken "15 months out of the business" (later accounts say 18). "I was like a lot of people in their 20s, wondering, 'Who am I? What do I want to be when I grow up?'"

Twenty years on, his explanations gather around one idea: others were defining him before he could define himself. He told NPR:

> "I felt like I hadn't defined myself yet. And so there were lots of people that were kind of putting their definition of me out there into the world, and that was affecting my own idea of who I am."

To [Hollywood Authentic](https://hollywoodauthentic.com/josh-hartnett) in 2024 he described "a lot of intrusion into my life at that point" and added, "I didn't know how to protect myself exactly at that point because I was young."

A 2014 interview with Details, [as later quoted by PopCulture.com](https://popculture.com/celebrity/news/josh-hartnett-says-he-walked-away-from-hollywood-for-mental-health-reasons-in-rare-interview/), put the peak years more bleakly: "I was alone. I didn't trust anyone." The Details original could not be checked for this analysis, so treat the wording as reported.

Wanting to define yourself is not peculiar to Sixes; Fours, Sevens and Nines guard it too. What is more Six-like is how he pictures fame: as exposure to attack. In 2021 he told [MR PORTER](https://www.mrporter.com/en-gb/journal/fashion/actor-josh-hartnett-interview-1597802) that the stars at the top "are terrified that someone's coming up behind them," so "you're going to spend your whole life looking over your shoulder. I never wanted that." His remedy was particular people, not solitude. "I wanted to be myself amongst people that I knew," he told Stiles. To MR PORTER: "Those people make me who I am."

## Saying no to the capes

Warner Bros. approached him about Superman around 2002, MR PORTER reported, and he said no. Asked why at the 2003 round table, he began: "It wasn't what I wanted to do. There's really no specific reason. I like the people involved in it." Then came a check on himself: "I asked myself, 'Am I going to feel fulfilled doing this?' The answer was no. Probably not."

Over the years his reason has widened, from the part to the career to the whole life. In 2003 it was the part itself: "putting on the tights just didn't make sense." In 2020 it was the frightened Superman the studio didn't want. By 2021 it was strategy: "it was so obvious to me to turn it down," he told MR PORTER. "I was being offered movies by the very top directors. And Superman was a risk." By 2024 it was the life itself. "I just didn't want my life to be swallowed up by my work," he told [the Observer's Stuart McGurk](https://www.theguardian.com/film/article/2024/jul/28/i-didnt-want-to-be-swallowed-up-actor-josh-hartnett-on-swapping-hollywood-for-hampshire).

Batman is often added to the list, but he never turned it down. [Christopher Nolan](/personality-analysis/christopher-nolan) said in 2023, [as reported by CNN](https://ktvz.com/?p=1181915), that "It never got that far." Nolan had "an initial conversation" with him in the early-to-mid 2000s, but Hartnett "had read my brother's script for 'The Prestige'" and was more interested in that. The Prestige role went to [Christian Bale](/personality-analysis/christian-bale), whom Nolan had cast as Batman.

In 2015, after a Playboy interview was reported as a boast, Hartnett told USA Today, [as quoted by ComicBook.com](https://comicbook.com/comicbook/news/josh-hartnett-clarifies-comments-on-turning-down-christopher-nol), that he did not regret Superman. The Nolan meeting was another matter:

> "it was about relationships I wish I'd formed but because of my fear of the concept of being one thing in my career and one thing only, I decided I would just stay away from that one aspect. That was only part of what I was doing at the time."

"That one aspect" appears to mean the superhero franchise on offer.

The studios saw it differently. "They looked at me as someone who had bitten the hand that fed me," he told Gilbey. "It wasn't that. I wasn't doing it to be recalcitrant or a rebel." He "didn't respond to the idea of playing the same character over and over," so he went looking for smaller films and "burned my bridges at the studios because I wasn't participating." Yet in 2026 he told [The Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/tv/tv-features/josh-hartnett-below-interview-widows-bay-car-crash-season-2-1236730830/), "I had to fight really hard to get out of that box." Quietly not taking part is how a Nine resists; a hard fight is not. His two accounts point opposite ways.

Of his Superman and Batman explanations, only the secondhand 2015 one names fear outright. Read together, they still share one worry, being fixed in place by other people's idea of him, and Sixes are not the only type who has it. Why he overruled his fear for _Pearl Harbor_ and not for Superman is still open; a three-film deal, as MR PORTER described the offer, may simply have been the bigger threat.

## A pretty good bullshit meter

At 23, he told Cooke that his choices were "completely down to me." His advisers gave suggestions, he said, "but I'm one of those people who when someone says 'turn right,' go left. I don't know whether they've figured that out yet..." Resistance the advisers may not even have noticed is as much a Nine's quiet stubbornness as a Six's open challenge. He told her he had "a pretty good bullshit meter."

Before playing the journalist Victor Malarek in _Target Number One_ (2020), he did what Gilbey called his homework and met the real man. Why? "I wanted to see if he was full of shit." Malarek's critics had accused him of putting himself at the center of his own stories. Hartnett's verdict: "Ultimately, Victor is a humble man."

Gilbey also asked about Harvey Weinstein, whose label had made those first films. A few days after their Zoom interview, Hartnett phoned him back because he didn't feel he had made his feelings about Harvey Weinstein clear. "I wasn't surprised he was a creep," he said. "But I guess I was surprised at the extent of his creepiness." Having been part of the Weinstein brothers' "stable of talent," as Gilbey put it, Hartnett also had a reputational reason to be clear.

None of these episodes settles the type, and some are ordinary professional care. Together they describe a man who does not smooth things over: he checks a subject's story and goes back to make a condemnation plainer, not softer. That counts against a Nine's peacekeeping, though a One would do the same.

## Choosing people instead of parts

The later Hartnett has a different answer to the same worry. When Nolan called him about two decades later for _Oppenheimer_, in which he played the physicist Ernest Lawrence, "the implication was: 'I'd better take it.' You only get so many opportunities with Chris Nolan." In 2024 he told McGurk what he had learned, echoing the 2015 regret:

> "I recognise the missed opportunity to work with a guy like Chris. And I've figured out that as much as you're worried about curating your career to things you're interested in, I don't believe that's the most important thing any more. It's about finding people who you really trust."

McGurk called it "his new rule: work with people you trust." The rule is older than that. In 2021 he said that while people "thought I was crazy for not chasing that brass ring a little harder," he "felt completely comfortable being with the people I knew liked me before I was making films." He told [The Film Stage](https://thefilmstage.com/josh-hartnett-on-verity-and-working-with-warren-beatty-oliver-stone-and-sofia-coppola/) in 2026 that _Lucky Number Slevin_ (2006) "is one of my favorite films I've ever been a part of, because that was all friends," though by Gilbey's account Dimension had attached itself to _Slevin_ too.

He lives in the Hampshire countryside with his wife, the English actor Tamsin Egerton, whom he married in November 2021, and their four children. He returned to studio work, he told Stiles, because "we had a couple of kids, so I wanted to establish something that was a little bit more solid."

Asked on [CBC's Q](https://www.cbc.ca/listen/cbc-podcasts/q-with-tom-power) in 2026 for the best note a director ever gave him, he described Oliver Stone's _White Lies_, unreleased as of October 2026. When Stone asked him, he told The Film Stage, "I had to do it"; he plays a character based on Stone ("it's not officially Oliver"). In one scene Stone complimented everyone except him. "I knew that he was messing with me," Hartnett said, but he wanted badly to please him and pushed further and further. Stone got what he needed, he said, "by playing off my own insecurity and not giving me anything."

## Why not Type 9?

The case for Nine starts in his own words. In the same 2002 interview as the "turn right" line, he told Cooke:

> "I just kind go along for the ride. You need some people in the world who are determined to do one thing in life, to make that thing their goal. But luckily, because you have all those other people being pillars, holding up the world, someone like me can just wander through."

As he left for lunch, he added, "You know, I'm not interested in fighting the publicity machine." Then: "That's not my fight. There are other things I want to fight for. I want interesting roles. I want to do some good in the world."

His life in the 2020s sounds like a Nine's too. Of the countryside, he told Hollywood Authentic in 2024: "Honestly, I've become really fond of being here: the quiet, the still; all of it." Of life with four children, he told McGurk: "In a way, less is happening. But more of the important stuff is happening."

He credits his father with a different measure of success: "My dad was not someone who valued achievement in that way – high-level work achievement as a means of proving himself." His advice about bad press is a Nine's: "you've just got to let it slide off your back because it's not the important thing anymore," he told Stiles in 2024. Losing control of a finished film is "terrifying," he told [SHARP](https://sharpmagazine.com/2026/08/25/josh-hartnett-interview-2026-below/) in 2026, then gave the same answer: "Eventually you develop a thick skin about it and learn to let it go."

Even the _Pearl Harbor_ answer opens with a Nine's wish that things stay as they are.

What separates the two types is how they handle pressure. Under strain a Nine keeps the peace inside, going numb, letting things slide or quietly digging in, and explains hard choices by what would disturb them least. A Six scans for the threat, tests whoever is in charge and recruits allies, and explains hard choices through fear and trust.

Run that test on the 2002 retreat and it comes out close to even. His words for it are a Nine's: it "had more to do with just plain sanity," he told Stiles, and "it didn't feel entirely healthy. So I just sort of took a step back," he told Hollywood Authentic. What he left, and where he went, read more like a Six's: intrusion he "didn't know how to protect myself" from, and a return to "people that I knew." A Nine retreats to be left in peace. A Six retreats toward people he can count on.

The Six reading leads on three other points. He pictured the attack before it came, as with the tallest poppy. He has built safety out of specific trusted people for two decades, from the friends who "liked me before I was making films" to his "new rule." A Nine can also settle among familiar people; what looks more like a Six is the filter, friends who liked him before fame gave anyone a reason to.

And he keeps naming a fear and then walking into it, which Enneagram writers call counterphobic: a Six who moves toward the thing he fears. _Pearl Harbor_ is one case. For _Trap_ (2024), he told [/Film](https://slashfilm.com/1632836/m-night-shyamalan-trap-has-different-twist-you-wont-see-coming-exclusive-interview), his first reaction to Shyamalan's interest was "Yes, 100%," then the script brought "'Oh my God, if this goes wrong, it's all on me.'" After a laugh, he called it "a terrific challenge, but also I love challenges." The car racing fits too, though a label that stretches over almost any bold act means little without the trust pattern beside it.

The 2002 drift line remains unexplained. At 23, months before stepping back, he described himself much as a Nine would. A Six reading has to treat that as a young man's modesty or a pose for a journalist, and it might be neither. That is why the confidence stays at medium.

Two other types have a claim. [Type 4](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-4) is driven by the search for an authentic, distinct identity. It fits Huck Finn, his 2024 wish to be "more myself than that" and his joke to Stiles, "But I like being the outlier." Yet the painting came from his stepmother, and he grounds identity in old friends, not difference. He calls talking about his own career "self-indulgent."

[Type 7](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-7) is driven to keep options open and escape confinement. Its best evidence is the worry at the center of the Six case: in his 2015 words, "being one thing in my career and one thing only." The thrill-seeking fits too: "I used to track-race Porsche cars all the time," he told Hollywood Authentic in 2024, and that year Shyamalan recalled Hartnett telling him that "danger is what really drives him," [as TheWrap reported](https://thewrap.com/m-night-shyamalan-why-he-cast-josh-hartnett-trap). The difference shows when achievement went flat. "I had to find something else to fill that gap," he told Stiles. A Seven would reach for new options; he reached for people: "I felt, and still feel, that community is what fills it." The Seven question narrows but does not close.

## What the type doesn't explain

Some of Hartnett's caution has plain causes. Asked in 2024 whether his step back traced to one moment, he told McGurk it wasn't so simple, then said: "People's attention to me at the time was borderline unhealthy." Then: "Well, look, I don't want to give this a lot of weight." He described people stalking him and, when he was 27, "a guy showed up at one of my premieres with a gun, claiming to be my father." That was around 2005, after the first break, but it shows what the attention could become. Back then, he told Stiles, "it was then the press's mission to tear you apart."

Frightening years can teach anyone to be vigilant. The type reading cannot rule out that the vigilance was learned rather than built in.

The fear words have also softened into idiom. In 2026 he told The Film Stage that the week _Verity_ and _Below_ came out felt "simultaneously terrifying" and joked, "this might be the last time we speak." The anxiety he names directly is narrow. At the 2018 British Independent Film Awards he said, [on the red carpet](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZQoGIUaFeug), "I get a lot of anxiety about these sort of events because I have to go up on stage and read."

Other things fit no tidy pattern. A Six is often attached to familiar ground, yet moving to England, he said in 2024, "didn't feel unnatural." And his deepest value may simply be inherited: "Work is never going to fulfill you in the way the family does," he told Q in 2026.

What does hold across his accounts is the habit behind the _Pearl Harbor_ question. He looks for the danger, says so out loud and then decides what to do about it. In his early twenties the answer was distance. In his forties it is a short list of people he trusts.
