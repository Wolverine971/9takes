In the early 2000s, Josh Hartnett was asked to play Superman, and he had an idea for the part. He described it to [the Guardian's Ryan Gilbey](https://www.theguardian.com/film/2020/oct/23/josh-hartnett-target-number-one-pearl-harbor-the-virgin-suicides) in October 2020:

> "I had this idea that because he lives in this world where he can't touch anything without it flying across the room, he has become almost afraid of himself and his own power. He doesn't know how to be Superman any more."

The studio "didn't really want a fear-based character at the centre of their movie," he said. He walked away.

Offered the most invulnerable hero in fiction, Hartnett went looking for the fear in him. Gilbey read the pitch as a metaphor for the actor's own life at the time, and Hartnett's own explanations point the same way.

Across 24 years of interviews, when he explains the biggest choices of his career, the same few words keep returning: afraid, trust, control, defined.

Hartnett was born on July 21, 1978, in Saint Paul, Minnesota. Between 1997 and 2002 he went from the TV series _Cracker_ to _Halloween H20_, _The Faculty_, _The Virgin Suicides_, _Pearl Harbor_, _Black Hawk Down_, _O_ and _40 Days and 40 Nights_, and the press billed him as "the next Tom Cruise." Then he stepped back. He moved home to Minnesota and spent about a year and a half away from the business.

By his forties he was back in big studio films, including Christopher Nolan's _Oppenheimer_ (2023) and M. Night Shyamalan's _Trap_ (2024). In October 2026 he had two releases in one week: the thriller _Verity_, opposite [Anne Hathaway](/personality-analysis/anne-hathaway) and [Dakota Johnson](/personality-analysis/dakota-johnson), and Netflix's _Below_, which he also executive produced.

He has spent years correcting the story that he vanished. "The narrative that's been created over the last couple months is that I disappeared, and I was some hermit for the last few years and joined a cult or something," he told [the Associated Press](https://www.csmonitor.com/The-Culture/TV/2014/0514/Penny-Dreadful-actor-Josh-Hartnett-discusses-his-role-on-the-Victorian-TV-show) in 2014. The more useful question is what he was protecting each time he said no.

## What is Josh Hartnett's personality type?

Josh Hartnett's personality type is Enneagram Type 6, the Loyalist. Sixes are head-triad types, and fear is the emotion their whole strategy is built around. When Hartnett explains his biggest choices, from taking _Pearl Harbor_ to stepping back from Hollywood to picking directors, he keeps returning to fear, trust and the question of who gets to define him.

A [Type 6](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6) scouts for what could break, and wants a person or a plan that will hold. Sixes test before they trust, whether the thing under test is a school, a studio or a person. Their doubt also turns inward. A Six can treat their own fear as a suspect and interrogate it.

That inward interrogation is the most distinctive thing in Hartnett's record. It shows up first in the biggest yes of his career.

The strongest rival is [Type 9](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-9), the Peacemaker. A man who leaves the noise of fame for Minnesota and later an English village looks like a Nine at first glance, and 9takes reads other actors with walk-away stories, such as [Keanu Reeves](/personality-analysis/keanu-reeves), as Nines. The Six reading is held at medium confidence, and the case for Nine gets its own section below.

## "Then it defined me"

Hartnett played high-school football until a knee injury as a teenager pushed him toward youth theater, where he was cast as Huck Finn in _Tom Sawyer_. He had wanted to be a painter. At a 2003 press round table, [reported by the Philippine Star](https://www.philstar.com/entertainment/2003/09/19/220926/heart-josh), he said he identified with the character:

> "Yes, that's the person I've always wanted to be, somebody like Huck Finn. Somebody who's not always accepted by society but who finds his own ideals and isn't afraid to go out on a limb."

The ideal he named was defined against fear.

Within a few years he was offered a lead in Michael Bay's _Pearl Harbor_, playing the best friend of [Ben Affleck](/personality-analysis/ben-affleck)'s character. Its producers, [the Observer's Rachel Cooke reported](https://www.theguardian.com/film/2002/apr/21/features.magazine) in April 2002, kept telling him the film would "change his life." He told her: "Yeah, I nearly turned it down." Then: "It was a role a lot of people wanted but... I didn't want to be famous."

Eighteen years later he gave Gilbey the fuller version, and both halves of it matter. He began with the instinct to stay put. "I didn't necessarily want things to change that much," he said. "I was happy with the amount of fame I had and the types of roles I was getting." Then he described what he did with that instinct:

> "At the same time, I asked myself: 'Am I just afraid that by doing Pearl Harbor, I'm going to enter a new category of film-making that I might not be ready for?' I ultimately chose to do it because turning it down would've been based on fear. Then it defined me, which means I was right to fear it."

The first half is a contented young man. The second half is the move that matters for typing. He suspected his own reluctance, named it as fear and refused to let fear make the call. Then, when the film did change his life, he counted the outcome as proof that the fear had been right.

The rest of the decade suggests he took that lesson seriously.

Around the same time, he felt the backlash coming. A splashy Vanity Fair piece written on the set of _Black Hawk Down_ was, he told Gilbey, "an awful piece." He wondered aloud whether it had even quoted him, then said: "People got a chip on their shoulder about me after that. They genuinely thought I'd been thrust on them."

Rereading it years later, he decided it wasn't so bad. He remembered the feeling, though: "I'm not the tallest poppy yet." He called it "a set-up-to-fail moment."

He was bracing for the cut before he had finished rising.

## A habit of testing the people in charge

The fear in Hartnett's record rarely looks timid. More often it looks like a test.

At 18 he enrolled in the acting conservatory at SUNY Purchase. He objected to the school admitting more students than it would graduate and thought its competitive structure "stifled students' creativity," and instead of writing an assigned essay, he wrote to the people in charge. As he told the 2003 round table:

> "I wrote a letter to the dean and to my teachers about the policy. They didn't appreciate it, so they asked me to leave. I stayed in that school for only five months."

At 23, with his career exploding, he told Cooke that his choices were "completely down to me." His advisers gave suggestions, he said, "but I'm one of those people who when someone says 'turn right,' go left. I don't know whether they've figured that out yet..."

Cooke watched him closely. She described him walking in "so gauche and anxious," floating a theory about fame and then checking it ("tell me if it's bullshit"), and adding praise for [Tom Cruise](/personality-analysis/tom-cruise), "perhaps afraid that he might sound as if he is dissing Cruise." He told her he had "a pretty good bullshit meter."

Before playing the journalist Victor Malarek in _Target Number One_ (2020), he met the real Malarek. Gilbey asked why. "I wanted to see if he was full of shit," Hartnett said.

The test runs in the other direction too. Gilbey's piece records a small, telling act. A few days after their Zoom interview, Hartnett phoned him back because he didn't feel he had made his feelings about Harvey Weinstein clear.

On the call he put it plainly: "I wasn't surprised he was a creep," he said. "But I guess I was surprised at the extent of his creepiness." Asked whether he had been harassed himself, he started an answer, changed tack and declined to claim it: "That's not my experience and it's not my place to claim that."

"He is nothing if not conscientious," Gilbey wrote.

Hartnett had described the same reflex in 2015, after remarks he made to Playboy about Batman were reported as a boast. He told USA Today, [as quoted by ComicBook.com](https://comicbook.com/comicbook/news/josh-hartnett-clarifies-comments-on-turning-down-christopher-nol), that he avoids reading his own press and that "you have to be cautious about how you put certain things."

He tests the people he is about to trust. He also checks his own words for how they will land and goes back to repair them.

## Who gets to define him

After _Black Hawk Down_, the pressure peaked. "After Pearl Harbor came out and Black Hawk Down was coming out, it felt oddly stifling," he told [Tom Power on CBC's Q](https://www.cbc.ca/arts/q/josh-hartnett-on-escaping-the-heartthrob-label-to-explore-more-interesting-roles-1.7282192) in 2024. He was on magazine covers and chased by paparazzi, he told [NPR's Juana Summers](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/02/nx-s1-5788798/josh-hartnett-reflects-on-verity-and-living-a-much-less-hollywood-existence) in October 2026, "and I didn't want to feel like I wasn't in any control of my own life."

He went home to Minnesota. In 2004, promoting _Wicker Park_, he told [Newcastle's Evening Chronicle](https://www.chroniclelive.co.uk/whats-on/film-tv/hell-stay-out-of-limelight-1630334) that he had taken "15 months out of the business." (Later accounts say 18.) "I was like a lot of people in their 20s, wondering, 'Who am I? What do I want to be when I grow up?'" He had considered quitting for school or painting: "if I had the means I might just hermit myself away and do that for a while."

Twenty years on, his explanations gather around one idea. Other people were defining him before he could define himself. He told NPR:

> "I felt like I hadn't defined myself yet. And so there were lots of people that were kind of putting their definition of me out there into the world, and that was affecting my own idea of who I am."

He gave the same account to [Hollywood Authentic](https://hollywoodauthentic.com/josh-hartnett) in 2024, describing "a lot of intrusion into my life at that point" and adding, "I didn't know how to protect myself exactly at that point because I was young." Looking back, he said, "it was self-protective, but it was also much more positive than that."

A 2014 interview with Details, [as later quoted by PopCulture.com](https://popculture.com/celebrity/news/josh-hartnett-says-he-walked-away-from-hollywood-for-mental-health-reasons-in-rare-interview/), put the peak years more bleakly: "I was alone. I didn't trust anyone." The Details original could not be checked for this analysis, so treat the wording as reported.

On NPR, Summers read him a swooning line from Cooke's 2002 profile. Hartnett said he now has "a pretty clear, clearly defined life" and that "I am less afraid of other people's opinions." At the time, he added, "I would have curled up into a ball."

## The fear that won

Superman came next. Asked about it at the 2003 round table, he described another self-audit: "I asked myself, 'Am I going to feel fulfilled doing this?' The answer was no." His reasons have shifted with the years. In 2003 it was fulfillment and "putting on the tights just didn't make sense." In 2021 he told [MR PORTER](https://www.mrporter.com/en-gb/journal/fashion/actor-josh-hartnett-interview-1597802) that "Superman was a risk." In 2024 he told [the Observer's Stuart McGurk](https://www.theguardian.com/film/article/2024/jul/28/i-didnt-want-to-be-swallowed-up-actor-josh-hartnett-on-swapping-hollywood-for-hampshire), "I just didn't want my life to be swallowed up by my work."

Batman is often added to the list, but he never turned it down. [Christopher Nolan](/personality-analysis/christopher-nolan) said in 2023, [as reported by CNN](https://ktvz.com/?p=1181915), that "It never got that far." Nolan had "an initial conversation" with him, and Hartnett "had read my brother's script for 'The Prestige' at the time and was more interested in getting involved with that."

Hartnett's 2015 version: "Warner Brothers wanted me to talk about Batman. I wanted to talk about The Prestige." The Prestige role went to [Christian Bale](/personality-analysis/christian-bale), whom Nolan had cast as Batman.

In the same 2015 clarification he named the motive himself:

> "because of my fear of the concept of being one thing in my career and one thing only, I decided I would just stay away from that one aspect."

He fought the studios on smaller things too. He told Gilbey he had "a sort of antagonistic relationship" with Dimension, the Weinstein brothers' horror label behind _Halloween H20_ and _The Faculty_, because his contract for those films guaranteed him a part in "like, five more or something."

He told MR PORTER about a time "I wanted my character to chew gum in a scene. It was run up so many flagpoles and it was the subject of conversation for days and in the end, they decided just no."

The cost was real. "They looked at me as someone who had bitten the hand that fed me," he told Gilbey, and he said he had "burned my bridges at the studios because I wasn't participating." In 2026 he told [The Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/tv/tv-features/josh-hartnett-below-interview-widows-bay-car-crash-season-2-1236730830/), "I had to fight really hard to get out of that box when I was younger."

Read in order, the choices suggest that once _Pearl Harbor_ had proved a fear right, he stopped overruling it. The fear was specific: being fixed in place by other people's idea of him.

## Choosing people instead of parts

The later Hartnett has a different answer to the same fear. When Nolan called him two decades later for _Oppenheimer_, in which he played the physicist Ernest Lawrence, "the implication was: 'I'd better take it.' You only get so many opportunities with Chris Nolan." He told McGurk what he had learned:

> "I recognise the missed opportunity to work with a guy like Chris. And I've figured out that as much as you're worried about curating your career to things you're interested in, I don't believe that's the most important thing any more. It's about finding people who you really trust."

McGurk called it "his new rule: work with people you trust." The rule has a long history. Hartnett told [The Film Stage](https://thefilmstage.com/josh-hartnett-on-verity-and-working-with-warren-beatty-oliver-stone-and-sofia-coppola/) in 2026 that _Lucky Number Slevin_ (2006) "is one of my favorite films I've ever been a part of, because that was all friends." In 2021 he said that while people "thought I was crazy for not chasing that brass ring a little harder," he "felt completely comfortable being with the people I knew liked me before I was making films."

He lives in the Hampshire countryside with his wife, the English actor Tamsin Egerton, whom he married in November 2021, and their four children. In the village, he told McGurk, "nobody cares" about his career. He returned to studio work, he told his _O_ co-star Julia Stiles for [Interview](https://www.interviewmagazine.com/film/josh-hartnett-tells-julia-stiles-how-he-escaped-the-hollywood-trap) in 2024, because "we had a couple of kids, so I wanted to establish something that was a little bit more solid."

The fear has not gone. It now gets handled through people and through a say in the outcome.

Success, he told [Postmedia](https://ca.news.yahoo.com/why-josh-hartnett-couldn-t-153012348.html) in 2026, means approaching projects "with an openness and have the knowledge that it will be released in a good way." Being an executive producer on _Below_ was "really relieving," he told [SHARP](https://sharpmagazine.com/2026/08/25/josh-hartnett-interview-2026-below/), because on most jobs the studio decides what happens to your work after you wrap. "That's terrifying because they have complete control over the work you've done."

With the younger cast, he told [CBC's Q](https://www.cbc.ca/listen/cbc-podcasts/q-with-tom-power) in 2026, he "took on the dad role," wanting to make sure they came out of the shoot "fully intact as a family."

Under a director he admires, the old insecurity still shows. Describing a loosely autobiographical Oliver Stone film, still unreleased in October 2026, he told Q that during one scene Stone complimented everyone except him. Hartnett wanted badly to please the director and pushed his performance further and further. Stone got what he needed, Hartnett said, "by playing off my own insecurity and not giving me anything."

When Shyamalan sent him the _Trap_ script, his first reaction, he told [/Film](https://slashfilm.com/1632836/m-night-shyamalan-trap-has-different-twist-you-wont-see-coming-exclusive-interview), was "'Oh my God, if this goes wrong, it's all on me.'" He laughed, then said: "I love challenges."

## Why not Type 9?

The case for Nine is strong, and some of it comes from his own mouth. In the same 2002 interview as the "turn right" line, he told Cooke:

> "I just kind go along for the ride. You need some people in the world who are determined to do one thing in life, to make that thing their goal. But luckily, because you have all those other people being pillars, holding up the world, someone like me can just wander through."

As he left for lunch, he added that he wasn't interested in fighting the publicity machine. "That's not my fight."

His recent life sounds like a Nine's too. Of the English countryside: "Honestly, I've become really fond of being here: the quiet, the still; all of it." Of family life with four children: "In a way, less is happening. But more of the important stuff is happening."

He traces his modest ambition to his father, a musician turned building manager who owned his own company: "My dad was not someone who valued achievement in that way." And he describes his studio battles as non-participation, which is how a Nine resists: "I wasn't doing it to be recalcitrant or a rebel."

Even the _Pearl Harbor_ answer opens with a Nine's wish that things stay as they are.

What separates the two types is how they handle pressure. A Nine keeps the peace inside. Under strain a Nine tends to go numb, let things slide or quietly dig in, and explains hard choices by what would disturb them least. A Six scans for the threat, tests whoever is in charge and recruits allies, and explains hard choices through fear and trust.

On that test Hartnett's record leans Six. He names his fear and argues with it, as in the _Pearl Harbor_ question. He tests institutions and people, from the dean's office to Malarek. He goes back to fix his own words when he worries they will be misread, as with the Weinstein call. And in the same 2002 breath as "That's not my fight," he said, "There are other things I want to fight for."

The 2002 drift line remains unexplained. At 23, months before stepping back, he described himself much as a Nine would. A Six reading has to treat that as a young man's modesty or a pose for a journalist, and it might be neither. That is why the confidence stays at medium.

Two other types have a claim. [Type 4](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-4) fits the Huck Finn self-image, the painter and his joke to Stiles, "But I like being the outlier." Yet he calls talking about his own career "self-indulgent," and on _Below_ he asked that his character not be "constantly talking about his trauma."

[Type 7](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-7) fits the thrill-seeking. He told Hollywood Authentic, "I used to track-race Porsche cars all the time," and Shyamalan recalled Hartnett telling him that "danger is what really drives him," [as TheWrap reported](https://thewrap.com/m-night-shyamalan-why-he-cast-josh-hartnett-trap). Yet the life he has built narrows his options. His UK marriage visa limits him to about 180 days a year of work abroad, roughly one film, which, McGurk wrote, "also suits him fine."

## What the type doesn't explain

Some of Hartnett's caution has plain causes. Asked in 2024 whether he could trace his step back from fame to one moment, he told McGurk it wasn't so simple, then said: "People's attention to me at the time was borderline unhealthy." Then: "Well, look, I don't want to give this a lot of weight." He described people stalking him and, when he was 27, "a guy showed up at one of my premieres with a gun, claiming to be my father."

To Stiles he recalled that in those years "it was then the press's mission to tear you apart." By April 2002 he had also, he said, "worked for four years straight."

Frightening years can teach anyone to be vigilant. The type reading cannot rule out that the vigilance was learned rather than built in.

The fear words have also softened into idiom. In 2026 he told The Film Stage that the week _Verity_ and _Below_ came out felt "simultaneously terrifying" and joked, "this might be the last time we speak." The anxiety he names directly is narrow. At the 2018 British Independent Film Awards he said, [on the red carpet](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZQoGIUaFeug), "I get a lot of anxiety about these sort of events because I have to go up on stage and read."

Other things fit no tidy pattern. The racing streak would need a counterphobic Six or a Seven wing to explain it, and neither is demonstrated. He relocates easily; moving to England, he said, "didn't feel unnatural." And his deepest value may simply be his father's. "Work is never going to fulfill you in the way the family does," he told Q in 2026, describing what he learned at home.

What does hold across 24 years is the habit behind the _Pearl Harbor_ question. He finds the danger first, says so out loud and then decides what to do about it. In his twenties the answer was distance. In his forties it is a short list of people he trusts.
