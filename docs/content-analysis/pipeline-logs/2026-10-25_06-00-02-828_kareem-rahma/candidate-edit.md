In 2007, Kareem Rahma's father died of lung cancer, about six months after he got sick. Rahma was 21 and a student at the University of Minnesota.

He went home for the funeral and slept at his parents' house for one night. On Monday he was back at school.

"It was obviously sad and difficult," he told Anna Sale on [Death, Sex & Money](https://www.wnyc.org/story/death-sex--money--how-the-subway-takes-guy-became-his-own-nepo-baby/) in April 2025. Of how he got through it, he said, "for me, it was like all about motion and momentum and moving forward." He did not want, he said, "to let anything slow me down."

Seventeen years later, in September 2024, with a baby daughter and two hit shows, he wrote in his newsletter that he needed a break, "but I don't know how to take breaks because I love work." Then:

> "I am addicted to momentum. I used to tell my therapist that the best part of traveling was the airplane ride. I have a habit of arriving somewhere and then looking forward to going home."

([Another New Thing, September 18, 2024](https://kareemrahma.substack.com/p/baby-i-could-never-win))

He has not linked that line to the funeral in anything reviewed here. The pairing, and the test of it, belong to this profile.

Rahma is the creator and host of SubwayTakes, the talk show he started with Andrew Kuo in 2023. A guest on a New York subway car offers one opinion, and Rahma agrees or disagrees a hundred percent, with no in-between ([The New Yorker, August 24, 2025](https://www.newyorker.com/culture/the-new-yorker-interview/the-creator-of-subway-takes-one-hundred-per-cent-disagrees)). In July 2026 the show was nominated for an Emmy for Outstanding Short Form Comedy, Drama or Variety Series ([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/creative-content/tv-shows/subway-takes-emmy-nomination-kareem-rahma-andrew-kuo-intervew/)). That September it lost to *Colbert Before Air* ([The Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/tv/tv-news/2026-creative-arts-emmys-winners-list-night-one-1236683725/)).

Before SubwayTakes came Keep the Meter Running, the 2022 series in which he hails a New York cab, asks the driver to take him to their favorite place and pays whatever the meter says.

He is also the man who, at 33, made a plan to get famous and then carried it out. "About five years ago, I made a plan to get famous, and I stuck to it," he wrote in August 2024 ([Another New Thing](https://kareemrahma.substack.com/p/i-always-forget-that-im-old)).

So which is he? A striver who learned to sell fun, or a fun-seeker who learned to strive? His own words lean, narrowly, toward the second. That Monday is where both answers start.

## What is Kareem Rahma's personality type?

Kareem Rahma most likely fits Enneagram Type 7, the Enthusiast, at medium confidence. Sevens are fear types whose fear is of being trapped or deprived. Rahma ranks fun "number one" when deciding what to say yes to, and he used to tell his therapist the best part of traveling was the airplane ride. Type 3 is a close rival.

The Enneagram sorts its nine types by the hard emotion at their center. A [Seven](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-7) sits with fear and keeps moving toward the next option so the bad feeling never catches up. A [Three](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-3), the Achiever, sits with shame, the fear of being worth little unless seen succeeding. So the cleanest test is which feeling runs Rahma's choices: dread of a trapped life, or the need to be seen as valuable. The Type 3 section runs it.

## "That's great. Keep going."

Rahma was born in Cairo in 1986 and came to Minnesota around 1989, when he was 3 or 4 ([Brooklyn Magazine, 2023](https://www.bkmag.com/2023/01/02/kareem-rahma/)). His father, Sayed, who went by "Sam," tried many trades, from gas station owner to vegetable farmer. For several years when Kareem was small, he drove a cab.

In Rahma's memory, as The New York Times put it in 2024, his father was "always one step away from his big break." Ramy Youssef, a comedian also raised by Egyptian immigrants, had a name for the family's mix of optimism and hustle: "It's called being Egyptian." ([The New York Times, November 4, 2024](https://www.nytimes.com/2024/11/04/arts/kareem-rahma-subway-takes.html)) Culture explains some of what follows before any type does.

Rahma describes the relationship as one long debate, increasingly over "the freedom to operate and to make decisions on my own." "It wasn't like a bad relationship," he told Sale. "He was a good dad and I loved him and he loved me." But his father was "really controlling" and "loved me too much as the eldest son," and "he also just wanted me to succeed." The praise, he says, never came.

> "I think in our relationship, there was never a moment where he was like, that's great. Good job. It was always like, that's great. Keep going."

Then 2007. He knew his father had six months to live, and he kept his routine. "I was deflecting the reality of what was happening," he told [Rolling Stone](https://www.rollingstone.com/culture/culture-features/subway-takes-kareem-rahma-tiktok-podcast-feature-1235292805/) in March 2025. "I didn't move home. I just continued coming home on Saturdays." Whichever type is right, that pattern had a cost, and he names it: "now as an adult, I'm regretful that I didn't spend that time."

He gives reasons, and they matter. He worried that if he took a break he would never go back, "because I wasn't really interested in college." He also "knew my dad would want me to finish." His father had told him many times to "just keep going," which Rahma glosses as "one up yourself" or "don't stop." "And so that's what I did," he told Sale. "Like that's how I coped." He piled on credits, throwing himself less into the schoolwork ("I wasn't a good student") than into "the experience of school," "in order to finish."

After the death he stayed in Minnesota to help his mother and two younger siblings, and he did not leave for New York until 2012, the Times reported. That was duty, not escape.

Read straight, 2007 is shared ground. A Seven keeps moving so grief can't catch up; a Three shelves the feeling to keep performing; a dutiful son finishes the degree he promised. His account holds all three, so the Monday counts for neither type.

Before comedy, he wrote poems. One, set in an airport full of people "waiting for the departed to arrive," includes these lines: "A moment of realization buzzes near my face / and I swat it away like a fly." Then, set apart: "I hate the truth." ([Another New Thing, August 28, 2024](https://kareemrahma.substack.com/p/before-i-was-a-comedian-i-was-a-poet))

A poem is not testimony about 2007. It is still the image he picked.

In 2020 it happened again. His brother was in the hospital, near death, and COVID kept Rahma from visiting. His first marriage was ending. (He has since remarried.) Back in Minnesota when George Floyd was murdered, he went to the protests. "For the next three months, I lost myself to the cause," he told Maury Povich in [Interview](https://www.interviewmagazine.com/culture/kareem-rahma-maury-povich-keep-the-meter-running) in May 2026. It was, he told the New Yorker, "a way of coping with grief."

The protests were in his hometown, so conviction explains them as well as grief does. Twice, by his own account, he met grief with motion. What the motion protected is the open question.

## The plan to get famous

His accounts of when he chose comedy don't line up. In 2023 he traced it to the 2018 flop of his Museum of Pizza and the poetry readings after it; in 2025 and 2026, to the summer of 2020, "a total midlife enlightenment." He was 33 from July 2019 to July 2020, so both fit "at 33." Keep the Meter Running followed in 2022, SubwayTakes in 2023. The record can't say which trigger came first, so the type argument leans on neither.

He started working at 14 and, by his account, never really stopped. He told Rolling Stone what drove him.

> "Interests, likes, none of that was part of the equation. It was simply, I want to be rich. I need money. I don't want to be in poverty."

In 2012 he moved to New York to "just get rich," he told the New Yorker, worked at Vice and helped launch video at The New York Times, then started his own company, the Nameless Network.

Ask what the money was for, and the answer shifts. A shared East Village apartment made him feel like "the most successful person in the world," he told Brooklyn Magazine in 2023. Then: "I want to go upstate. I want to go skiing. I don't want to say no to fun."

His 2018 Museum of Pizza had two goals, he said: "I wanted to go down in New York history as a legend," and to make money, which he lost. It "didn't achieve either goal." He started posting poems on Instagram and doing readings, and when audiences laughed, he thought, "Oh, that is the feeling that I desire." Laughter is applause and pleasure at once, so that origin story fits either type.

Asked by the New Yorker in 2025 how he chose comedy, he recalled asking himself, "Do I want to leave a legacy?" and "what would I want to do that I think I would be good at?" His answer: "I've always been a magnet. People seem to gravitate toward me."

Here the two readings collide. Fame is a Three's prize. But listen to why he wanted it. In a 2025 Forbes interview he recalled telling friends, "I'm going to become famous," and calling someone to say, "I'm going to become talent," because "I didn't want to send emails every day." Then came the reason.

> "What I realized is that talent has a very fun job."

The perks he listed were experiences: "You get to do new things. You get to experience new things." ([Forbes, March 20, 2025](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5zntIFBHHpY)) Of business, he told [Mpls.St.Paul Magazine](https://mspmag.com/arts-and-culture/in-conversation-with-kareem-rahma/) in 2025, "I dreaded the idea of doing it for another 40 years. I was just like, I think I'll die." On Death, Sex & Money he pictured himself emailing for a living until he was 80.

## Number one is fun

On Death, Sex & Money, Sale suggested his work had a through line: a fight with authority for the right to be playful. He agreed and went further.

> "Fun is literally a priority. It is the number one thing on my list when it comes to choosing a job or a project or something that I want to work on."

When he asks himself whether to say yes or no to something, he added, "number one is fun."

That could be one good quote handed to him by a friendly host. It keeps coming back in other rooms, though. "I'm literally trying to entertain myself. I want to see novel ideas," he told Mpls.St.Paul Magazine in March 2025. In a March 2026 newsletter he described both of his hits as "Something to entertain myself" ([Another New Thing, March 17, 2026](https://kareemrahma.substack.com/p/do-not-launch-a-new-short-form-unscripted)).

His accounts of the same decision change with the room. Two months after that newsletter, he told Povich that SubwayTakes began because Keep the Meter Running cost too much: "Not because I was like, 'Oh, this is a novel idea.' It was out of need." In June 2026 he recalled telling his wife, "Here I go again, doing another thing on the internet, when all I want to do is make TV and films in Hollywood." She encouraged him ([Awards Radar](https://awardsradar.com/2026/06/15/interview-subway-takes-kareem-rahma/)). Creators are rewarded for saying they do it for fun, and he concedes, "I do want people to like it."

Keep the Meter Running is where the fun rule meets money, and the record cuts both ways. A production company co-produced and funded the first season ([Brooklyn Magazine](https://www.bkmag.com/2023/01/02/kareem-rahma/)). After that, he told Povich, an episode cost him about $2,000 of his own money; the Times reported in 2024 that he was making it "at a loss." He calls it "the pride and joy of my life, the thing that I have the most fun with." But when it got "too expensive," he moved to a cheaper format: "The subway is a free set." And the show bought him his first real press. Its losses can't separate fun from recognition.

By March 2025 he had paused the short-form version and was "assembling it for its transition from the internet to TV" with Ramy Youssef, he told Mpls.St.Paul, though he chafed that "I don't have full control over the outcome." In May 2026 it came back as a long-form YouTube series. His reason: "Man, I miss driving around with these cabbies and going on these crazy adventures."

Even arguing is play. He can argue "about something that I don't care about for multiple hours simply because it's fun," he told [The Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/tv/tv-features/kareem-rahma-interview-subway-takes-emmys-1236609132/) in 2026.

That matters for the typing. The show's all-or-nothing verdicts can make him look like an Eight, the type that meets the world head-on to stay in control. But Rahma argues about things he says he doesn't care about, for the pleasure of it, and he admits needing validation, a dependence Eights rarely confess.

When a cabbie named Vinny took him to Buffalo Wild Wings for an early episode, he recalled, "my initial reaction was like, 'Oh, this sucks.' And then it was literally half a second where I was like, 'Actually, this is going to be fucking hilarious and amazing.'" That half second looks like the Seven's reframe in miniature, though turning a dull stop into a bit is also a host's job, and he brings what he calls "years of formal improv and comedy experience."

## Why not Type 3?

The case for Type 3 is strong, and most of it comes from Rahma himself.

At school he was bullied, he told Povich, and "punched in the face for not speaking English." His response: "I need to learn how to be a person that people like." That, he said, is "where my initial sense of humor came from, is to avoid getting punched in the face." Growing up, he told Sale, "I was trying to be a chameleon and to be not the center of attention, but accepted into the centers of attention." That is weak evidence about an adult, but the adult record keeps the theme.

"You want someone to love you, or a lot of people to love you," he told Povich of his early thirties. "I was like, 'I'm destined for greater things. I should stand out.'"

About a year in, he agonized over whether he could put "comedian" in his Instagram bio, "trying to figure out my identity as quickly as possible and become that new identity," he told Brooklyn Magazine. To Rolling Stone he named his nightmare: being "a 33-year-old divorced guy and introducing myself to women as an aspiring comedian. That's the worst."

He keeps score, posting about seven times a week in April 2025 "because I want to hit a million" followers. When the first Keep the Meter Running episode went up, he told Forbes, he turned his phone off: "I can't deal with another failure."

Then the strongest piece. Keep the Meter Running drew press in 2022, including Vanity Fair, and he told Forbes what that did.

> "I think that was the first time in my life that I felt validated and recognized as a comedian."

A lot of people would say you don't need validation from anyone, he said. "Personally, I do." He "felt really whole," and in terms of validation, "the press is still more important" than money. In 2026 he tied it to his father, who "never really gave me a pat on the back when I definitely deserved a pat on the back. So anytime there was external validation, I would feel more motivated to keep going."

That is about as Three as a sentence gets, and it is the main reason this read stays at medium confidence.

Now the center test. The shame side is loudest when he is becoming someone new: the bullied boy, the would-be comedian who needed the press, the resolve he described to Povich, "I'm not going to be embarrassed." It persists in how he keeps score. The fear side runs through his whole adult account and has one shape, a trapped, smaller life: poverty as a teenager, emails until 80, 40 more years of business. Even his money caution serves the leap: he kept consulting clients while starting comedy ("I became my own rich parent," he said). A trapped life, not a worthless one, is the Seven's fear. That leans Seven without settling it.

There is a timing problem too. Nearly every fun-first line dates from 2023 or later, after a rebrand meant to "become that new identity," in an industry that rewards a fun-first voice. Before about 28, by his account, he was fitting in and making money: "I certainly wasn't following my dreams and my desires," he told Sale. The older Seven-shaped evidence is thin: money for skiing, and a move from Vice to the Times for "experiencing all of the things that I can experience," both recalled in 2023.

So why Seven? Two things keep it narrowly ahead. First, the shape of that fear. Explaining why he left business, he described escape from confinement to Forbes, Mpls.St.Paul and Death, Sex & Money, and named experiences as the perks. The goal looks like a Three's. The reason he gave in those three interviews is a Seven's; the one he gave the New Yorker, a legacy and being good at something, is a Three's.

Second, he names the ride, not the arrival. The work half of "addicted to momentum" fits a Three as well. The rest doesn't: the airplane ride as the best part, arriving and then looking forward to going home, and a year later, "I always felt more comfortable being in transit." Arrival does land sometimes ("I finally have arrived," he told [Vanity Fair](https://www.vanityfair.com/hollywood/2022/12/tiktok-show-keep-the-meter-running-interview) in 2022), so this is a lean too.

Two other types trail on motive. The chameleon years suggest Type 9, which merges with others to keep the peace, but a Nine rarely drafts a five-year plan to be famous. Type 6 fits the money hedges, but his make the leap possible rather than replacing it.

A Seven can crave applause, and the validation quotes are what the Seven read absorbs least comfortably. The test going forward is simple. If he starts choosing recognition over fun when the two split, the case for Three gets stronger. No clean split has happened yet. The likeliest: if a TV, film or streaming deal arrives, does he talk about it as arrival, or as one more adventure, and does the cab show he calls his most fun survive it?

## What the type leaves out

Rahma has said he was diagnosed with ADHD as an adult, around 30. His brain, he told Sale, "is designed to jump from thing to thing." Novelty-seeking and restlessness are where ADHD and the Seven pattern overlap, and this public record can't pull them apart. The type argument rests instead on his stated reasons at choice points and his preference for the ride over the arrival. The type describes a pattern of motives, not a diagnosis or a cause.

The Harris episode complicates both types. In August 2024 he was invited to interview Kamala Harris and Tim Walz, which the Times called "the status symbol of the season." He is Muslim, says the campaign refused his request to raise Gaza, and accepted its terms anyway. At the taping, by his account, Harris offered "Bacon is a spice" instead of the take he had been told to expect; two senior campaign officials told the Times the topic had been raised in advance, which Rahma and his manager dispute. The campaign apologized and proposed a reshoot. Rahma, who had been "apprehensive about potential criticism from other Muslims," the Times reported, published Walz ("I'm not gonna pass up a good story," he told [the Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/ng-interactive/2024/nov/13/kareem-rahma-interview-subway-takes)) and let Harris go; the New Yorker later called that mutual. "I never wanted to be a politics person," he said in November 2024, though Zohran Mamdani has appeared on SubwayTakes twice, most recently in June 2025. Keeping the good story and dropping the half that risked his standing with Muslim viewers fits a Three managing image and a Seven escaping a bind equally well.

Finally, almost everything here comes from Rahma. In the interviews reviewed for this profile, through October 2026, his co-creator Andrew Kuo talks about the show, not about him, and his wife's view reaches us only through him. The best outside read is from CULTURED's Annie Hamilton, who found him open for an hour, then left with "the sense that what I had perceived as a vulnerable exchange was actually no sweat off his back." Rahma has his own image for the part he keeps. "The sunglasses are a disguise," he told Rolling Stone. "There's a lot of emotions in the eyes. So I'm preserving a piece of myself."

## On the way

In 2024 Rahma told the Guardian he wanted to be at the Emmys, "Hopefully for something I've made." By June 2026, deep in a six-month Emmy campaign that grew out of his joke sign-off to famous guests ("I'll see you at the Emmys"), he described the Hollywood goal differently: "When I started, my goal was to make the leap. Now, it's just another opportunity to make something interesting." ([Awards Radar, June 15, 2026](https://awardsradar.com/2026/06/15/interview-subway-takes-kareem-rahma/))

By July he was back in the cabs, deciding in one episode to produce a longtime driver's one-man play "for a sold-out audience... the very next night" ([Another New Thing, July 16, 2026](https://kareemrahma.substack.com/p/this-taxi-ride-cost-4100-but-it-was)).

A year and a half earlier, in March 2025, he told Rolling Stone about regretting his father's last months. Then, swiping through picture after picture of his daughter, he tried to describe something new in himself. The man who kept moving through 2007 was describing, with his own child, not being in a hurry. What came after doesn't settle whether it lasted.

> "I'm not in a hurry to go to the next spot, which may be the first time in my life. I always felt more comfortable being in transit, not literally, but also literally. I love it. It's a magical feeling to be on the way."
