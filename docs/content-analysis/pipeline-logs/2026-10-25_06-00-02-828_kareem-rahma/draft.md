In 2007, Kareem Rahma's father died of lung cancer, about six months after he got sick. Rahma was 21 and a student at the University of Minnesota.

He went home for the funeral and slept at his parents' house for one night. On Monday he was back at school.

Looking back on it in April 2025, he told Anna Sale on [Death, Sex & Money](https://www.wnyc.org/story/death-sex--money--how-the-subway-takes-guy-became-his-own-nepo-baby/) that "for me, it was like all about motion and momentum and moving forward." He did not want, he said, "to let anything slow me down."

Seventeen years after the funeral, with a baby daughter and two hit shows, he wrote this in his newsletter:

> "I am addicted to momentum. I used to tell my therapist that the best part of traveling was the airplane ride. I have a habit of arriving somewhere and then looking forward to going home."

([Another New Thing, September 18, 2024](https://kareemrahma.substack.com/p/baby-i-could-never-win))

Rahma is the creator and host of SubwayTakes, the talk show he started with Andrew Kuo in 2023. A guest on a New York subway car offers one opinion, and Rahma agrees or disagrees a hundred percent, with no in-between ([The New Yorker, August 24, 2025](https://www.newyorker.com/culture/the-new-yorker-interview/the-creator-of-subway-takes-one-hundred-per-cent-disagrees)). In July 2026 the show was nominated for an Emmy for Outstanding Short Form Comedy, Drama or Variety Series ([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/creative-content/tv-shows/subway-takes-emmy-nomination-kareem-rahma-andrew-kuo-intervew/)). That September it lost to *Colbert Before Air* ([The Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/tv/tv-news/2026-creative-arts-emmys-winners-list-night-one-1236683725/)). Rolling Stone ranked him No. 11 among 2026's most influential creators ([Rolling Stone](https://www.rollingstone.com/culture/culture-lists/top-social-media-influencers-creators-2026-1235605189/)).

Before SubwayTakes came Keep the Meter Running, the 2022 series in which he hails a New York cab, asks the driver to take him to their favorite place and pays whatever the meter says.

He is also the man who, at 33, made a plan to get famous and then carried it out. "About five years ago, I made a plan to get famous, and I stuck to it," he wrote in August 2024 ([Another New Thing](https://kareemrahma.substack.com/p/i-always-forget-that-im-old)).

So which is he? A striver who learned to sell fun, or a fun-seeker who learned to strive? His own words point mostly to the second, and the reason starts with that Monday.

## What is Kareem Rahma's personality type?

Kareem Rahma's personality type is Enneagram Type 7, the Enthusiast. Sevens are fear types who push the fear down and keep moving. Rahma calls fun the "number one" test for saying yes, and when grief has hit him, he has sped up instead of stopping.

The Enneagram groups its nine types by the hard emotion at their center: anger, shame or fear. [Type 7](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-7) sits with fear, alongside Types 5 and 6, the three thinking types. The thinking part shows quickly in Rahma. Rolling Stone's CT Jones found him hard to interview because he kept turning her questions back on her, and he told her why: "I'm always absorbing information to see how I can apply it to my life."

Each of the three handles fear differently. A Six uses it and scans for what could go wrong. A Five barely notices it. A Seven pushes it down. Not right now.

The pattern runs in a few plain steps. Something painful or confining shows up: a loss, a dull job, a single path. The Seven tells the feeling to wait. They go find the next thing, the fun version, another option. Then they keep moving, because stopping is when the feeling catches up. The usual cost is whatever got skipped along the way.

Rahma has named his. Of the months when his father was dying, he told Rolling Stone in 2025, "now as an adult, I'm regretful that I didn't spend that time."

This read comes from his interviews, his newsletter and press coverage from 2022 to 2026, and it is held at medium confidence. The closest rival is [Type 3](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-3), the Achiever. Rahma gives that case real evidence, including press coverage that made him feel "really whole," and it gets its own section below.

## "That's great. Keep going."

Rahma was born in Cairo in 1986 and came to Minnesota around 1989, when he was 3 or 4 ([Brooklyn Magazine, 2023](https://www.bkmag.com/2023/01/02/kareem-rahma/)). His father, Sayed, went by "Sam" there. He had first come to the U.S. in 1969, and he worked at various times as a gas station owner, a truck driver, an exporter and a vegetable farmer. For several years when Kareem was small, he drove a cab.

In Rahma's memory, as The New York Times put it in 2024, his father was "always one step away from his big break." Ramy Youssef, a comedian also raised by Egyptian immigrants, had a name for the family's mix of optimism and hustle: "It's called being Egyptian." ([The New York Times, November 4, 2024](https://www.nytimes.com/2024/11/04/arts/kareem-rahma-subway-takes.html)) Culture explains some of what follows before any type does.

Rahma describes the relationship as one long debate. "He treated me like a man when I was like 11," he told Sale. As he got older, the arguments turned to "the freedom to operate and to make decisions on my own." They settled it with a deal: once Kareem got into college, "I could do whatever I want."

He calls his father "really controlling," a man who "loved me too much as the eldest son." The praise, he says, never came.

> "I think in our relationship, there was never a moment where he was like, that's great. Good job. It was always like, that's great. Keep going."

Then 2007. Rahma knew his father had six months to live, and he kept his routine. "I was deflecting the reality of what was happening," he told [Rolling Stone](https://www.rollingstone.com/culture/culture-features/subway-takes-kareem-rahma-tiktok-podcast-feature-1235292805/) in March 2025. "I didn't move home. I just continued coming home on Saturdays."

He gives reasons, and they matter. He worried that if he took a break he would never go back, "because I wasn't really interested in college." He also "knew my dad would want me to finish." His father had told him so, he said on Death, Sex & Money: "just keep going."

So he kept going. He piled on credits that last year. It was not about grades, he admits ("I wasn't a good student"); he threw himself into "the experience of school." Then came the funeral, the one night at home and the Monday.

After the death he stayed in Minnesota to help his mother and two younger siblings, and he did not leave for New York until 2012, the Times reported. That was duty, not escape.

Before comedy, he wrote poems. In August 2024 he republished one called "Three Hundred Miles To Empty," set in an airport full of people "waiting for the departed to arrive." It includes these lines: "A moment of realization buzzes near my face / and I swat it away like a fly." Then, set apart: "I hate the truth." ([Another New Thing, August 28, 2024](https://kareemrahma.substack.com/p/before-i-was-a-comedian-i-was-a-poet))

A poem is not testimony about 2007. It is still the image he picked.

The same move showed up in 2020. His brother was in the hospital, near death, and COVID kept Rahma from visiting. His marriage was ending. He was back home in Minnesota when George Floyd was murdered, and he went to the protests. "For the next three months, I lost myself to the cause," he told Maury Povich in [Interview](https://www.interviewmagazine.com/culture/kareem-rahma-maury-povich-keep-the-meter-running) in May 2026. He told the New Yorker in 2025 that he threw himself into the uprising "because it was a way of coping with grief."

He came out of that summer with what he calls a "midlife enlightenment" and a decision to become an entertainer. (In other interviews he dates the decision to 2019.)

The protests were in his hometown, so conviction explains them as well as grief does. What repeats across 2007 and 2020 is the reflex. When something hurts, Rahma speeds up.

## The plan to get famous

He started working at 14 and, by his account, never really stopped: McDonald's, busing tables at a St. Paul yacht club, telemarketing, flipping motorcycles in college. He told Rolling Stone what drove him.

> "Interests, likes, none of that was part of the equation. It was simply, I want to be rich. I need money. I don't want to be in poverty."

In 2012 he moved to New York. "I'm gonna go to New York and just get rich," he told the New Yorker, recalling the thinking. He worked at Vice and helped launch video at The New York Times, then started his own company, the Nameless Network.

Ask what the money was for, though, and the answer shifts. In 2023 he told Brooklyn Magazine that a shared East Village apartment had made him feel like "the most successful person in the world." Then he wanted more: "I want to go upstate. I want to go skiing. I don't want to say no to fun." So, he said, "I had to keep working as hard as possible."

The business years did not land. His 2018 Museum of Pizza in Brooklyn lost money and, by his account, "didn't achieve either goal." Around then he caught himself thinking, "I'm cosplaying as a CEO. I'm cosplaying as a businessman. I'm not even good at this."

Then came the plan. The Guardian's Sam Wolfson reported its core in 2024: "to work harder than anyone" and "rebrand myself from a civilian to a comedian." Rahma explained the hurry.

> "I knew I had to put 10 years of work into five years because I'd be competing with kids who were 23 who were way funnier and way better. But I had one thing they didn't: perspective."

([The Guardian, November 13, 2024](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/ng-interactive/2024/nov/13/kareem-rahma-interview-subway-takes))

By August 2024 he had a following, a short film that premiered at Tribeca, two viral series, a feature film and a full-time career. "I have checked off all the boxes on the plan," he wrote, though he still wouldn't call himself famous. A few lines later: "Nonetheless, I need a new plan."

Here the two readings collide. Fame is a Three's prize. But listen to why he wanted it. He told a Forbes interviewer in 2025 that he remembers calling someone to say, "I'm going to become talent," because "I didn't want to send emails every day." Then came the reason.

> "What I realized is that talent has a very fun job."

The perks he listed were experiences: "You get to do new things. You get to experience new things." ([Forbes, March 20, 2025](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5zntIFBHHpY))

Of business, he told [Mpls.St.Paul Magazine](https://mspmag.com/arts-and-culture/in-conversation-with-kareem-rahma/) in 2025, "I dreaded the idea of doing it for another 40 years. I was just like, I think I'll die."

## Number one is fun

On Death, Sex & Money, Sale suggested his work had a through line: a fight with authority for the right to be playful. He agreed and went further.

> "Fun is literally a priority. It is the number one thing on my list when it comes to choosing a job or a project or something that I want to work on."

When he asks himself whether to say yes or no to something, he added, "number one is fun."

That could be one good quote handed to him by a friendly host. It keeps coming back in other rooms, though. "I'm literally trying to entertain myself. I want to see novel ideas," he told Mpls.St.Paul Magazine in March 2025. In the Forbes interview that month he said "at the core of all of this is me entertaining Kareem." In a March 2026 newsletter he described both of his hits as "Something to entertain myself" ([Another New Thing, March 17, 2026](https://kareemrahma.substack.com/p/do-not-launch-a-new-short-form-unscripted)). In August 2026 he told Rolling Stone, "I'm really the person I'm trying to entertain."

There is a limit here. Creators are rewarded for saying they do it for fun, so the line could be polish. His own 2025 checklist, as he gave it to Rolling Stone, also asks whether the audience is entertained ("I hope so"), and he said plainly, "I do want people to like it."

What gives the line weight is what it costs him. Keep the Meter Running is the clearest case. He pays the full fare, and an episode once cost him about $2,000 out of pocket, he told Povich. The Times reported in 2024 that he was making it "at a loss." He told Mpls.St.Paul Magazine it is "the pride and joy of my life, the thing that I have the most fun with."

It has a private thread too. His father drove a cab, and Rahma has said he "always found myself gravitating toward older men as father figures."

When the show no longer fit short-form video, he shelved it instead of keeping it on TikTok. In May 2026 he brought it back as a long-form YouTube series. His reason: "Man, I miss driving around with these cabbies and going on these crazy adventures." Then: "There's a little more danger to it."

Other choices follow the same rule. At 36 he started a rock band, Tiny Gun. Why? "Because, first, I like it and I think it's fun, and second, I'm like, Holy shit, I can make music," he told [CULTURED](https://www.culturedmag.com/article/2025/04/29/kareem-rahma-subwaytakes-tiktok-2/) in 2025. (He also wanted something more lasting than viral clips, so it is not only a lark.)

Even arguing is play. "I can sit in the middle of an argument and have a conversation about something that I don't care about for multiple hours simply because it's fun," he told [The Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/tv/tv-features/kareem-rahma-interview-subway-takes-emmys-1236609132/) in 2026. His favorite night out, he told Sale, is "four people at a dinner table at a four hour dinner having debates."

That matters for the typing. An Eight argues to win. Rahma argues about things he says he doesn't care about, for the pleasure of it.

When a plan goes sideways, he flips it fast. On an early Keep the Meter Running shoot, a cabbie named Vinny took him to Buffalo Wild Wings. Rahma recalled that "my initial reaction was like, 'Oh, this sucks.' And then it was literally half a second where I was like, 'Actually, this is going to be fucking hilarious and amazing.'"

That half second is the Seven's reframe in miniature.

## Why not Type 3?

The case for Type 3 is strong, and most of it comes from Rahma himself.

Start with childhood. At school he was bullied, he told Povich, and "punched in the face for not speaking English." His conclusion was practical: "I need to learn how to be a person that people like." Growing up, he told Sale, "I was trying to be a chameleon and to be not the center of attention, but accepted into the centers of attention." Adapting to be accepted is a classic Three move.

Then there is the drive. Of his early thirties, he told Povich: "You want someone to love you, or a lot of people to love you. I was like, 'I'm destined for greater things. I should stand out.'" When he switched to comedy, he told the New Yorker, "I just felt like I had to prove that I was good, fast."

He cared how the new identity read. About a year in, he agonized over whether he could put "comedian" in his Instagram bio. "You shouldn't be aspiring. You have to be," he told Rolling Stone. Years earlier, when the Museum of Pizza sold tickets before he had a plan to run it, the only thing he could think about, he said, was "I have a good reputation. I have to pull this off no matter what."

He keeps score. In April 2025 he was posting about seven times a week to reach a million followers on Instagram and TikTok: "The reason I'm doing that is because I want to hit a million." He barred celebrity guests from SubwayTakes for its first 100 episodes because, he told CULTURED, "This show will make me the talent." And in 2026 his joke sign-off to famous guests ("I'll see you at the Emmys") became a six-month campaign backed by YouTube.

Then the strongest piece. Keep the Meter Running drew press in 2022, including Vanity Fair. He told Forbes what that did.

> "I think that was the first time in my life that I felt validated and recognized as a comedian."

A lot of people would say you don't need validation from anyone, he said. "Personally, I do." Later in the same answer: "I felt like I had completed the metamorphosis." And: "I felt really whole." In 2026 he tied it to his father, who "never really gave me a pat on the back when I definitely deserved a pat on the back. So anytime there was external validation, I would feel more motivated to keep going."

That is about as Three as a sentence gets, and it is the main reason this read stays at medium confidence.

So why Seven? Three things tip it.

First, when he explains why he wanted the Three's prizes, he names what they buy. Money meant skiing and never saying no to fun. Fame meant "a very fun job" where you get to do new things. The goal looks like a Three's. The reason he gives is a Seven's.

Second, under the worst pain of his life, what he protected was motion. He says he wasn't a good student, so it was not a record. He calls it momentum.

Third, a Three tends to edit failure out of the story. Rahma leaves it in. "I tried very, very hard to be a startup founder and to get rich. I failed at that. I was unhappily married. I failed at that," he wrote in 2024. When he started comedy, he told himself, "I'm not going to be embarrassed. I'm going to be cringe. People are going to make fun of me, and I don't care."

And the payoff he names is the ride, not the arrival.

The chameleon story could also suggest Type 9. Nines blend in to keep the peace, though, and Rahma's favorite evening is a four-hour argument.

A Seven can crave applause, and the validation quotes are what the Seven read absorbs least comfortably. The test going forward is simple. If he starts choosing recognition over fun when the two split, the case for Three gets stronger.

## What the type leaves out

Rahma has said he was diagnosed with ADHD as an adult, around 30. "I'm diagnosed now, but I think I've been undiagnosed my whole life," he told Sale. He handles stress well, he said, "because my brain is designed to jump from thing to thing." That explains a lot about how he works, but not why he names fun as his rule, or why he went back to school the Monday after a funeral. The type describes a pattern of motives, not a diagnosis or a cause.

He also does not see himself the way his audience does. "People tend to assume I'm unhinged and hyperactive. The truth is, I'm pretty mellow and low-key," he told CULTURED in 2025. Yet a year earlier he told the Guardian, while sick, "Usually I'm more hyperactive than this, make sure you say that," and the Times described him in "near-constant motion." His own line splits the difference: "The inside of my head is quiet but my body is always in motion."

He is careful with money in ways the impulsive Seven of stereotype is not. "I'm very strategic to this day about money," he told Sale. He kept consulting clients while he started comedy. "I became my own rich parent," he said. He bought the SubwayTakes camera kit on a zero-interest credit card and told THR, "If it doesn't work out, I can always sell this stuff."

The Harris episode fits neither type neatly. In 2024 Kamala Harris's campaign asked to appear on SubwayTakes, an invitation the Times called "the status symbol of the season." Rahma, who is Muslim, says the campaign refused his request to raise Gaza. He also says he was told Harris would argue against taking your shoes off on planes; at the taping she offered "Bacon is a spice," and he does not eat pork. Two senior campaign officials told the Times the bacon topic had been raised in advance, which Rahma and his manager dispute.

The campaign proposed a reshoot, and he decided not to go ahead (the New Yorker later described the decision as mutual). "I never wanted to be a politics person," he told the Times. "The more I think about it, the more I feel like I got lucky." ([The New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2024/11/04/arts/kareem-rahma-subway-takes.html)) His faith, he told Povich in May 2026, is "probably stronger than it's ever been."

Arrival is unsettled too. "I finally have arrived where I've always wanted to go," he told [Vanity Fair](https://www.vanityfair.com/hollywood/2022/12/tiktok-show-keep-the-meter-running-interview) in 2022. In 2026, with the plan done, he told Povich, "But see, the thing is, I don't feel successful." Either type can explain that.

Finally, almost everything here comes from Rahma. His co-creator, Andrew Kuo, talks publicly about the show, not about him. The best outside read is from CULTURED's Annie Hamilton, who found him open for an hour, then left with "the sense that what I had perceived as a vulnerable exchange was actually no sweat off his back." Rahma has his own image for the part he keeps. "The sunglasses are a disguise," he told Rolling Stone. "There's a lot of emotions in the eyes. So I'm preserving a piece of myself."

## On the way

In 2024 Rahma told the Guardian he wanted to be at the Emmys: "Hopefully for something I've made but if it's a plus-one that's fine." Two years later he had the nomination, and the loss.

By then the Hollywood goal had changed shape. He had nearly skipped SubwayTakes, he said, until his wife encouraged him. "When I started, my goal was to make the leap. Now, it's just another opportunity to make something interesting." ([Awards Radar, June 15, 2026](https://awardsradar.com/2026/06/15/interview-subway-takes-kareem-rahma/))

By summer he was back in the cabs. In one episode he decided to produce a longtime driver's one-man play "for a sold-out audience... the very next night" ([Another New Thing, July 16, 2026](https://kareemrahma.substack.com/p/this-taxi-ride-cost-4100-but-it-was)). The episode's title puts the ride at $4,100.
