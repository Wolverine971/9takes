The worst advice Dhar Mann says he ever got was to change his name.

He told the story on Jay Shetty's podcast in May 2026. Someone had told him to drop the name, "because at that point in my life, I had failed so many times that the only thing associated with my name was failure." He added, "for a long time, honestly, I did think about changing my name." ([On Purpose, May 20, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2UW5wnan1bA))

The record attached to that name was public: in December 2013 he pleaded no contest to five felony counts of defrauding a City of Oakland grant program.

He kept the name. In the same answer he gave his reason: "there's so many people out there just like me that feel like a failure," so he built a studio around second chances, and now "my name is associated with positivity and overcoming hardship."

That studio, Dhar Mann Studios, makes scripted moral fables performed by actors, with titles like "RICH Kid WON'T TIP Pizza Boy, He Lives to Regret It." Forbes ranked him No. 2 on its 2026 Top Creators list, with estimated earnings of $65 million. ([Forbes, June 24, 2026](https://forbes.com.au/news/leadership/forbes-top-creators-2026))

His face is another story. In the early videos he appeared at the end of each fable to deliver the moral. When he started focusing on YouTube, before the channel's 2021 breakout, he checked the analytics. He told Guy Raz's _How I Built This_ in 2024 what he found:

> "I was humbled really quickly when I saw our audience retention would tank as soon as I would come on to tell the moral. So I literally had to fire myself."

Raz summed it up: "Dhar Mann Studios doesn't actually need Dhar Mann." ([How I Built This, April 22, 2024](https://traffic.megaphone.fm/VMP8163864444.mp3))

So he kept the name and cut the face. Read together, both choices protect what an audience thinks of when it hears "Dhar Mann": the "positivity" he says he wanted the name to carry.

Mann was born in 1984 ([birthday post, May 29, 2021](https://web.archive.org/web/20210529192005/https://twitter.com/dharmann/status/1398720779538345993)) and grew up in and around Oakland, California, the son of Sikh immigrants from India whose taxi company, Friendly Cab, became one of the city's biggest. ([SF Chronicle, May 18, 2012](https://www.sfgate.com/crime/article/Oakland-pot-entrepreneur-charged-with-fraud-3567342.php)) He traces his urge to scale to "seeing my dad go from one taxi cab to my parents building their big taxi business." Plenty of immigrants' children share that drive, which is one reason ambition alone can't settle his type.

## What is Dhar Mann's personality type?

Dhar Mann's personality type is Enneagram Type 3, the Achiever. Threes are in the heart triad, where the core emotion is shame, and they cope by becoming what success looks like to the people watching. Mann's own account describes chasing love and worth by being seen to succeed, before YouTube and after it.

In a [Type 3](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-3), shame turns into a question asked of every room: what counts as winning here? They find the answer, turn themselves into it and keep score. When the picture and the facts start to separate, the pull is to rescue the picture first and settle the bill later, which is what Mann says he did with a Lamborghini in 2007.

In 2026 he told Shetty that professional belonging had come only recently:

> "As much as I could try to convince myself of that before, I would never have been able to until I reached a certain level of success objectively."

That was the professional side, he added; the belonging that matters more comes from being a father to his two daughters.

The read is held at medium confidence, because the main narrator of the Dhar Mann story is Dhar Mann, and redemption is what his studio sells. The closest rival is [Type 2](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-2), the Helper, which gets its own section below.

## The Lamborghini he wouldn't give up

His parents worked long hours building the cab company, and at school he was bullied for his turban until his mother had his hair cut in fourth grade. He grew up feeling "too Indian for the Americans and I was too American for the Indians." Then he found something that worked: when he paid for classmates' lunches, snacks and arcade games, "a lot of people wanted to be my friend."

> "I didn't realize this until my adult years, but I associated money with love since that's how my parents expressed their love to me since they were never around."

At that stage, he told Raz, he was "only concerned with how could I make as much money as possible," so he could be "as loved as possible."

At 21, running a mortgage business in Davis, California, he bought an orange Lamborghini. "So for the first time in my life, I saw what having all this attention felt like," he told Raz. Through high school, he said, "I felt invisible with no friends. And now I was being seen by everyone." Then: "Everyone wanted to be my friend at that point."

When the 2007 housing crash closed the mortgage business, he moved back to Oakland but would not give up the car, which "in my head was the key to all this attention I was getting."

> "So instead of giving up the car, I started an exotic car rental company. I learned some basic web design and put up my Lamborghini for rent. So I was able to keep my car and more importantly, the appearance that I was a lot more successful than I actually was."

That is the plainest statement in the record of what he was protecting, and it came from him. It is also a 2024 look back, and redemption stories reward a vivid "before." So the question is whether a reporter who met him at the time saw the same man.

## "Somewhat appealing to watch"

In the fall of 2010, Mother Jones reporter Josh Harkinson followed Mann, then 26, around weGrow, the hydroponics superstore he had opened in Oakland that January, as a crew filmed him for _Hempire_, a reality-show pilot he was costarring in with his business partner, Derek Peterson.

"I personally have always wanted a reality show 'cause I just think that my life is somewhat appealing to watch," Mann told him. ([Mother Jones, December 3, 2010](https://www.motherjones.com/politics/2010/12/wegrow-dhar-mann-derek-peterson/))

The show had a business logic too. "When your business plan depends on publicity, there is no upside to playing it safe," Harkinson wrote, and Peterson called the show "a hedge."

Hydroponics shops then pretended their customers grew tomatoes. Mother Jones described Mann's plan as "the first honest hydro store." When the PR firm handling weGrow's opening handed him a release that read "Urban gardening megastore opens by airport," he fired the firm and rewrote it: "Marijuana Superstore Opens in East Oakland."

In his 2024 telling, the tomato version "just felt a little hypocritical to me." Both can be true: the release was candid, and it was also the louder headline.

The louder headline cost him. Within hours of opening, Mother Jones reported, his wholesale supplier cut him off, and in his 2024 telling "my business was pretty much doomed from the start." By March 2011 the original store had closed and the partnership with Peterson had ended in lawsuits on both sides. ([Mother Jones, March 1, 2011](https://www.motherjones.com/crime-justice/2011/03/wegrow-joint-venture-goes-smoke/))

He showed off his connections too. He helped host a 2010 fundraiser for Jerry Brown and posted a photo of them drinking champagne. Michael Hunt, a former council aide who worked for weGrow in 2010, said Mann didn't necessarily get special treatment because of his parents' ties to city leaders. "But the manner in which (Mann) has attempted to highlight and capitalize on those relationships," Hunt said, "probably attracted as many people to him as were scared away because of any lingering gray area or questions of ethics." Mann did not respond to the outlet. ([Bay Citizen via NBC Bay Area, September 6, 2012](https://www.nbcbayarea.com/news/local/indicted-oakland-pot-entrepreneur-leaned-on-political-relationships/1927584/))

That is the record from eight years before his first video. No algorithm was paying him to be watched, though his business plan rewarded it. No business plan needed the line he volunteered about his life being worth watching. The wish to be watched was already there, and he said it out loud.

## The grant case

The trouble came from his property business. Through MannEdge Properties he renovated commercial buildings in parts of Oakland where the city reimbursed up to half the cost. One project prosecutors cited was a warehouse where he later opened a hydroponics store. In his 2024 account, he "always wanted to do things quickly," took on too many renovations at once and hit reimbursements that took a year or two.

> "I decided to take some shortcuts on my grant application. I changed the dates on my paperwork to make it seem like the projects had been completed and contractors had been paid sooner than they were in order to start the reimbursement process faster."

In that version the work was done and paid for, and only the dates were wrong. Prosecutors described more. In May 2012 they charged him with 13 felonies, including grand theft and forgery. An investigator's court filing said he paid contractors much less than he claimed and redeposited the cashier's checks he had shown the city, which paid him more than $44,000 on that paperwork. His lawyer conceded that he "took shortcuts" but called the charges overblown and said Mann's own spending exceeded the city's matching grants. ([SF Chronicle, May 18, 2012](https://www.sfgate.com/crime/article/Oakland-pot-entrepreneur-charged-with-fraud-3567342.php))

On December 10, 2013, he pleaded no contest to five counts, a conviction without an admission of the underlying facts. ([Mother Jones, December 11, 2013](https://www.motherjones.com/crime-justice/2013/12/marijuana-entrepreneur-dhar-mann-fraud-oakland/)) In January 2014 he was sentenced to five years' probation, a $10,000 fine and "full restitution" of $44,399.51, in line with what the investigator said the city had paid. ([SF Chronicle, February 1, 2014](https://www.sfgate.com/crime/article/Ganja-preneur-of-Oakland-sentenced-fined-5195154.php)) His dates-only account leaves that order unexplained. He told the Times in 2021 that the conviction was later expunged.

Asked whether the hit to his reputation might follow him for life, he said, "I definitely felt like a failure," and that he wished he could tell his younger self "how important it is to do things the right way and how much your reputation really matters."

## "Money and fame"

By his account, the next collapse began with a friend whose affiliate-marketing business paid him monthly returns. Around 2013 or 2014 he put in everything he had, then sold his rental cars to put in more. As of October 2026, no public record of the scheme was found, so it is told here as his account, and the man is not named.

The two of them moved to Los Angeles around 2014, the year his five-year probation began. "I realized in LA, you need two things, money and fame," Mann said. He decided "to do whatever it took to build a large following on Instagram," leased a house in the Hollywood Hills, a Ferrari and a Rolls-Royce, and found that "the flashier I was on Instagram, the faster my following would grow."

> "I've never done any hard drugs in my life, but I would say Instagram was more addictive for me than any drug out there for someone who valued social acceptance as much as I did."

"My life really felt like a movie," he told Raz. In 2021, the About page on his own website was headed "My Life is like a Movie." ([dharmann.com, archived December 2021](https://web.archive.org/web/20211220085945/https://www.dharmann.com/about/))

Then the money stopped. He says the friend's last payment bounced, the friend went silent, and the business turned out to be a Ponzi scheme. When relatives saw his posts, he says, he told them about the investment, and "my cousins, my brother, and even eventually my parents" put money in. They lost it too, and he says they "all invested based on me saying this was a good investment."

He was 30 and sleeping on a couch, with "less than $600" in the bank.

"For me, as a 30-year-old Indian man, I was looking at it as like my family is ashamed of me, my culture I'm letting down," he told Codie Sanchez in July 2026. ([BigDeal, July 29, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Jx1gRjdgyVs)) And to Shetty:

> "I had worries that on my tombstone people were going to say, 'Here is Dhar Mann, the biggest failure that has ever existed.'"

A felony plea and relatives' losses would weigh on anyone, and that sense of where a man should be at 30 is partly cultural. That is why the choices he made when things were going well carry more weight in this read than the fall.

## The package decides

He says the recovery began when he stopped blaming the economy, the government and the friend for his three failures, in real estate, marijuana and the investment, and saw that he "was the common denominator." Soon after, around 2015, an online makeup school he built with a popular makeup artist brought in six figures within 24 hours of going live.

"I decided that I would not be flashy. I would not spend money wastefully. I didn't upgrade my lifestyle even 1%," he said, until he had repaid the relatives. Those losses had followed his own pitch, so the repayment settled a debt as much as it broke the old pattern, and only he has described it.

Through those classes he met Laura Avila, who became his partner in the cosmetics company LiveGlam and later his wife. He credits her with his ethics: "I grew up where operating in a gray area was okay. Right? Like, if as long as you made money, it was okay." Laura, he said, "was so black and white about everything." Gray area was the phrase Hunt had used about him in 2012.

On May 29, 2018, his 34th birthday, he posted his first motivational video to Facebook, figuring "I'm going to get extra love." It never broke 100 views. The first comment came from a friend: "Who do you think you are? [Tony Robbins](/personality-analysis/tony-robbins)?"

About three months later, a sketch he had planned as his last, about a husband who takes his stay-at-home wife for granted, went viral. From there the studio became a system, and the system runs on packaging. He told Sanchez that ideas without a strong thumbnail die:

> "If it's not great packaging, then by default, it can't be a great story."

The stories follow a fixed rule, which he described to Raz: "in my videos, unlike in the real world, the good person always wins in the end. And the bad person always learns their lesson."

Every creator at his scale tests thumbnails, so none of this alone says much about his personality, which is why the older record, from the 2007 car to the 2014 Instagram, carries more weight. What the testing adds is the order of operations: in his studio, the package decides whether the story gets made.

The same caveat covers cutting his face, a call any operator reading retention data might make. What it does rule out is the simpler story, that he just wants to be looked at. A Three gives up the spotlight when the spotlight stops winning.

## Why not Type 2?

[Type 2](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-2) shares the heart triad and its core emotion of shame. Both types want love and worth; the difference is the currency. Twos earn it by being needed, noticing what people want before they ask. The Two case for Mann is real, and most of it comes from him.

The boy who bought classmates lunch was giving to be liked. The man anticipates his wife's needs: since a concert where her phone died, he charges it as soon as they reach a hotel, and he carries snacks, Advil and safety pins in case she needs them. He says he started making videos "hoping to make even one person feel seen and inspired." ([Variety, May 11, 2026](https://variety.com/2026/digital/news/dhar-mann-podcast-what-happens-jameela-jamil-anderson-paak-1236735622/))

The one intimate witness on record backs the warmth. Lilly Ghalichi of Bravo's _Shahs of Sunset_ ended their 2014 engagement that December without giving a reason and wrote that "it was so nice to be with someone as honest and loving as he was." ([E! News, December 19, 2014](https://www.eonline.com/news/607973/shahs-of-sunset-s-lilly-ghalichi-calls-off-engagement-i-know-that-every-ending-is-a-new-beginning)) A gracious breakup statement is limited testimony, but it is independent. On a Two reading, the cars and the Instagram were a lonely young man buying affection, and the studio is the same giving at scale.

Here is where Three pulls ahead.

He never separated love from success; he chased money to be "as loved as possible." What changed was how he tried to earn it. The boy's money went to other people; the man's went into the image. In 2007 he kept a Lamborghini he couldn't afford, to keep "the appearance." In 2014 he leased a Ferrari and a Rolls-Royce for an Instagram following he tied to "social acceptance." The car brought friends, by his account, but it did so by making him look successful, not by meeting anyone's needs.

The same currency runs through his story of an early influencer trip. Walking past Laura's upgraded friends to the economy seats a brand had paid for, he said, "I felt like I was nothing and I was just humiliated." Laura said she didn't care. His coda is about rank: many of those first-class passengers "are now the ones riding in economy."

His story about his wife's phone ends the same way:

> "So love taught me that it's in noticing the small details, and you win when you serve others. Taking that same application can also apply to business. And when I did that, that's when I started winning big."

The examples that follow come from business, "when it came to my team members, for instance, or it came to certain brand partners." He surprises people with concert tickets after overhearing them, and remembers their children by name and grade, because that "makes somebody feel seen." He says it shows "that you truly care about them," not a "business purpose." He also files it under what made him win.

None of that makes the care fake. It is specific and it costs him time, and the Three read does not fully explain it.

[Type 7](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-7) fits his twenties too: the Ferrari, the Las Vegas trips, what the Bay Citizen called his "work hard, party hard ethic," and ventures from car rentals to hydroponics to makeup schools. But he names attention and "social acceptance" as the reason, not pleasure. The collapse brought shame, not a hunt for the next thrill, and the moral-fable formula held from 2018 through 2026.

## What the big house was for

He told Sanchez in 2026 what the business had cost his family:

> "I just thought that because I was trying to improve the life of my wife and my kids that that made every decision okay. So, I skipped out on the date nights, I skipped out on the dinners, I skipped out on the school plays."

One day his 5-year-old daughter came into his office and asked him to play, and he said he was busy. Later, in the kitchen, he asked her, "Sweetheart, you see this big house that we live in?" That was why Daddy had to work so hard, he told her. A couple of minutes after he went back to work, she knocked on his door. "I don't want to have a big house anymore."

When providing and being present collided, providing won, and he told himself it made every decision okay.

What he took from it: "the thing that I valued, that I thought that I was delivering on as a dad to help make everyone else's lives better or dreams come true, was actually not even the thing that they really wanted."

A Two reading can claim that line, a provider who misjudged what his family needed. Two details tip it toward Three. His daughter asked for his time, and he pointed to a status object. And he framed the correction as a question about success: "what is success if I can't even spend time with my own family."

## When the kindness brand was accused

A studio that sells kindness gets judged on how it treats people. In February 2023 several of its regular actors protested over pay and treatment. One of them, Colin A. Borden, described "a culture at the studio of, if you talk, you're out." ([CGMagazine, February 10, 2023](https://www.cgmagonline.com/news/dhar-mann-paying-actors-poorly)) The actors said Mann kept missing meetings they had asked for. ([Passionfruit, March 9, 2023](https://passionfru.it/dhar-mann-actors-protest-3292/))

Mann said their wish to meet him personally was "never communicated," called the low-pay claims "100% not true," said speaking roles paid $33 to $44 an hour and blamed "misinformation." He did meet with actors, and listed changes that came out of that meeting, writing that "there's always opportunity for growth." Tubefilter reported that one actor issued an official retraction. ([Tubefilter, February 14, 2023](https://www.tubefilter.com/2023/02/14/protest-dhar-mann-actors-speak-out-moral-videos-response/))

As of October 2026, the workplace claims remain disputed. His public answer came as a rebuttal with numbers, then a meeting and a list of fixes. That fits a Three defending a reputation, and almost any company under attack.

## What the type leaves out

He volunteers the vivid failures. The Lamborghini rental and the Instagram "addiction" come from his own mouth on national podcasts, and he told Shetty that because he had "failed a lot more than everybody else," he decided, "instead of hiding from that, I'm going to embrace that." That is an odd habit for a man guarding an image, unless the redemption story is the image.

The failure that sits in a court record gets the narrowest telling. His 2021 About page tells his life as a timeline that starts in 2014, with "I felt lost, depressed and was struggling to find purpose." WeGrow and the fraud case do not appear, and in his two long 2026 interviews he never names the case.

Public sources can't settle whether the restraint since 2015 is a changed motive or a sturdier image. "Stop trying to look rich before you actually are rich," he told Sanchez's audience in 2026; once you have real wealth, "you'll not want to show it because you just become a bigger target in everyone's eyes anyway." Even the case for quiet runs through how other people see you. In 2020 he bought [Khloé Kardashian](/personality-analysis/khloe-kardashian)'s Calabasas mansion for $15.5 million, according to [the Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2021/08/19/style/dhar-mann-youtube.html).

## Back in the frame
