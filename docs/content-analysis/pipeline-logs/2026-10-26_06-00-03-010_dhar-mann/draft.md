The worst advice Dhar Mann says he ever got was to change his name.

He told the story in the rapid-fire round that closes Jay Shetty's podcast, in May 2026. Someone had told him to drop the name, "because at that point in my life, I had failed so many times that the only thing associated with my name was failure." He added, "for a long time, honestly, I did think about changing my name." ([On Purpose, May 20, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2UW5wnan1bA))

The record attached to that name was public. In December 2013 he pleaded no contest to five felony counts of defrauding a City of Oakland grant program, and in January 2014 he was sentenced to five years' probation, a $10,000 fine and $44,399.51 in restitution. ([SF Chronicle, February 1, 2014](https://www.sfgate.com/crime/article/Ganja-preneur-of-Oakland-sentenced-fined-5195154.php))

When the city settled a related civil suit in 2015, its city attorney said the case showed that "no matter how connected you are, or how much money you have, or how many photos of your Lamborghini you post on social media, we will hold you accountable if you attempt to defraud Oakland taxpayers." ([East Bay Express, April 24, 2015](https://eastbayexpress.com/oakland-taxicab-family-hit-with-37500-fine-for-defrauding-oakland-2-1/))

He kept the name.

Forbes ranked him No. 2 on its 2026 Top Creators list, with estimated earnings of $65 million and a team of 200 whose shows draw nearly 300 million views in a typical week. ([Forbes, June 24, 2026](https://forbes.com.au/news/leadership/forbes-top-creators-2026))

His face is another story. In the early videos he appeared at the end to deliver the moral. When he started focusing on YouTube, he checked the analytics.

> "I was humbled really quickly when I saw our audience retention would tank as soon as I would come on to tell the moral. So I literally had to fire myself."

That was on Guy Raz's _How I Built This_ in April 2024. Raz summed it up: "Dhar Mann Studios doesn't actually need Dhar Mann." ([How I Built This, April 22, 2024](https://traffic.megaphone.fm/VMP8163864444.mp3))

So he kept the name and cut the face. Both choices protect what an audience thinks of when it hears "Dhar Mann."

Mann was born in 1984 ([birthday post, May 29, 2021](https://web.archive.org/web/20210529192005/https://twitter.com/dharmann/status/1398720779538345993)) and grew up in and around Oakland, California, the son of Sikh immigrants from India whose taxi company, Friendly Cab, became one of the city's biggest. ([SF Chronicle, May 18, 2012](https://www.sfgate.com/crime/article/Oakland-pot-entrepreneur-charged-with-fraud-3567342.php)) Since 2018 his studio has made scripted moral fables with titles like "RICH Kid WON'T TIP Pizza Boy, He Lives to Regret It."

He tells his own life the same way, as a redemption story. The open question is what steered him before the turn and what steers him now. His own answers point to Enneagram Type 3.

## What is Dhar Mann's personality type?

Dhar Mann's personality type is Enneagram Type 3, the Achiever. Threes are in the heart triad, where the core emotion is shame, and they cope by becoming what success looks like to the people watching. Mann's own account puts his worth in visible success and his dread in public failure, before YouTube and after it.

In a [Type 3](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-3), shame turns into a question asked of every room: what counts as winning here? They find the answer, turn themselves into it and keep score. If the picture and the facts start to separate, the picture usually gets rescued first, and the bill arrives later.

Mann has described those steps himself. As a boy he "associated money with love." At 21 he bought a Lamborghini and was, in his words, "being seen by everyone." In 2026 he told Shetty he had only recently started to feel he belonged:

> "As much as I could try to convince myself of that before, I would never have been able to until I reached a certain level of success objectively."

That was the professional side, he said. The belonging that matters more to him now comes from being a father to his two daughters.

The read is held at medium confidence, because the main narrator of the Dhar Mann story is Dhar Mann, and redemption is what his studio sells. The closest rival is [Type 2](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-2), the Helper, which gets its own section below.

## The Lamborghini he wouldn't give up

His parents worked long hours building the cab company. "Because they weren't around a lot, instead of being able to give me their time, they gave me money to do things," he told The New York Times in 2021. ([NYT, August 19, 2021](https://www.nytimes.com/2021/08/19/style/dhar-mann-youtube.html))

Bullied for his turban until his mother had him and his brother cut their hair, in fourth grade, he grew up feeling "too Indian for the Americans and I was too American for the Indians." In high school he sometimes ate lunch in a bathroom "because I didn't want anyone to know that I had no one to sit with."

Then he found something that worked.

When he paid for classmates' lunches, snacks and arcade games, "a lot of people wanted to be my friend."

> "I didn't realize this until my adult years, but I associated money with love since that's how my parents expressed their love to me since they were never around."

At UC Davis he went from a flyer business to a telemarketing operation feeding refinance leads to his brother, a real estate broker, and then to his own mortgage offices. At 21 he bought an orange Lamborghini.

"So for the first time in my life, I saw what having all this attention felt like," he told Raz. Through high school, he said, "I felt invisible with no friends. And now I was being seen by everyone."

The 2007 housing crash closed the mortgage business, and he moved back to Oakland with an expensive car payment. He would not give up the car, which "in my head was the key to all this attention I was getting."

> "So instead of giving up the car, I started an exotic car rental company. I learned some basic web design and put up my Lamborghini for rent. So I was able to keep my car and more importantly, the appearance that I was a lot more successful than I actually was."

That is the plainest statement in the record of what he was protecting, and it came from him. It is also a 2024 look back, and redemption stories reward a vivid "before." So the question is whether a reporter who met him at the time saw the same man.

## "Somewhat appealing to watch"

In the fall of 2010, Josh Harkinson of Mother Jones followed Mann, then 26, around weGrow, the hydroponics superstore he had opened near the Oakland airport that January. At a party there, a three-person crew filmed him for _Hempire_, a reality-show pilot he was costarring in, with a promo he and his partner had paid for themselves.

"I personally have always wanted a reality show 'cause I just think that my life is somewhat appealing to watch," Mann told him. ([Mother Jones, December 3, 2010](https://www.motherjones.com/politics/2010/12/wegrow-dhar-mann-derek-peterson/))

Hydroponics shops then kept up a pretense that their customers grew tomatoes. When the PR firm handling weGrow's opening handed Mann a release that read "Urban gardening megastore opens by airport," he fired the firm and rewrote it: "Marijuana Superstore Opens in East Oakland." A TV crew was outside on opening morning, and within hours his wholesale supplier said it would stop selling to him.

In his 2024 telling, the tomato version "just felt a little hypocritical to me." Both can be true: the release was candid, and it was also the louder headline.

Michael Hunt, a former council aide who worked for weGrow in 2010, said Mann didn't necessarily get special treatment because of his parents' ties to city leaders. "But the manner in which (Mann) has attempted to highlight and capitalize on those relationships," Hunt said, "probably attracted as many people to him as were scared away because of any lingering gray area or questions of ethics." Mann did not respond to the outlet. ([Bay Citizen via NBC Bay Area, September 6, 2012](https://www.nbcbayarea.com/news/local/indicted-oakland-pot-entrepreneur-leaned-on-political-relationships/1927584/))

That is the record from eight years before his first video, when no algorithm was paying him to be watched. The wish to be watched was already there, and he said it out loud.

## The shortcut

The trouble came from his property business. Oakland reimbursed owners for up to half the cost of renovating commercial buildings in certain areas. In Mann's 2024 account, he "always wanted to do things quickly," took on several renovations at once and then learned the reimbursements could take a year or two.

> "I decided to take some shortcuts on my grant application. I changed the dates on my paperwork to make it seem like the projects had been completed and contractors had been paid sooner than they were in order to start the reimbursement process faster."

Prosecutors alleged more. In May 2012 they charged him with 13 felonies, including grand theft and forgery. An investigator's court filing said he paid contractors much less than he claimed and redeposited the cashier's checks he had shown the city. His lawyer conceded that he "took shortcuts" but called the charges overblown. ([SF Chronicle, May 18, 2012](https://www.sfgate.com/crime/article/Oakland-pot-entrepreneur-charged-with-fraud-3567342.php))

On December 10, 2013, he pleaded no contest to five counts, a conviction without an admission of the underlying facts. ([Mother Jones, December 11, 2013](https://www.motherjones.com/crime-justice/2013/12/marijuana-entrepreneur-dhar-mann-fraud-oakland/)) He told the Times in 2021 that the conviction was later expunged.

He tells this part with a line from his own videos: "Or as I say in my videos, what happens in the dark always comes to light because that's exactly what happens."

Asked whether the hit to his reputation might follow him for life, he said, "I definitely felt like a failure," and that he wished he could tell his younger self "how important it is to do things the right way and how much your reputation really matters."

Doing things the right way is in that answer, and so is reputation. After a fraud plea, both are ordinary lessons. The second is also the lesson of a man for whom other people's verdict is the main event.

## "Money and fame"

By his account, the next fall began with a friend whose affiliate-marketing business paid him monthly returns. Around 2013 or 2014 he put in everything he had, then sold his limousines and cars to put in more. No public record of the scheme was found, so it is told here as his account, and the man is not named.

The two of them moved to Los Angeles. "I realized in LA, you need two things, money and fame," Mann said. He decided "to do whatever it took to build a large following on Instagram," leased a house in the Hollywood Hills, a Ferrari and a Rolls-Royce, and found that "the flashier I was on Instagram, the faster my following would grow." Relatives who saw the posts invested with the friend too.

> "I've never done any hard drugs in my life, but I would say Instagram was more addictive for me than any drug out there for someone who valued social acceptance as much as I did."

"My life really felt like a movie," he told Raz. Seven years later, the About page on his own website was headed "My Life is like a Movie." ([dharmann.com, archived December 2021](https://web.archive.org/web/20211220085945/https://www.dharmann.com/about/))

Then the money stopped. He says the friend's last payment bounced, the friend went silent, and the business turned out to be a Ponzi scheme.

That year he was engaged to Lilly Ghalichi of Bravo's _Shahs of Sunset_, who had announced it on Instagram on April Fools' Day and told E! News the idea was his. ([E! News, April 4, 2014](https://www.eonline.com/news/528442/shahs-of-sunset-s-lilly-ghalichi-engaged-to-entrepreneur-dhar-mann-but-will-she-keep-the-ring)) She ended the engagement that December without giving a reason, in a statement that called him "honest and loving." ([E! News, December 19, 2014](https://www.eonline.com/news/607973/shahs-of-sunset-s-lilly-ghalichi-calls-off-engagement-i-know-that-every-ending-is-a-new-beginning))

He was 30 and sleeping on a couch, with "less than $600" in the bank. He deactivated his Instagram "because I could no longer afford to keep up that lifestyle."

"For me, as a 30-year-old Indian man, I was looking at it as like my family is ashamed of me, my culture I'm letting down," he told Codie Sanchez in July 2026. ([BigDeal, July 29, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Jx1gRjdgyVs)) And to Shetty:

> "I had worries that on my tombstone people were going to say, 'Here is Dhar Mann, the biggest failure that has ever existed.'"

A felony plea and relatives' losses would weigh on anyone, and that sense of where a man should be at 30 is partly cultural. That is why the choices he made when things were going well carry more weight in this read than the fall.

## The package decides

He says the recovery began when he stopped blaming the economy, the government and the friend for his three failures and saw that he "was the common denominator." Soon after, an online makeup school he built with a beauty influencer brought in six figures within 24 hours of going live.

"I decided that I would not be flashy. I would not spend money wastefully. I didn't upgrade my lifestyle even 1%," he said, until he had repaid the relatives who lost money. That is a costly break from the old pattern, though only he has described it.

Through those classes he met Laura Avila, who became his partner in the cosmetics company LiveGlam and later his wife. He credits her with his ethics: "I grew up where operating in a gray area was okay. Right? Like, if as long as you made money, it was okay." Laura, he said, "was so black and white about everything." Gray area was the phrase Hunt had used about him in 2012.

On May 29, 2018, his 34th birthday, he posted his first motivational video to Facebook. He picked the date, he told Shetty, because "I'm going to get extra love," and he expected the algorithm to favor a page everyone would visit that day.

It took "hundreds of takes." He "refreshed and refreshed thinking I'd get millions of views," and it never broke 100. "That's how delusional I was." The first comment came from a friend: "Who do you think you are? [Tony Robbins](/personality-analysis/tony-robbins)?"

About three months later, a sketch he had planned as his last, about a husband who takes his stay-at-home wife for granted, went viral. From there the studio became a system, and the system runs on packaging. He told Sanchez he'd run "about 100 different title tests" for a single podcast episode, and that ideas that can't become a strong thumbnail die: "you'll have to kill the idea."

> "If it's not great packaging, then by default, it can't be a great story."

The stories follow a fixed rule, which he described to Raz: "in my videos, unlike in the real world, the good person always wins in the end. And the bad person always learns their lesson." Robin Kaiser-Schatzlein put the effect plainly in The New York Times Magazine: "Mann guarantees us both a happy ending and the feeling of being right." ([NYT Magazine, August 4, 2021](https://www.nytimes.com/2021/08/04/magazine/wholesome-youtube-dhar-mann.html))

Every creator at his scale tests thumbnails, so none of this alone says much about his personality, which is why the 2010 and 2014 record carries more weight. What the testing adds is the order of operations: in his studio, the package decides whether the story gets made.

And when his own face hurt the numbers, the face went. That choice cuts against reading him as someone who simply wants attention. A Three gives up the spotlight when the spotlight stops winning.

## Why not Type 2?

[Type 2](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-2) shares the heart triad and its core emotion of shame, but Twos earn their worth by being needed, noticing what people want before they ask. The Two case for Mann is real, and most of it comes from him.

The boy who bought classmates lunch was giving to be liked. On Shetty's show he described how he looks after his wife. Since a concert where her phone died, he charges it as soon as they reach a hotel, and he carries snacks, Advil and safety pins in case she needs them. If he overhears someone mention a favorite artist, he buys them tickets. He remembers acquaintances' children by name and grade, because that "makes somebody feel seen."

He says he started making videos "hoping to make even one person feel seen and inspired." ([Variety, May 11, 2026](https://variety.com/2026/digital/news/dhar-mann-podcast-what-happens-jameela-jamil-anderson-paak-1236735622/)) On a Two reading, the cars and the Instagram were a lonely young man buying affection, and the studio is the same giving at scale.

Here is where Three pulls ahead.

A Two's worth rests on being loved for what they give, and the dread is being unwanted. At Mann's costliest turning points, worth sat in visible success and the dread was public failure.

The car in 2007 served no relationship; it preserved "the appearance." The fear at 30 was a ruined name and a family's shame. Professional belonging came only after "a certain level of success objectively." Even his account of giving ends on a scoreboard. Here is how the story about his wife's phone wraps up:

> "So love taught me that it's in noticing the small details, and you win when you serve others. Taking that same application can also apply to business. And when I did that, that's when I started winning big."

None of that makes the care fake. It is specific and it costs him time, and a Three with a strong Two wing would account for much of it.

[Type 7](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-7) fits his twenties too: the Ferrari, the Las Vegas trips, what the Bay Citizen called his "work hard, party hard ethic." But the reason he gives for that life is attention and "social acceptance," not pleasure, and since 2018 he has stuck with one formula.

## What the big house was for

When the studio took off, he told Sanchez, family came second:

> "I just thought that because I was trying to improve the life of my wife and my kids that that made every decision okay. So, I skipped out on the date nights, I skipped out on the dinners, I skipped out on the school plays."

One day his 5-year-old daughter came into his office and asked him to play, and he said he was busy. Later, in the kitchen, he asked her, "Sweetheart, you see this big house that we live in?" That was why Daddy had to work so hard, he told her. A couple of minutes after he went back to work, she knocked on his door. "I don't want to have a big house anymore."

He told Raz he had "always had this insane work ethic" and was "neglecting my family." What stopped him was physical: after COVID hit the family on Christmas Eve 2022, he had panic attacks "every couple hours for a month straight." In 2023 he handed human resources and finance to Laura and production to Ruben Ortiz, his first hire.

When providing and being present collided, providing won, and he called it love. That is closer to a Three's over-work than to a Two's over-giving.

In February 2023, the studio's kindness brand met its first public test, when several of its regular actors protested over pay and treatment. One of them, Colin A. Borden, described "a culture at the studio of, if you talk, you're out." ([CGMagazine, February 10, 2023](https://www.cgmagonline.com/news/dhar-mann-paying-actors-poorly)) The actors said Mann kept missing meetings they had asked for. ([Passionfruit, March 9, 2023](https://passionfru.it/dhar-mann-actors-protest-3292/))

Mann said their wish to meet him personally was "never communicated." He called the low-pay claims "100% not true," said speaking roles paid $33 to $44 an hour and blamed "misinformation." In the same statement he wrote, "I do acknowledge that there's always opportunity for growth," and listed changes. ([Tubefilter, February 14, 2023](https://www.tubefilter.com/2023/02/14/protest-dhar-mann-actors-speak-out-moral-videos-response/))

In 2024 he told Raz that "a small subset of our actors" had wanted to be paid "more than the business could support" and that pay rates had since gone up. The workplace claims remain disputed. His public answer came as a rebuttal with numbers, then a list of fixes. That fits a Three defending a reputation, and almost any company under attack.

## What the type leaves out

He volunteers the worst of his old self. The Lamborghini rental, the Instagram "addiction" and the grant "shortcuts" all come from his own mouth on national podcasts. That is an odd habit for a man guarding an image, unless the redemption story is the image.

The telling is still selective, and it moves. His 2021 About page tells his life as a timeline that starts in 2014, with "I felt lost, depressed and was struggling to find purpose." WeGrow and the fraud case do not appear, and in his two long 2026 interviews he never names the case. Even the origin story shifts: on Shetty's show in 2026, the video he planned as his last was an infidelity sketch, not the stay-at-home-wife one.

Public sources can't settle whether the restraint since 2015 is a changed motive or a sturdier image. "Stop trying to look rich before you actually are rich," he told Sanchez's audience in 2026. Real wealth "is built quietly," he said, and once you have it, "you'll not want to show it because you just become a bigger target in everyone's eyes anyway." Even the case for quiet runs through how other people see you.

In 2020 he bought [Khloé Kardashian](/personality-analysis/khloe-kardashian)'s Calabasas mansion for $15.5 million, according to the Times. The two facts don't cancel each other out. They sit side by side.

He also has a soft spot for a life he hasn't chosen. Some people, he told Sanchez, "want to be able to clock out at 4:00 p.m." and have dinner and watch the game with their spouse, "and honestly I kind of envy that." A moment later he was back to "Why not me?"

## "Why can't we get there?"

He told Sanchez how he talks himself past limits: "I just think, well, look at what Disney's doing. Why can't we get there?" Three weeks later, on August 21, 2026, Disney ordered 20 episodes from his studio. "I grew up with Disney stories," he said.
