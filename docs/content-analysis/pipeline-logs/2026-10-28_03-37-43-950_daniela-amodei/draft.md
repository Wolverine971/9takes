At OpenAI, Daniela Amodei had an advisory council made of stuffed animals.

She and her husband, Holden Karnofsky, kept a large collection, which they treated as their "personal advisory council for managerial role-playing," Kevin Roose reports. Each animal had a character profile. Jinji, a lazy cat, encouraged her to cancel meetings and take on less work. Maura, a pink bear, was "a detail-oriented, type-A manager." A panda named Beary Bonds was the "patron bear of generosity and empathy."

She treated the exercises as "something between an inside joke and an earnest management technique," Roose writes, and in 2023 she told him the animals were "very useful in analyzing different personality types." ([The Atlantic, September 29, 2026](https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/2026/09/openai-v-anthropic-inside-biggest-rivalry-tech/688819/))

The bear and the panda make a decent sketch of the question people ask about her.

Amodei is a co-founder and the president of Anthropic, the company behind the AI model Claude. Its [IPO registration statement](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026067168/anthropicpbc-sx1.htm), filed October 26, 2026, also lists her as chair of the board. Her brother Dario is the chief executive. "My job is the second and Daniela's job is the first," he told the [Cheeky Pint podcast in August 2025](https://cheekypint.transistor.fm/5/transcript): strategy for him, operational execution for her. By June 2026, [TechCrunch reported](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/10/anthropics-dario-amodei-has-just-one-direct-report/), everyone on the executive team except Dario's chief of staff reported to her.

Inside OpenAI, some employees saw her as "her brother's high-EQ counterpart." She calls herself a "people leader." Asked what her own reports say about her, she named both animals at once.

> "I'm a supportive manager. I really want people to succeed. And I also hold a high bar."

That was at Stanford's [Entrepreneurial Thought Leaders series](https://stvp.stanford.edu/node/10761/printable/print) in February 2024. The revealing moments come when the two pull in different directions. Her record shows which one usually wins, and it isn't the one her reputation suggests.

## What is Daniela Amodei's personality type?

Daniela Amodei's personality type is Enneagram Type 1, the Reformer. Ones are driven by a need to be good and to set things right. When a principle costs Amodei something, she explains the choice by what is right, not by whom she protected, and the rules she builds apply to her too.

[Type 1](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-1) sits in the body triad, the three types whose core emotion is anger. In a One, anger rarely arrives as a tantrum. It shows up as correction.

The pattern runs in a few plain steps. A One notices the gap between how things are and how they should be, and feels the gap as a duty. They write a standard to close it and hold themselves to it first. The usual cost is rigidity.

Her account of her first career has that shape. Explaining what pulled her into international development, she told a [Stanford Graduate School of Business audience](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/daniela-amodei-says-curiosity-underrated) in 2026:

> "there's something that feels really wrong to me about the fact that I was born in America"

Then the question she asked herself: "How do I make that more fair?" The sentence names a wrong and goes looking for the fix.

This read comes from her interviews, company filings and reporting, and it is held at medium confidence. A good deal of what looks like Type 1 is also a president's job description, since building review systems and hiring rubrics is what operators do. In her own interviews she shows almost no anger at anyone; the angry scenes in her story come from other people's accounts. The closest rival is [Type 2](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-2), the Helper, and it gets its own section below.

## A do-gooder who followed her brother

She was born in 1987, four years after Dario, and the two grew up in San Francisco's Mission District, [Wired reported in March 2025](https://www.wired.com/story/anthropic-benevolent-artificial-intelligence/). Their parents had moved from Italy to San Francisco in the 1970s. As kids, Daniela told Wired, she and Dario played games in which they saved the world. She won a classical-flute scholarship in high school and later earned a B.A. in English literature at UC Santa Cruz.

"We were always just like best friends like since we were really little," she told Walter Isaacson on [*The Builders*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1OdoSzfUkec) in October 2026. Becoming a vegetarian was "the only thing that I did first," she said. When Dario guessed he had followed "three years later or something," she corrected him on the spot: "Oh, not even. ... You got on board real quick."

Her first career followed his interests. In a [March 2022 podcast](https://futureoflife.org/podcast/daniela-and-dario-amodei-on-anthropic/) she said she went into international development partly because "it was an interest area of Dario's at the time."

> "I'm a few years younger than him and so I was very keen to understand the things that he was working or interested in as a potential area to have impact."

That is the strongest Type 2 evidence in her own words. It also ends on impact rather than on him.

She worked for NGOs in Washington, for a public-health organization in East Africa, on a congressional campaign and on Capitol Hill. A friend at an old job, she said, used to call her "the classic do-gooder." Capitol Hill didn't last: "It just was slow and bureaucratic," she said at Stanford in 2024.

For a supposed rule-lover, that's worth noticing. She left the institution most devoted to procedure because the procedure got in the way of the work.

She joined Stripe in May 2013, when it had about 40 people, managed recruiting and later worked in risk. She left in July 2018 because payments didn't fully meet her aim of "wanting to like, positively impact the world." Greg Brockman, a friend she had met at Stripe, recruited her to OpenAI.

## Brought in to fix a mess

At OpenAI, from October 2018 to December 2020, she held roles including vice president of safety and policy, vice president of people and engineering manager, according to Anthropic's S-1. In Anthropic's [December 2024 co-founder video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=om2lIWXLLN4), she said the job "felt very me-shaped":

> "there's all these really great people with these really good intentions, but it seems like they're a little bit of a mess."

She "felt a very strong desire to come and sort of just try to help make the company a little more functional." A Two and a One could both say that sentence. The Two hears "help." The One hears "a mess" and "functional."

What happened next comes from other people. In 2026 three outlets reported on her OpenAI years, drawing on unnamed sources and, in one case, on Dario's contemporaneous notes. She has not given her own account.

**The language project.** According to the [Wall Street Journal](https://www.tovima.com/wsj/the-decadelong-feud-shaping-the-future-of-ai/) (March 28, 2026), Brockman wanted a role in the new GPT research, but Dario, then research director, "wanted him nowhere near it." Daniela, who co-led the language project with Alec Radford, told Brockman he couldn't work on it. When Sam Altman asked whether there was any way to make it work, "she offered to step down as head of the project rather than allow Brockman on it." Brockman had recruited her and, The Atlantic adds, gave a toast at her 2017 wedding to Karnofsky.

Read as a One, that is a standard held against a friend at personal cost. Read as a Two, it is a sister backing her brother, and the Journal's sequence, with Dario's objection first and Radford also unwilling, makes that reading at least as plausible.

**The peer review.** By March 2020, the Journal reports, [Altman](/personality-analysis/sam-altman) had asked his executives to write peer reviews of one another. Brockman's feedback accused her, in the Journal's paraphrase, of "abusing her power to create bureaucratic processes to get her way." Altman called it "tough but fair." She "delivered a long response, rebutting him point by point." The Atlantic adds that she clashed with [Mira Murati](/personality-analysis/mira-murati) over who made safety decisions, and that Brockman and others came to see her as "the third member of an inflexible triumvirate."

**The accusation.** The [New Yorker](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/04/13/sam-altman-may-control-our-future-can-he-be-trusted) (April 13, 2026), drawing on Dario's notes and one person briefed on the exchange, says Altman told the siblings he had it on "good authority" from a senior executive that they were plotting a coup. Daniela, the notes say, "lost it," and brought in that executive, who denied having said anything. Altman then denied making the claim: "I didn't even say that."

> "You just said that," Daniela replied.

Altman told the magazine this was "not quite his recollection" and that he had accused them only of "political behavior." The Journal's version, from unnamed sources, ends with the siblings "shouting angrily at him."

If the accounts are right, her move under accusation was to put the facts in the room and correct the record on the spot. That is what a One's anger tends to look like. It is also what most people accused of a coup would do, and every version comes from her side's notes or unnamed sources. These episodes line up with the Type 1 read. They can't carry it.

When the group left in late 2020, she led their side of the exit negotiations, which ended in a mutual non-disparagement agreement, The Atlantic reports.

## The rules apply to her too

The evidence that does carry the read comes from her own descriptions of how she runs Anthropic.

In May 2021, when Anthropic had about 15 people, she built a spreadsheet with every employee's name down the side and across the top. Each person had to tell every colleague at least one thing they appreciated and one way they could do better, practice it with someone first, then deliver it. She described it on the Kleiner Perkins [*Grit* podcast](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BhUyWzf7cWc&t=5444s) in February 2024:

> "I made everyone go do that and they had to mark in the spreadsheet when they had done it"

Then she asked each person how the conversation had gone. The spreadsheet recorded only that each conversation happened, not what was said.

The compulsory appreciation would suit a Two. What reads as One is the form: a value turned into a universal procedure, with compliance checked.

The procedure reaches the top. Everyone at Anthropic who has been there more than two months gets a performance review every six months, she said on the same podcast, "and that includes me and [Dario] we get reviews from the board." She checks references on senior hires and invites them to check hers. "What I want is no surprises," she said. "I let senior hires talk to references on me."

On the Sixth Street podcast [*It's Not Magic*](https://sixthstreet.com/podcasts/) in February 2026, she said "something that feels the most important to me about our culture at Anthropic is this concept of integrity." Going behind a colleague's back to avoid a difficult conversation: "We have no tolerance for that." Asked whether Anthropic had a reliable way to screen hires for empathy and the ability to work well with others, she said:

> "We have a culture interview that we have perfected over the five years that Anthropic has been--"

"Perfected?" the host asked. "Yes, yes," she said, and went on. The interview looks for people who "made difficult decisions in their life to stand up for their values." On Vanta's [*Frameworks for Growth*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bXT3ciKUHak) in June 2025, she said values that aren't creating tension are "probably too bland."

In the co-founder video, a co-founder not identified on screen says, "I credit Daniela with keeping the bar high." Someone adds, "Keeping out the clowns." A voice that sounds like hers answers: "Chief clown wrangler! That's my job." Then comes another line: "No, but you're the reason the culture scaled, I think."

## When the right thing costs money

The sharper test is what she says when a principle costs the company revenue.

**Customers.** Some prospective customers, she said at Stanford in 2024, get told: "I don't think we can do anything for you right now. And we don't wanna sell you a bill of goods ... to close a deal with you and get your logo." She called it "a fundamental kind of honesty and integrity."

**Ads.** On [*The Oprah Podcast*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=w5dJqHilu5s) in May 2026, she said keeping ads out of Claude was never a question for the co-founders, "but there were quiet forces that, you know, within the company or investors who were like, hey, this is a great source of revenue."

> "And we just said like, sorry, that's just not how we're going to do things. We'll find another way."

With Isaacson she tied the no-ads decision to sycophancy, which she called harder "to stamp out" than more obvious model failures. Rivals with ads, she said, make more money when users engage longer. "Do you then want to tell your user that they're wrong? No, you don't."

**The Pentagon.** In February 2026, according to Anthropic, the Pentagon said it would only contract with AI companies that accepted "any lawful use" of their models, which meant dropping Anthropic's two exceptions: mass domestic surveillance and fully autonomous weapons. It threatened to designate the company a "supply chain risk." Anthropic refused. "We cannot in good conscience accede to their request," Dario wrote in a [statement on February 26](https://www.anthropic.com/news/statement-department-of-war).

Asked by Oprah whether they had been scared, [Daniela said](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=w5dJqHilu5s&t=1838s) "it was a hard time" and "I don't think we want to sugarcoat that." But "the thing that we all had in common was we just felt like this was the right thing to do." Later, asked directly what she had been thinking, she called it "a policy disagreement," not a political one:

> "look, this is the right thing to do. These are the right red lines."

Two caveats. These were collective decisions, and "the right thing" is house vocabulary at Anthropic. A former OpenAI colleague told The Atlantic that Dario "had a habit at the time of just saying, We must do X; it is the morally correct thing to do." Her words alone can't separate her from her brother.

What separates her is what she leaves out. In none of these accounts does she say she was protecting Dario, her staff or her own standing. She cites what is right or honest.

Status gets the same treatment. Asked at [Bloomberg Tech in June 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Kfz-NxEvu-U) whether Anthropic was now the front-runner, she said "that is just completely the wrong way to think." The figures, she added, are "actually not the point," and the company exists to build AI "in a way that is ethical, that's responsible, that's fair."

Correcting the question is a habit. "I don't think I would put it that way," she told ABC's Rebecca Jarvis in February 2026, when [Jarvis suggested](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qUDG06BvWxc) harmlessness wasn't Anthropic's priority. Media-trained executives reframe premises all the time, so the habit proves little alone.

## The sharper sibling

The clearest contrast between the siblings comes from their joint interview with Isaacson.

He asked how they felt when OpenAI took the Pentagon work Anthropic had refused. Daniela answered first. "You can say we were wrong about what those red lines were," she allowed. Then she went further:

> "I found myself asking the question, was this a really principled stand that another technology company took based on their own ethics or was this an opportunistic moment?"

[Dario](/personality-analysis/dario-amodei) spoke next and moved the question somewhere else. "I actually think it's a beautiful illustration of the democratic process," he said. Companies took different approaches, and the public rendered a judgment he thought was "decisively on our side." She judged the other company's motive. He turned it into a story about process and public verdict.

It happened again on GPT-2. When Isaacson assumed the model had frightened her, she corrected him: "There wasn't something about [GPT-2], the model itself that made us, you know, concerned." After Dario described the skeptics who had dismissed it, she pushed past his framing: "I think you were quite polite about it, but" some well-respected scientists had "bullied some of the researchers who worked on it on Twitter." In fairness, the sharpest line in that exchange came from Dario: "You have no credibility on this matter."

She is careful about people, though. When Isaacson asked about Altman directly ("And you felt that about Sam?"), she said, "I think the co-founders are an unusually high integrity group of people." She judges conduct more readily than character.

9takes reads Dario as a Type 6, and the conversation shows how the pair differ. Explaining why disagreements at Anthropic get resolved, Dario said it's "because they're held in good faith." A Six asks whether people can be trusted. Her test was whether a choice was "a really principled stand." A One asks whether it is right. That contrast is an interpretation of two moments, but it fits a line she quotes with approval from Anthropic's chief people officer: "Dario makes us ambitious and I make us successful."

They also describe a lot of give. "About 80 to 90% of the time what ends up happening is we meet in the middle," she said at Bloomberg. And the bond gets protected time: "Dario comes over, he plays with my kids, we hang out together," she told Oprah.

## Why not Type 2?

The Type 2 case deserves a full hearing, and it starts in her own words.

She chose her first field partly to understand Dario's interests. Her reports, she says, call her "a supportive manager." On *Grit* she said, "I think in my heart the thing I most [am] is a people leader," and "I'm actually not an expert in any field I'm an expert in people." Some OpenAI colleagues saw her as the warm one. She calls her brother "the most impressive technical visionary in this space" and her job, she told [Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/90948058/how-anthropics-daniela-amodei-is-keeping-ai-grounded-in-safety) in 2023, is helping translate his vision "into sets of operating norms." A Two could explain the OpenAI fights as a sister defending her brother and her people.

Here is where it breaks.

Under cost, she moves toward a standard, not toward approval or closeness. The ads decision, the Pentagon lines and the customers she turns away are each explained by what is right or honest, and none by loyalty. Her signature management moves are impersonal rules she applies to herself. And she rejects the need to be needed, the engine of Type 2. Describing what she doesn't like about her work, she said:

> "if I'm temporarily in charge of a particular function and there's no leader for it my number one goal is getting myself out of that job by finding the right person"

Her idea of warmth has edges, too. For Claude she wants what she calls "professional warmth," she told Bloomberg's [*The Circuit*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v1wZwxY3CMg) in June 2026: "the goal is not for it to be your best friend," and "It should feel approachable, but distant, right?" (The documentary's captions don't label speakers; the line is very probably hers.)

The Two material sits mostly in how she describes herself and in a job that rewards people skills. The One material sits in what she does when something is at stake.

Two other types come up. **Type 6** has real support. She told The Atlantic it's "scary because we can lose it at any time," and asked, "Are we accidentally making things worse?" But her fear is aimed at being wrong, not at losing safety, and she acts decisively once she has decided. **Type 9** fits her give with Dario and her habit of handing disputes back to the people in them ("I probably shouldn't even be the arbiter," she said of technical leads who disagreed). It fails on the reported standoff with a friend, the point-by-point rebuttal and her readiness to judge. Of AI romantic companions she told Arianna Huffington, in an interview posted in October 2026: "I think that's a terrible use of AI and people should not do it."

## What the One reading leaves out

Several things don't fit a classic One, and they belong on the page.

She is easy on herself in public. Anthropic ran payroll, "which like we messed up multiple times," she told [Notion's *First Block*](https://www.notion.com/blog/first-block-with-daniela-amodei) in 2023. Her plan to give each weekday its own focus has "probably like 70% hit rate." Of the memoir titles Claude suggested for her, one that resonated was "I need an adult." Ones can be self-deprecating in public, and an inner critic isn't observable from outside. Still, there's no visible strain.

She is pragmatic about values. Anthropic retired one ("move fast and safe things") because "not everyone loved it," she said on Vanta, and "if not everyone loves it," then "people are not going to use it." She wants hires who can say "I changed my mind because I actually realized, you know, I wasn't right about this." She told Isaacson that Claude shouldn't be "locked into sort of one philosophical tradition."

She is charitable about other people's errors. Describing a serious infection that three sets of doctors misdiagnosed, she said: "this is not because any of those are bad doctors, right? They're really busy." ([Arianna Huffington interview, October 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IEQJmtTqY2g))

And she keeps her irritations private. On *Grit*, asked about her own working-with-me guide, "what's the thing that triggers you," she named what lights her up and what she's less good at, and no trigger. When Huffington asked about self-reflection and "learning from mistakes," she answered about social media and technology.

A One reading has to treat that quiet as control or privacy, not as proof. The only anger on record is reported by others. The Atlantic, citing "several people who know them well," says the siblings "still despise Altman and Brockman." In public she stays diplomatic about the split: "We were running towards something versus running away from something," she told Stanford GSB.

The leaving was harder than that line suggests. Before the group left OpenAI, she recalls, they wondered: "Maybe we should stay. Maybe we could make this work." She called a friend and mentor outside work, who told her she didn't really need the call.

> "You already know what the right answer is."

She named it the best advice she has ever received.
