At OpenAI, Daniela Amodei had an advisory council made of stuffed animals.

She and her husband, Holden Karnofsky, treated their collection as a "personal advisory council for managerial role-playing," Kevin Roose reports. Jinji, a lazy cat, encouraged her to cancel meetings and take on less work. Maura, a pink bear, was "a detail-oriented, type-A manager." A panda named Beary Bonds was the "patron bear of generosity and empathy." In 2023 she told Roose the animals were "very useful in analyzing different personality types." ([The Atlantic, September 29, 2026](https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/2026/09/openai-v-anthropic-inside-biggest-rivalry-tech/688819/))

The bear and the panda make a decent sketch of the question people ask about her.

Amodei is a co-founder and the president of Anthropic, the company behind the AI model Claude. Its [IPO registration statement](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026067168/anthropicpbc-sx1.htm), filed October 26, 2026, also lists her as chair of the board. Her brother Dario is the chief executive. "My job is the second and Daniela's job is the first," he told the [Cheeky Pint podcast in August 2025](https://cheekypint.transistor.fm/5/transcript): strategy for him, operational execution for her. By June 2026, [TechCrunch reported](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/10/anthropics-dario-amodei-has-just-one-direct-report/), everyone on the executive team except Dario's chief of staff reported to her.

At OpenAI, The Atlantic's unnamed sources say, some employees saw her as "her brother's high-EQ counterpart," while some leaders saw her as part of "an inflexible triumvirate" with Dario and Karnofsky. She calls herself a "people leader." Asked what her own reports say about her, she named both animals at once.

> "I'm a supportive manager. I really want people to succeed. And I also hold a high bar."

That was at Stanford's [Entrepreneurial Thought Leaders series](https://stvp.stanford.edu/node/10761/printable/print) in February 2024. The revealing moments come when the two pull in different directions, and her record is less decisive about which one she reaches for than either reputation suggests.

## What is Daniela Amodei's personality type?

Our leading read on Daniela Amodei's personality type is Enneagram Type 1, the Reformer, at medium confidence. Ones are driven by a need to be good and to set things right, and she repeatedly turns values into procedures that include her. But her record does not yet separate One from Type 2, and Type 6 stays live.

[Type 1](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-1) sits in the body triad, the three types whose core emotion is anger. In a One, anger rarely arrives as a tantrum. It shows up as correction: a gap between how things are and how they should be, felt as a duty to close.

Her account of her first career has that shape. "For me, it was always very interest and impact driven," she told a [Stanford Graduate School of Business audience](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/daniela-amodei-says-curiosity-underrated) in 2026:

> "there's something that feels really wrong to me about the fact that I was born in America, I had access to all of the sort of basic things that we take for granted in life."

Then the question she asked herself: "How do I make that more fair?" The sentence names a wrong and goes looking for the fix.

She has told the same story in a Two's language, too. In Anthropic's [December 2024 co-founder video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=om2lIWXLLN4), she said she had expected to return to development work because "I really wanna help people that have, you know, less than I do." Which language wins when something is at stake is what this profile tries to read.

A good deal of what looks like Type 1 is also a president's job description, since building review systems and hiring rubrics is what operators do. In her own interviews she shows almost no anger at anyone; the angry scenes in her story come from other people's accounts. Two rivals stay live: [Type 2](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-2), the Helper, and [Type 6](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6), the Loyalist, which is how 9takes reads her brother.

## The classic do-gooder

She was born in 1987, four years after Dario, and the two grew up in San Francisco's Mission District, [Wired reported in March 2025](https://www.wired.com/story/anthropic-benevolent-artificial-intelligence/). Their father, Riccardo, a leather craftsman from near Elba, and their mother, Elena Engel, a Chicago-born project manager for libraries, had moved from Italy to San Francisco in the 1970s; Riccardo died when the children were young adults. She later earned a B.A. in English literature at UC Santa Cruz.

"We were always just like best friends like since we were really little," she told Walter Isaacson on [*The Builders*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1OdoSzfUkec) in October 2026. Becoming a vegetarian was "the only thing that I did first," she said. When Dario guessed he had followed "three years later or something," she corrected him on the spot: "Oh, not even. ... You got on board real quick."

In a [March 2022 podcast](https://futureoflife.org/podcast/daniela-and-dario-amodei-on-anthropic/), she said "part of why" she went into international development was that "it was an interest area of Dario's at the time." He was "a very early GiveWell fan," and as students the two "started donating small amounts of money to organizations that were working on global health issues like malaria prevention."

> "I'm a few years younger than him and so I was very keen to understand the things that he was working or interested in as a potential area to have impact."

That is some of the strongest Type 2 evidence in her own words. It also ends on impact rather than on him.

She worked for NGOs in Washington, for a public-health organization in East Africa, on a congressional campaign and on Capitol Hill. A friend at an old job, she said, used to call her "the classic do-gooder." Capitol Hill didn't last: "It just was slow and bureaucratic," she said at Stanford in 2024.

For a supposed rule-lover, that's worth noticing. She left the institution most devoted to procedure because the procedure got in the way of the work.

She joined Stripe in May 2013, when it had about 40 people. Greg Brockman, one of its earliest employees, "was my boss at Stripe for a while," she said in the co-founder video, and when he was starting OpenAI she pitched him her brother: "The smartest person that I know is Dario." Dario joined by mid-2016, the Wall Street Journal reported. She left Stripe in July 2018 because payments didn't fully meet her aim of "wanting to like, positively impact the world," and Brockman, by then a friend, recruited her to OpenAI.

## Brought in to fix a mess

At OpenAI, from October 2018 to December 2020, she held roles including vice president of safety and policy, vice president of people and engineering manager, according to Anthropic's S-1. In the co-founder video she said the job "felt very me-shaped":

> "there's all these really great people with these really good intentions, but it seems like they're a little bit of a mess."

She "felt a very strong desire to come and sort of just try to help make the company a little more functional." A Two and a One could both say that sentence. The Two hears "help." The One hears "a mess" and "functional."

What happened next comes from 2026 reporting built on unnamed sources and, in one case, Dario's contemporaneous notes. As of October 2026, she has not publicly described these episodes.

**The language project.** According to the [Wall Street Journal](https://www.tovima.com/wsj/the-decadelong-feud-shaping-the-future-of-ai/) (March 28, 2026, syndicated by To Vima), Brockman, an OpenAI co-founder, wanted a role in the new GPT research, but Dario, then research director, "wanted him nowhere near it." Daniela, who co-led the language project with Alec Radford, told Brockman no. When Sam Altman, OpenAI's chief executive, asked whether there was any way to make it work, "she offered to step down as head of the project rather than allow Brockman on it." Altman asked her to stay and said he would find another way. Brockman had been her boss, had recruited her and, The Atlantic adds, gave a toast at her 2017 wedding.

Read as a One, that is a standard held against a friend at an offered cost. Read as a Two, it is a sister backing the brother she had recommended, and the Journal's sequence, with Dario's objection first and Radford also unwilling, makes that reading at least as plausible.

**The accusation.** A few months later, the Journal reports, Altman accused the siblings of plotting against him. "The brother and sister denied it." The [New Yorker](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/04/13/sam-altman-may-control-our-future-can-he-be-trusted) (April 13, 2026), drawing on Dario's notes and one person briefed on the exchange, says Altman told the siblings he had it on "good authority" from a senior executive that they were plotting a coup. Daniela, the notes say, "lost it," and brought in that executive, who denied having said anything. Altman then denied making the claim: "I didn't even say that."

> "You just said that," Daniela replied.

Altman told the magazine this was "not quite his recollection" and that he had accused them only of "political behavior." The Journal's version, from unnamed sources, ends with the siblings "shouting angrily at him."

If the accounts are right, she answered by putting the facts in the room, though not calmly. Dario had done the same months earlier, the New Yorker reports, reading a disputed Microsoft-deal provision aloud to Altman. Anyone accused of a coup might push back. These episodes line up with the Type 1 read. They can't carry it.

**The peer review.** By March 2020, the Journal reports, [Altman](/personality-analysis/sam-altman) had his executives write peer reviews of one another. Brockman's feedback accused her, in the Journal's paraphrase, of "abusing her power to create bureaucratic processes to get her way." Altman called it "tough but fair." She "delivered a long response, rebutting him point by point," and Brockman "at one point offered to withdraw" his feedback. The Atlantic adds that she clashed with [Mira Murati](/personality-analysis/mira-murati), then a vice president, over who made safety decisions.

In December 2020, Dario told OpenAI's staff that he and six other leaders, Daniela among them, were leaving to start what became Anthropic, The Atlantic reports. She led their side of the exit talks, which ended in a mutual non-disparagement agreement. "We were running towards something versus running away from something," she told Stanford GSB.

## The rules apply to her too

Her descriptions of how she runs Anthropic carry more of the read, though they are her own accounts, given mostly on recruiting and promotional stages.

In May 2021, when Anthropic had about 15 people, she built a spreadsheet with every employee's name down the side and across the top. Each person had to tell every colleague at least one thing they appreciated and one way they could do better, practice it first, then deliver it. She described it on the Kleiner Perkins [*Grit* podcast](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BhUyWzf7cWc&t=5444s) in February 2024:

> "I made everyone go do that and they had to mark in the spreadsheet when they had done it"

The spreadsheet recorded only that each conversation happened, not what was said. The compulsory appreciation would suit a Two. What reads as One is the form: a value turned into a universal procedure, with compliance checked.

She also said what the feedback is for. Sometimes she has had to ask "can we like make it a little nicer," but "in general I would prefer to be in an organization where people are trying to be honest with each other and trying to give feedback and coming from a place of wanting the best for the other person and maybe they get it a little bit wrong than a place where people are not communicating feedback to others." Her comparison is caring feedback against none at all, not honesty against kindness, and "wanting the best for the other person" is a motive a Two could claim as easily as a One.

The procedure reached the top, at least as she described it in 2024: everyone got a review every six months, "and that includes me and [Dario] we get reviews from the board." The S-1 now lists her as the board's chair, and how it reviews its own chair is not public. She lets senior hires check references on her, too, because "the best relationships stem from mutual understanding about what you're both signing up for."

On Sixth Street's [*It's Not Magic*](https://shows.acast.com/its-not-magic/episodes/69965d067012ce53766dc252) podcast in February 2026, she called integrity "the most important to me about our culture at Anthropic." Going behind a colleague's back to avoid a difficult conversation: "We have no tolerance for that." She raised empathy herself, too: "the ability to work well with others, that is something that we look for more than ever." Asked how they screen for it, she said:

> "We have a culture interview that we have perfected over the five years that Anthropic has been--"

"Perfected?" the host cut in. "Yes, yes, has been in existence," she said, finishing her sentence. The interview looks for mission alignment, collaboration and integrity: people who "made difficult decisions in their life to stand up for their values, whatever those are."

In the co-founder video, a co-founder not identified on screen says, "I credit Daniela with keeping the bar high." After a joke about "keeping out the clowns," someone says, "No, but you're the reason the culture scaled, I think," though the captions don't show to whom. Then: "People say how nice people are here." The bar and the niceness arrive within seconds of each other.

## When the stand has a price

**Ads.** On [*The Oprah Podcast*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=w5dJqHilu5s) in May 2026, she explained keeping ads out of Claude first by what users share: "People have very private conversations with these AI models. People upload their health information." For the co-founders it "was never a question," "but there were quiet forces that, you know, within the company or investors who were like, hey, this is a great source of revenue."

> "And we just said like, sorry, that's just not how we're going to do things. We'll find another way."

Protecting users is a reason a Two reading can claim as care. With Isaacson she tied the no-ads decision to sycophancy, which she called harder "to stamp out" than more obvious model failures. Rivals with ads, she said, make more money when users engage longer. "Do you then want to tell your user that they're wrong? No, you don't." The flaw she singles out is a model that tells people what they want to hear.

**The Pentagon.** In February 2026, according to Anthropic, the Pentagon demanded "any lawful use" of its models, which meant dropping Anthropic's two exceptions: mass domestic surveillance and fully autonomous weapons. Anthropic refused. "We cannot in good conscience accede to their request," Dario wrote in a [statement on February 26](https://www.anthropic.com/news/statement-department-of-war).

It cost them. The President directed federal agencies to stop using Anthropic's technology and the Pentagon moved to designate it a supply-chain risk, the S-1 says. Altman announced that the military would use OpenAI's models, [the New Yorker reports](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/04/13/sam-altman-may-control-our-future-can-he-be-trusted). Anthropic largely won in district court on August 27, 2026, lost before a split appeals panel on September 25, and was seeking rehearing as of the October filing.

Asked by Oprah whether they had been scared, [Daniela said](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=w5dJqHilu5s&t=1838s) it was "a hard time," adding, "I don't think we want to sugarcoat that. It was a stressful time at the company." The end of the company? "I think it crossed our minds." But "the thing that we all had in common was we just felt like this was the right thing to do."

Two caveats. These were collective decisions, and "the right thing" is house vocabulary at Anthropic. A former OpenAI colleague told The Atlantic that Dario "had a habit at the time of just saying, We must do X; it is the morally correct thing to do." Her words alone can't separate her from her brother.

There is also a skeptical reading: each stand built Anthropic's brand. Dario told Isaacson that Congress taking up the red lines was "success," and some people at OpenAI, The Atlantic reports, call the Amodeis "power-hungry virtue-signalers, not principled humanitarians." A pure brand play would not obviously invite a federal ban, but sincerity can't be proved from outside. Nor does it help that she never cites loyalty to Dario or her team: neither does Dario.

## Beside Dario

The siblings describe a lot of give. "About 80 to 90% of the time what ends up happening is we meet in the middle," she said at [Bloomberg Tech in June 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Kfz-NxEvu-U). Describing what is great about their relationship to Oprah, she named accountability: "we hold each other accountable," asking "Is this the right decision for the company is this in keeping with our values."

Their public registers differ in one dated exchange. Isaacson asked how they felt when OpenAI took the Pentagon work. Daniela answered first: "we can't control any other companies." "You can say we were wrong about what those red lines were," she allowed. Then:

> "I found myself asking the question, was this a really principled stand that another technology company took based on their own ethics or was this an opportunistic moment?"

[Dario](/personality-analysis/dario-amodei) returned to an argument he had made shortly before: "I actually think it's a beautiful illustration of the democratic process." She raised the other company's motive as a question and left it open. He told a story about process.

The difference is narrow and runs both ways. In fairness, the harshest verdict in that interview was Dario's: of scientists who had dismissed GPT-2, he said what he and his colleagues think now, though never aloud, is "You have no credibility on this matter." In private, by reported accounts, he is the blunter sibling. The Atlantic says he has called Altman and OpenAI "evil" in internal messages.

She was careful about Altman. Dario had just said it is "hard to know what someone's values are if they don't have the value of honesty." Isaacson asked her, "And you felt that about Sam?" She answered about her own side: "I think the co-founders are an unusually high integrity group of people." Invited to call Altman dishonest, she declined, which the non-disparagement agreement may explain as well as temperament does.

9takes reads Dario as a Type 6. Whether their registers reflect different types or different jobs, one interview can't say.

## Why not Type 2?

Much of the Type 2 case is already on the page: a first career shaped partly by Dario's interests, the "supportive manager," the wish to "help people that have, you know, less than I do." On *Grit* she said, "I think in my heart the thing I most [am] is a people leader," and "I'm actually not an expert in any field I'm an expert in people." In 2022 she said that "for the majority of our lives, I think we've wanted to find something to work together on."

Her career has tracked his across three eras: development work, partly for his interests; OpenAI, two years after he joined and after she had recommended him; and Anthropic, which she runs as his operating counterpart. A Two could read every stage as staying close to the person she most admires. Shared values explain the same path: the two began giving as students, and she left Stripe, by her account, for impact.

The Two case weakens, though it doesn't break, when "people leader" is unpacked. What lights her up, she said on *Grit*, is figuring out "who are going to be the people to work on it, how do we get them organized." When she is "temporarily in charge of a particular function and there's no leader for it," her goal is "getting myself out of that job by finding the right person," because "there's always a person in the world who is a better focus leader than I am." She stays the hub nearly every executive reports to, so this isn't a rejection of being needed. It is a standard, the best person for the job, outranking her hold on a role.

Both cases rest mostly on her own words, so the split is not description versus behavior. The Two material is how she describes her identity, her motives and her bond with Dario. The One material is the form her values take: compulsory, tracked feedback, reviews that include her, flattery as the flaw to train out, a sibling bond described as mutual accountability. Even there, the motive she gives is often care, as in her case for feedback.

So the record does not yet separate One from Two. One leads narrowly because that form recurs across contexts and a co-founder credits her with keeping the bar high. A Two in her job who has learned that candor helps could produce the same record. Named colleagues describing what she does when someone cuts a corner would settle more than another interview.

## Why not Type 6?

[Type 6](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6) is the subtler rival, and the record does not exclude it. "It's scary because we can lose it at any time. That's just on the business side," she told The Atlantic. Then the mission: "Are we accidentally making things worse? Like, we think we have all the safety stuff figured out, but do we?" Fear of loss and doubt about her own conclusions are both Six signatures, and her career has run alongside one trusted figure.

The lean toward One rests on what her checks are for. Hers, in her telling, aim at error more than at threat: feedback on what each colleague "could be doing better," reviews that include her, and a "group accountability" of Dario, the other co-founders and staff who may disagree with them, even anonymously, so that "if we start to stray from the path we're at least going to know." That is a thin distinction, and Six stays live.

**Type 9** fits her give with Dario and her habit of handing disputes back to the people in them ("I probably shouldn't even be the arbiter," she said of technical leads who disagreed). It fails on her insistence that colleagues give each other feedback rather than none, and on flat judgments such as her verdict on AI romantic companions, in an [Arianna Huffington interview posted in October 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IEQJmtTqY2g): "I think that's a terrible use of AI and people should not do it." **Type 3** is less likely: she volunteers her limits ("I'm not the expert in anything," she told Stanford students in 2024) and, below, her mistakes.

## What the One reading leaves out

She is easy on herself in public. Anthropic ran payroll, "which like we messed up multiple times," she told [Notion's *First Block*](https://www.notion.com/blog/first-block-with-daniela-amodei) in 2023. Her plan to give each weekday its own focus has "probably like 70% hit rate." Among the memoir titles she said resonated with her, one was her own, not Claude's: "I need an adult." Ones can be self-deprecating in public, and an inner critic isn't observable from outside. Still, there's no visible strain.

She is pragmatic about values. Anthropic retired one ("move fast and safe things") because "not everyone loved it," she said on Vanta, and "if not everyone loves it," then "people are not going to use it." She wants candidates who can say "I changed my mind because I actually realized, you know, I wasn't right about this."

She is charitable about other people's errors. Describing a serious infection that three sets of doctors misdiagnosed, she said: "this is not because any of those are bad doctors, right? They're really busy." ([Huffington, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IEQJmtTqY2g))

She keeps her distance from a label her record invites. Of effective altruism, the movement whose affiliated sources supplied most of Anthropic's first funding, she told Wired in 2025: "I don't identify with that terminology. My impression is that it's a bit of an outdated term." Her husband co-founded "one of EA's most conspicuous philanthropy wings," Wired noted, and Dario wrote in [January 2026](https://darioamodei.com/essay/the-adolescence-of-technology) that "All of Anthropic's co-founders have pledged to donate 80% of our wealth," a pledge rather than a record of gifts. Principled objection to a label, or reputation management? The record can't settle it.

And she keeps her irritations private. On *Grit*, asked about her own working-with-me guide, "what's the thing that triggers you," she named what lights her up and what she's less good at, and no trigger.

A One reading has to treat that quiet as control or privacy, not as proof. The only anger on record is reported by others. The Atlantic, citing "several people who know them well," reported in September 2026 that the siblings "still despise Altman and Brockman." The non-disparagement agreement may explain her public restraint as well.

So what has the pattern cost her? On the public record, mostly relationships: a friendship with her former boss that, by unnamed-source accounts, ended in mutual hostility, and a reputation among OpenAI rivals as inflexible. In her own interviews, the cost is hard to see.

The leaving was harder than her "running towards something" line suggests. Before the seven left OpenAI, she recalls, they wondered: "Maybe we should stay. Maybe we could make this work." She called a friend and mentor outside work, who told her she didn't really need the call.

> "You already know what the right answer is."

She named it the best advice she has ever received.
