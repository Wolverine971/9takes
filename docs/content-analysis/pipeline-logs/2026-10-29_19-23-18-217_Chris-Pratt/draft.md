In 2015, GQ's Chris Heath asked Chris Pratt about his weight. Pratt had spent years as Andy Dwyer, the friendly doofus of _Parks and Recreation_, then rebuilt his body to play Star-Lord in _Guardians of the Galaxy_. He said his wife at the time, Anna Faris, had been "so okay with it" and had probably preferred him heavier. Heath asked why. [Pratt answered](https://www.gq.com/story/chris-pratt-cover-story):

> "I think I was outwardly having more fun. I was more fun to be around, probably. That image that I was casting, to convince people that I was okay, was a really fun person to hang around. Now I have less fun. I focus more."

Then he laughed: "I was like a great pet fat guy."

The joke softens a strange admission. Andy was one of the happiest characters on network television, and Pratt calls that happiness an image he was casting, then trades it away in two short sentences. In his own telling, fun is also something he learned to sell, as a teenager in a coupon-sales operation whose mornings began with a chant: PMA equals OPM.

Christopher Michael Pratt was born on June 21, 1979, in Virginia, Minnesota, and grew up mostly in Lake Stevens, Washington, a wrestling-mad town outside Seattle. He broke through on _Everwood_ in 2002, played Andy from 2009 to 2015, and became a leading man as Star-Lord in _Guardians of the Galaxy_ (2014) and Owen Grady in _Jurassic World_ (2015). He produces and stars in Prime Video's _The Terminal List_, whose second season premiered on October 21, 2026.

## What is Chris Pratt's personality type?

Chris Pratt reads as Enneagram Type 3, the Achiever, at medium confidence. Threes are heart-triad types who handle shame by becoming what earns approval. In his own accounts, from a failed sales job at 19 to the Andy Dwyer years, when fun and looking successful pulled apart, he protected the look. Type 7 is the strongest alternative.

A [Type 3](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-3) reads a room for what will be admired and becomes it, often so smoothly that the effort disappears. Ambition proves little in Hollywood, where everyone has it. The sharper tell is managing the impression when no camera is running, and treating your own fun as part of the presentation.

The strongest rival is [Type 7](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-7), the Enthusiast, which the pranks and appetites make the obvious first guess. The two separate when fun and image pull in different directions. A Seven protects the fun and reframes the loss. Pratt's record, mostly in his own words, protects the image and pays in fun.

## "What you're selling is yourself"

By his senior year of high school, Pratt was wrestling, playing football, starring in plays and writing assembly sketches. His wrestling coach asked what he planned to do with his life. His answer, as [Vanity Fair relayed it](https://www.vanityfair.com/hollywood/2017/01/chris-pratt-cover-story) from an Entertainment Weekly interview: "I was like, 'I don't know, but I know I'll be famous and I know I'll make a shit ton of money.'"

Pressed by Vanity Fair's Rich Cohen for the actual plan, Pratt talked about his father, a former high-school football star who lived off that glory for life. "I guess that's what I planned to do," he said. It was a boast retold for laughs, but the order is telling: fame and money first, the route undecided.

He dropped out of community college, answered a newspaper ad and started selling coupon books door to door. Cohen summarized Pratt's view that a salesman has only one product, himself. "I was great at that," Pratt said. On the podcast [_Not Dead Yet_](https://api.spreaker.com/download/episode/71008192/not_dead_yet_chris_pratt.mp3) in March 2026, he put the lesson in his own words: "regardless of the product you're selling, what you're selling is yourself and you're selling your attitude." The company's morning chant, he said, was "PMA equals OPM, which was positive mental attitude equals other people's money." He called it "terrible."

He sold well enough to win the company's prize, an office of his own outside Denver, before he turned 21. Managing it was another matter. "I was too much of a people pleaser to be a good manager," he said in 2026. He fell into debt and called his boss: "I'm more in debt every month. I'm so depressed. I can't do it." His mother sent him a ticket home. He calls the venture "definitely a failure."

What happened next matters most for typing, because no camera was involved. Back in Lake Stevens, he told Vanity Fair:

> "I had everyone convinced I'd been off doing this great sales job and making money, and I wasn't. I had no prospects, no job, was still sort of riding the glory of high school."

In 2026 he described the homecoming from the inside. He had been "kind of like the star of my hometown," and now "I just felt like super depressed."

Plenty of 19-year-olds hide a failure. Pratt talks freely about the depression; what he hid was the result, and he showed success in its place until a friend saw through it and bought him a ticket to Maui. The account is his own, and "everyone" is a storyteller's exaggeration. It is still the clearest case of protecting how he was seen with nothing professional at stake.

Money sat under the pride. His family lost their house while he was in high school, a loss he ties to his father's multiple sclerosis. "I've been money motivated my whole life because we never had anything," he said in 2026. His example puts status and hidden need in one sentence: "I was the king of my school and I don't have a yearbook because it was $35 and I just wouldn't ask them for that much money."

## Maui, told three ways

In Maui, Pratt lived in a van and a tent on the beach and waited tables at Bubba Gump Shrimp. He has told that year more than one way.

In 2014, promoting _Guardians_, he told [The Independent](https://nzherald.co.nz/entertainment/chris-pratt-gets-in-shape-and-takes-over-the-galaxy/OCBW2TTH64HG4ZNUJLXWWGSHMY), "It's a pretty awesome place to be homeless," and called the drinking, weed and short shifts "a charming time." In 2017 he gave Vanity Fair a darker arrival: "My friends picked me up in a van. They had a cooler of beer. But I was not in a great place."

Both can be true, and the interviewers asked different questions. Still, the emphasis tracks the room: an adventure for a blockbuster junket, a rescue for a cover story about finding his path.

The rescue came twice. Outside a grocery store, a stranger named Henry "came up and recognized something in me that needed to be saved," Pratt told Vanity Fair. Henry said Jesus had told him to talk to Pratt. "At that moment I was like, I think I have to go with this guy." Henry took him to church, and Pratt told his friends he was going to change his life.

Then, at Bubba Gump, where he was named "Gumper of the year," he waited on the actor and director Rae Dawn Chong. Asked whether he acted, he said yes and, in Vanity Fair's reconstruction, added, "Put me in a movie." In 2026 he called it his door-to-door sales training kicking in. Her never-released film _Cursed Part 3_ brought him to Los Angeles.

## Where Andy came from

Pratt's father, Dan, worked in mines and remodeled homes. "He was from the old school. He was a boxer and a bouncer," Pratt told [Men's Health](https://www.menshealth.com/entertainment/a40289867/chris-pratt-interview-new-movies-series/) in 2022, and he could be "mean." His son was built differently:

> "I am a sensitive person. My dad knew that when I was a youngster, and it kind of made him dislike me. Or not dislike me but act like he disliked me—'cause he probably grew up in a world where a guy like that could get eaten alive. And so he wanted to put calluses on me. Early on, I developed humor as a self-defense mechanism—I developed Andy, really."

This is Pratt's own causal story about a father who died in 2014 after years of multiple sclerosis he chose not to treat, and no other family member is on the record. Taken at his word, the clown began as a way to be acceptable to a man who found sensitivity dangerous. Andy, Pratt said, was "a guy who is affable, who's an intelligent person playing a dumb person."

The other model was his older brother, Cully. Pratt told [Rob Lowe](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hICQlA32BWM) in 2026 that he wrestled and got into acting because his brother did. As he told Vanity Fair: "One Christmas, he was in a play, a musical, and sang, and it knocked everyone's socks off. My mom was crying. And I was like, 'That's what I want to do.'"

Little brothers copy big brothers in every type. What stands out is what the boy wanted, in the man's telling: the effect. A room knocked out, and his mother in tears.

## The dumb fat guy was a business plan

In Los Angeles, Pratt spent years being cast, he says, as "the bad boyfriend." Early in the second season of _Parks and Recreation_, he saw on screen that he was gaining weight. He told GQ his next thought:

> "And then immediately I was like, 'This is the funniest...' I was making myself laugh. I was: 'That's where it's at. There's no one doing that. No one being like a super-confident dumb fat guy.' So I started."

He told the showrunner he wanted to get bigger. The laughs kept coming, and he thought, "Oh great, I found my niche." It was, he added, "paying better" than the bad-boyfriend parts.

The weight came with real appetite; as he explained to GQ, the highlights of his days were the eating and drinking. His reason for keeping it, though, is commercial. It was funny, nobody else was doing it, and it paid.

The persona had strategy behind it. Of the whole dumb-guy act, he told Men's Health, "It was absolutely a tactic to lower people's expectations of me." The magazine reported that Pratt had written Andy's long, apparently improvised list of names for his band, Mouse Rat, far in advance. He once joked to GQ about hiding his homework altogether: "Maybe that's my next lesson—just stop talking about it and be like, 'Oh, I don't know what it is.... It's just a gift, I guess.'"

Then the persona stopped paying. Auditioning for _Moneyball_, he heard for the first time, "We're not gonna cast you—you're too fat." He told Vanity Fair: "So I decided to drop the weight, like in wrestling." With no money for a trainer, that meant running, crash diets and no alcohol. He got the part. Bulking up to play a Navy SEAL in _Zero Dark Thirty_ convinced him he could carry action: "I was like, My God, I buy that guy." Describing his _Guardians_ preparation, he told The Independent in 2014, "I need to look like a superhero."

That is the context for "Now I have less fun. I focus more." The fun persona paid until it cost him a role. Then he dropped it, and by his own description much of the fun went with it.

## "You make them think you already are that guy"

Pratt told Vanity Fair he first passed on _Guardians_, and James Gunn passed on him. His agent pushed: "Guardians is everything you've been saying you want to do." Pratt went in and "played the room." He described the method:

> "You walk into the room as the character. You let them think the person you are is close to the character they want. You make them think you already are that guy."

That is common audition advice. What stands out is how long Pratt was better at the selling than the job. On the [_SmartLess_ podcast](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BRa2Vx-3A8Q) in 2022 he joked that for years he was great at auditioning and couldn't act, and that the person who had won the role was really a door-to-door salesman.

Once Pratt had the part, Gunn saw the habit from the other side. "I think that he had gotten a lot of success over the past few years being the funny sidekick," the director told GQ, "which means that he always has to juggle to entertain everybody. And the truth is, Chris didn't trust himself to just be Chris Pratt—and how desirable that is to an audience." Pratt agreed: "I thought I was bad." At the time, he said, his reaction to his own takes was "This screams inauthentic to me."

Gunn is a friend describing one film. His diagnosis still names the Three's central problem: fluent in becoming what the audience wants, unsure the unperformed version would be enough.

The wish to be liked was rehearsed, by his own account. Before his first talk shows, Pratt told Rob Lowe, he dreamed about going on Jay Leno: "Would I be funny? Would people like me?" He said he "would rehearse it over and over in my head," and that experience made it matter less. Being overlooked stung. In 2015 he remembered being "like the guy holding the purse at events and people just looked right through me," and joked that he hoped the actors who ignored him would audition for _Guardians_.

Self-promotion took a push. On [_Happy Sad Confused_](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SSjd73MXaqg) in January 2026, Pratt said his brother talked him into posting an underwear photo of the body he had built for _Zero Dark Thirty_, which he thought seemed weird. Later, he said, Marvel's Kevin Feige pulled out that photo and asked whether he could get back in that shape.

One line touches his private life. Describing on her podcast in 2021 her own habit of not discussing relationship trouble, Faris said, as [Good Morning America reported](https://www.goodmorningamerica.com/culture/story/anna-faris-reflects-divorce-chris-pratt-77483269), "with Chris, I think that we both protected that imagery even within our close circles." She said "we both," and was mostly describing herself, but it is the only outside testimony here about the private side of the pattern.

## Why not Type 7?

The case for Seven is real, and much of it comes from Pratt. As a child, he told GQ, he was "obnoxious, really hyperactive, disruptive," a jokester suspended from his high-school track team for getting naked on the bus. "I liked making people feel uncomfortable," he said. On _Parks and Recreation_ he did one take of a doorway gag fully naked to shock [Amy Poehler](/personality-analysis/amy-poehler), and NBC's HR sent him a letter. Gunn says Pratt has "a new idea every day about everything." He called a homeless year "a charming time."

The deeper Seven argument is about pain. Sevens use fun to keep pain out of the room, and Pratt says his humor began as a defense against a disapproving father. Read that way, "to convince people that I was okay" is a Seven describing his own mask.

The readings split on one question: when fun and the appearance of success point in different directions, which does he keep? A Seven keeps the fun and reframes what it cost. Pratt's record keeps the appearance:

- At 19, sinking into debt, he kept up the story of a successful sales career for the people back home.
- Around 30, he chose the fat-guy persona because it got laughs and was "paying better," then traded it for running and crash diets once it cost him a role.
- He describes the fun of that period as an image he was casting, and the change as "Now I have less fun. I focus more."
- A director who knows him well saw a performer who "always has to juggle to entertain everybody."

One GQ exchange cuts both ways. Retiring early to a ranch to hunt birds, he said, was "what I secretly want to do." Then he predicted that "three months later I would be tired of bird hunting and I would be like '...Guardians Ten?'" A Seven gets bored. A Three gets restless without a goal. In Pratt's version, the boredom sends him back to work.

The Three reading has limits. Acting rewards image management in every type, so the dieting alone proves little. The weightiest cases happened off camera, the hidden sales failure and Faris's remark, and each rests on one source. Most of the rest is self-report from promotional interviews, hence medium confidence.

## A vow that costs him

If image explained everything, Pratt would have stopped talking about God years ago. His faith has cost him with the mainstream audiences an image manager would court.

He told [The Christian Post](https://www.christianpost.com/news/chris-pratt-reveals-moment-he-surrendered-his-platform-to-god.html) in 2025 that he went to church altars as a child, at 19 and in his 30s: "I'd make promises, but I didn't keep them." In August 2012 his son Jack was born premature and spent about a month in neonatal intensive care. Pratt says he "made a deal with God again," and after Jack recovered, "That was the moment that I was like, 'Moving forward, I'm going to give my platform to God.'"

At the 2018 MTV Movie & TV Awards he told the audience, "God is real. God loves you. God wants the best for you." He told Men's Health in 2022 that he believes the speech helped light the online hostility toward him, the same hostility behind the recurring "Worst Chris" meme that ranks him against [Chris Hemsworth](/personality-analysis/chris-hemsworth), Chris Evans and Chris Pine.

In 2019 the actor Elliot Page criticized him over his church. Pratt's statement rejected the idea that he belonged to a church that is "infamously anti-LGBTQ" and said "everyone is entitled to love who they want free from the judgment of their fellow man." In 2022 he said he had never been to Hillsong, the church he had been linked to, and attends Zoe Church, whose founding pastor, Men's Health reported, executive-produced a 2017 film that equated "sexual brokenness" with "same-sex attraction." Asked why he hadn't corrected the Hillsong link at the time, Pratt said, "I'm gonna, like, throw a church under the bus?"

The same interview held the line critics seized on: "I didn't know that I would kind of become the face of religion when really I'm not a religious person." He separated religion as a human institution from belief in what he called "a very real God." The Daily Beast headlined its response "Why Is Chris Pratt Lying About His Religious Past?"

In 2025, during a Lent partnership with the Catholic prayer app Hallow, the emphasis ran the other way. He told The Christian Post, "I care enough about Jesus to take a stand, even if it cost me. It could cost me everything, but I don't care."

A skeptic can read this as the Three's instinct applied to faith: one framing for a men's magazine mid-backlash, another for a Christian outlet mid-promotion. The fairer reading holds both. He separates religion from following Jesus, so the statements conflict less than the headline suggests, and he has kept the commitment public through years of cost. This is the strand the Three reading explains least.

His stated policy on critics is Proverbs 26:20, "For lack of wood, the fire will go out." He hasn't always kept to it. In 2022, at his own prompting, he worked through the backlash at length with Men's Health ("Why are they coming after me?") and said he cried over coverage he feared his son, then nine, would read one day.

By 2026 he had a theory of the whole system. "I work in a business where we create idols and we worship them," he said on _Not Dead Yet_, "and no one is worthy of worship. And so not only do we create idols to worship, we create idols to burn." If he is a Three, this is a Three naming the trap his type is built to walk into, and placing his worth outside it.

## What the type doesn't explain

Other people make his biggest moves. His mother bought the ticket home, a friend the ticket to Maui, and a stranger took him to church. His brother, who joined the army, told him not to enlist: "I think he saw something in me." His agent pushed the _Guardians_ audition; his brother pushed the photo. In 2026 he said his manager has had him turn down "huge checks," and that "she's calculated and critical in a way that I'm not." A Three usually wants to be the strategist. Following the people who see something in him reads more like [Type 9](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-9), the Peacemaker, and so does "too much of a people pleaser."

He also says he wants a smaller life than his career suggests. On _SmartLess_ he said he doesn't want to spend his time building an empire and would rather work to live, and that the lake on his Washington farm takes up much of his mind. In 2015 he told GQ, "I have a feeling I'll be tested and I'll have to prove to him that he's more important to me than show business," meaning his son. In late 2016 he took a break from work, partly, Vanity Fair reported, on similar advice from Jim Carrey.

By 2026 he described the reordering directly. During his first marriage, "our priority here is our careers," he said, and "it costs you something." Now: "I still have a priority of my career being very high, but it's not as high as the priority of being present at home." He frames fatherhood through his own father. "I basically want to get right everything my dad got right and get right everything my dad got wrong," he told [Fox News Digital](https://www.foxnews.com/entertainment/chris-pratt-says-his-faith-the-most-important-thing-he-passes-his-kids) in October 2026. "And I'm sure I'll screw up both, you know?"

A Three can sincerely want a quieter life while living a louder one, and he still calls his career a very high priority. But the tears in interviews, the reluctance about the photo, the faith commitment and the habit of handing big decisions to trusted people belong to the portrait, and the type does not explain them away.

One detail from 2026 brings the story back to its start. After _Mercy_, the thriller he led in January 2026, became a critical and commercial failure, he said that he, his manager and his agent had settled on a direction: "we're going to steer more towards trying to do more comedy, trying to bring fun and lightheartedness back." That may simply be the market talking. It is also the 2015 move in reverse. Eleven years after "Now I have less fun. I focus more," the fun is back on the plan, chosen in a meeting by a man who learned as a teenage salesman that the attitude is part of what you sell.
