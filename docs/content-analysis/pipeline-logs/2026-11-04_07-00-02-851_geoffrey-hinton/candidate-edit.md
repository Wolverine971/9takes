On May 1, 2023, _The New York Times_ reported that Geoffrey Hinton, the computer scientist often called the godfather of AI, had quit Google so he could speak freely about the risks of the technology he helped build ([NYT](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/05/01/technology/ai-google-chatbot-engineer-quits-hinton.html)). The story handed him a platform.

The same day, he used it to correct the paper.

> "In the NYT today, Cade Metz implies that I left Google so that I could criticize Google. Actually, I left so that I could talk about the dangers of AI without considering how this impacts Google. Google has acted very responsibly."

That is his post on Twitter, as reproduced verbatim by [Notebookcheck](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Geoffrey-Hinton-leaves-Google-warns-about-AI-risks.712693.0.html). The Times had not invented its angle; the paper reported that Hinton "did not want to publicly criticize Google or other companies until he had quit his job." His post narrowed that implication in Google's favor and gave up the more flattering story, the whistleblower walking out on a powerful company. Goodwill toward an employer of ten years explains it plainly enough. But it is the first of several moments in the record where he would not let a slightly wrong account stand, even one that flattered him.

Hinton, born in London on December 6, 1947 ([Nobel Prize facts](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2024/hinton/facts/)), spent about fifty years arguing that networks of artificial neurons could learn the way brains do, through long stretches when most of his field thought the idea was a dead end. He co-wrote the 1986 paper showing how backpropagation, a procedure that lets a network learn from its own errors, could train such networks ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2023/05/02/1072528/geoffrey-hinton-google-why-scared-ai/)). In 2012 he and two students built AlexNet, an image-recognition network whose success helped set off the deep-learning boom behind today's AI. He shared computing's Turing Award with Yoshua Bengio and Yann LeCun, and in 2024 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physics with John Hopfield "for foundational discoveries and inventions that enable machine learning with artificial neural networks."

Since leaving Google he has turned the warning into a second career. On September 16, 2026, after a closed-door briefing for members of Congress organized by Sen. [Bernie Sanders](/personality-analysis/bernie-sanders), he told reporters lawmakers had "Maybe a year, but not much more than a year" to act on AI before it got away from them ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnewyork.com/news/national-international/geoffrey-hinton-congress-ai-regulation/6549094/)).

The usual picture of Hinton is a brilliant mind that wanted to understand the brain. That is true. But his most telling choices follow a second rule, and it shows up far from AI: when Hinton decides something is wrong, he cannot leave it alone.

## What is Geoffrey Hinton's personality type?

Geoffrey Hinton's personality type is most likely Enneagram Type 1, the Reformer. Ones live by an inner standard of how things should be. Hinton's most telling choices, from leaving Google rather than criticize it on its payroll to calling his advocacy a duty, follow a standard about conduct, not curiosity alone. Confidence is medium; Type 5 is the serious rival.

[Type 1](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-1) is one of the gut types, for whom anger is the central problem. Eights let it out, Nines go numb to it, and Ones convert it into correction: a quiet, steady sense that this is not how it should be, followed by the urge to fix it. In Hinton's own voice the anger runs low: "unfortunate," "ridiculous," "just crazy." That low setting is one reason the read is One rather than Eight. He leaves institutions instead of trying to run them, and his corrections defend accuracy or other people more than his own position.

The read is held at medium confidence. Much of the record is Hinton describing himself after being proven right, which flatters a story about standing alone, and fame, not just temperament, puts him in front of lawmakers. Some of the pattern predates the fame. In 2023 the Times reported that he left Carnegie Mellon for Canada in the 1980s because, he said, he was "reluctant to take Pentagon funding," and that he is "deeply opposed" to AI on the battlefield. His other accounts of the move credit his wife Rosalind's loathing of Ronald Reagan (New Yorker, 2023) and Canada's funding for basic research ([Global News, 2019](https://globalnews.ca/news/5929564/geoffrey-hinton-artificial-intelligence-toronto/)). The strongest rival, [Type 5](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-5), has real evidence in his own words: he defines himself by a question about how minds work. It gets a full hearing below, along with Types 3 and [6](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6).

## A box marked "Not insects"

His father, Howard Everest Hinton, was an entomologist who set the bar out of reach. The family had set it first: a great-great-grandfather was George Boole, whose algebra of binary logic is fundamental to computing ([New Yorker](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2023/11/20/geoffrey-hinton-profile-ai)). In that 2023 profile, Hinton recalled his father saying, "If you work twice as hard as me, when you're twice as old as I am you might be half as good."

"He knew a lot more about beetles than he knew about people," Hinton told [60 Minutes](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/geoffrey-hinton-ai-dangers-60-minutes-transcript/) the same year. When his father died, Hinton went to his study at the university. The walls were lined with boxes of papers on different kinds of beetle. "And just near the door there was a slightly smaller box that simply said, 'Not insects,' and that's where he had all the things about the family."

The father also forbade him to study biology, the New Yorker reports, for ideological reasons: Howard Hinton was a committed Communist, and genetics cut against the belief that human nature could be remade. Remembering that, Hinton said: "I hate faiths of all kinds."

None of this proves a type; a father like that could produce a striver, a worrier or a perfectionist. What it shows is a split Hinton draws himself, between the work he had to do and the work he chose. "There was a lot of pressure to succeed academically, so I kind of knew from a very early age that I had to be a successful academic or a failure," he said in a [Nobel Week interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=66WiF8fXL0k&t=135s) in December 2024.

At Cambridge, the New Yorker reports, he "was dismayed to find that he was never the brightest student in any given class," and he tried architecture "for about a day" before settling on experimental psychology. Building stayed the other kind of work. "I've often wondered whether I'd have been happier as an architect, because I didn't have to force myself to do it," he told the magazine.

> "Whereas, with science, I've always had to force myself. Because of the family, I had to succeed at it—I had to find a path. There was joy in it, but it was mostly anxiety. Now it's an enormous relief that I've succeeded."

On the night of the Nobel banquet he put it more simply: "there was never any pressure with carpentry. I'm not very good at it, but I enjoy it" ([SVT interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qlEMrGwIWuM&t=65s)).

By his own account, the science that changed the world ran mostly on anxiety.

## "It was just completely obvious to me"

Ask Hinton where his independence comes from and he goes back to school. "My parents were atheists and sent me to a Christian school and I was the only person who didn't believe in God. As time went by, it turned out I was right," he told SVT.

Asked by 60 Minutes when he realized he had been right about neural networks, he said: "I always thought I was right." Of the decades when the method barely worked, he told the New Yorker: "I was always convinced it wasn't nonsense. It wasn't really faith—it was just completely obvious to me."

His advice to young scientists, given in December 2024, is the same rule. If an idea seems right to you and differs from what everyone else believes, "don't give up on it until you've figured out why it's wrong," he said in the Nobel Week interview, adding: "you should just ignore what other people say. I'm very good at ignoring what other people say."

That could describe any stubborn genius, and on its own it does not separate a One from a Five. Both can be sure of themselves against a room.

His favorite algorithm shows where the certainty bends. Hinton loved the Boltzmann Machine, the learning algorithm he built with Terry Sejnowski in the 1980s. "It should be true," he told the New Yorker. "If I was God, I'd make it true." Yann LeCun, his former student and collaborator, remembered the disagreement: "Geoff thought it was the most beautiful algorithm. I thought it was ugly." When Yoshua Bengio trained a Boltzmann Machine for his master's thesis, Hinton, an outside reviewer, wrote "something like 'This should not work.'" It worked.

Those are a theorist's "shoulds," a taste in models and a prediction, and a Five's love of an elegant theory explains them as well as a One's standard does.

Meanwhile Hinton was building the method that would beat it. Even as he worked with Sejnowski, he was collaborating with David Rumelhart and Ronald Williams on backprop, and in 1986 the three published their paper in Nature. Both methods were his. The one he loved lost. "It's so unsatisfying intellectually," he said of backprop. "Unfortunately, it just works better."

Decades later the unsatisfying method frightened him. For forty years, MIT Technology Review's Will Douglas Heaven wrote in May 2023, Hinton had seen artificial neural networks as a poor attempt to mimic biological ones; now he thought they had become better. "It's scary when you see that," Hinton said. "It's a sudden flip."

Letting the beautiful algorithm go for the one that worked is the Five's move: the model yields to the data. The evidence for a One lies somewhere else, in how he talks about conduct rather than theories.

## Notes written on the shelf

In 2023, the New Yorker's Joshua Rothman spent four days with Hinton on his island in Georgian Bay, Ontario. "On the island, he is always cutting down trees to create more orderly and beautiful tableaus," Rothman wrote. The contractors Hinton hired for the house "made needless mistakes (running a drainage pipe uphill, leaving floors half finished) that still enrage him today."

"Almost every room harbors a corrective mini-project," Rothman reported. Hinton had left notes for the next contractor, often written on the materials themselves. A baseboard propped in a bathroom: "Bathroom should have this type of baseboard (maple trim in front of shower only)." Masking tape along a closet shelf: "Do not prime shelf, prime shelf support."

This rests on one reporter's visit, and an engineer's precision about a building is not unusual. The tell, if there is one, is not the notes but the irritation at other people's carelessness that outlived the mistakes. "Enrage" is Rothman's word, not Hinton's.

Even his tenderness gets an edit. When his third wife, Jackie, was ill with pancreatic cancer and could no longer manage the cottage stairs, he built a small basket on a string so she could lower her tea to the floor below, where he would warm it in the microwave. Telling the story, he added: "I should've just moved the microwave upstairs."

## Room 731

In December 2012, Hinton ran an auction for a three-person company, DNNresearch, from room 731 of Harrah's at Lake Tahoe. The other two people in the company were his students Alex Krizhevsky and [Ilya Sutskever](/personality-analysis/ilya-sutskever), who had built AlexNet with him. Cade Metz reconstructed the week for his book _Genius Makers_, excerpted in [Wired](https://www.wired.com/story/secret-auction-race-ai-supremacy-google-microsoft-baidu/).

He gave his reason for selling in 2025, on [The Diary of a CEO](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=giT0ytynSqg&t=4272s): "I have a son who has learning difficulties and in order to be sure he would never be out on the street I needed to get several million dollars and I wasn't going to get that as an academic."

Two details from Metz's account point in opposite directions. When a bidder from Baidu left his backpack in the room, Hinton and his students wondered whether to look inside for a hint of how high Baidu would go. "But knowing it wasn't the right thing to do, they didn't," Metz writes. Then, as the price hit $44 million, Hinton stopped the auction and sold to Google. In Metz's reading, "finding the right home for his research was ultimately more important to him than commanding the maximum price." Both lines are Metz's narration, not Hinton's words.

The second detail is less flattering. Afraid of getting sick in the dry mountain air, Hinton had rigged a humidifying chamber from a mattress, an ironing board and wet towels, and he had his students hide it whenever the Baidu executive came by. "I didn't want them thinking I was old and decrepit," he told Metz. "This is what vice presidents do," he told his students.

That is a man managing how he looks to buyers, a point for image (Type 3), not for Type 1.

## "It just feels wrong to me"

Hinton left Google in spring 2023, at 75. Days before the announcement he told [MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2023/05/02/1072528/geoffrey-hinton-google-why-scared-ai/): "I want to talk about AI safety issues without having to worry about how it interacts with Google's business. As long as I'm paid by Google, I can't do that."

Two years later he gave a fuller and less heroic account to Steven Bartlett. "The main reason I left Google was because I was 75 and I wanted to retire. I've done a very bad job of that," he said. He was also "making many more mistakes when I programmed, which is very annoying." Google, he said, had encouraged him to stay and work on safety.

> "You kind of censor yourself if you work for a big company, you don't feel right saying things that will damage the big company. Even if you could get away with it, it just feels wrong to me."

That sentence is the type argument in miniature. He had permission to stay. What he could not do was criticize an employer while taking its money, whether or not anyone would notice.

The same rule kept him quiet for a decade. Margaret Mitchell, a former leader of Google's AI ethics team, said in May 2023 that she was upset Hinton had not spoken out during his years in a position of power at Google, especially after the company's 2020 ouster of the researcher Timnit Gebru ([AP via Fortune](https://fortune.com/2023/05/03/ai-scientists-dire-warnings-mixed-opinions/)). On CNN on May 2, 2023, Jake Tapper asked whether, looking back, he wished he had stood behind whistleblowers such as Gebru more. Hinton said "they were rather different concerns from mine. I think it's easier to voice concerns if you leave the company first" ([CNN transcript](https://transcripts.cnn.com/show/cg/date/2023-05-02/segment/01)).

Read generously, that is the employer rule again. Read skeptically, it shows a selective reflex, one that fires at the wrongs he ranks as serious, and more freely late in a career, when speaking out costs less. Either way it narrows the rule this profile started with. Hinton does not chase every wrong. He will not let go of the ones his own standard picks out.

He calls what came next a duty. "So I feel I have a duty now to talk about the risks," he told Bartlett in 2025. On the day of his Nobel, at a University of Toronto press conference, he said: "I'm going to spend my time advocating for people to work on safety."

His judgments of other people come out the same way, flat and moral. At that press conference he said: "I'm particularly proud of the fact that one of my students fired Sam Altman" ([U of T press conference](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H7DgMFqrON0&t=195s)). He meant Ilya Sutskever, then OpenAI's chief scientist, who helped lead the concerns behind the board's removal of [Sam Altman](/personality-analysis/sam-altman) as chief executive on November 17, 2023. The board's stated reason was that Altman had not been "consistently candid in his communications." Sutskever later apologized publicly for his part, and Altman was reinstated on November 22; an outside investigation concluded in March 2024 that his conduct "did not mandate removal" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Removal_of_Sam_Altman_from_OpenAI)). Asked to elaborate, Hinton gave his own view: "over time, it turned out that Sam Altman was much less concerned with safety than with profits. And I think that's unfortunate."

One wrong he returns to is a promise dropped when it becomes expensive. In December 2024 he backed a legal brief opposing OpenAI's proposed move away from nonprofit control. "OpenAI was founded as an explicitly safety-focused nonprofit and made a variety of safety-related promises in its charter," he said in a statement quoted by [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/12/30/nonprofit-group-joins-elon-musks-effort-to-block-openais-for-profit-transition/). "Allowing it to tear all of that up when it becomes inconvenient sends a very bad message to other actors in the ecosystem." The plan he objected to did not go through as proposed. On May 5, 2025, OpenAI said its nonprofit would stay in control ([Reuters via CNN](https://krdo.com/news/2025/05/05/openai-to-remain-under-non-profit-control-in-change-of-restructuring-plans/)), and on October 28, 2025, it converted its business into a public benefit corporation that remains under the nonprofit's control ([AP](https://globalnews.ca/news/11498213/openai-chatgpt-for-profit-business-structure)).

## Why not Type 5?

Asked on the morning of the Nobel how he would describe himself, Hinton said: "I would say I am someone who doesn't really know what field he's in but would like to understand how the brain works" ([Nobel Prize interview](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2024/hinton/interview/)). That is a Five's sentence: identity organized around a question, not a role. He theorizes even feeling. "I think feelings are counterfactual statements about what would have caused an action," he told the New Yorker. His fear of AI arrived through a model, not a mood: "The closest I had to a eureka moment was when a Google system called Palm was able to say why a joke was funny," he told Bartlett.

He also admits mistakes without visible pain. In 2016 he predicted AI would be reading all the scans radiologists read. "That prediction was wrong. I was a bit overenthusiastic," he said in 2024. A [Type 5](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-5) treats a wrong model as information. So does Hinton.

On a first read, Five is the obvious answer, and speaking out does not rule it out; plenty of Fives are fierce public critics. The difference is the voice. A Five speaks from the authority of the model: here is what the evidence shows. Hinton has that voice, but when he acts he reaches for a second one, about conduct. He would not criticize an employer on its payroll, even if he could get away with it. He calls his advocacy a duty. He objected to OpenAI's plan not as a mistake but as promises torn up "when it becomes inconvenient." His irritation, where the record shows it, runs at carelessness: the contractors' (on Rothman's 2023 account) and his own aging code. That is how a One's anger usually shows: held down, leaking out as correction. Curiosity explains why he studied the brain for fifty years. It does not explain the heat, low as it usually runs.

Some evidence supports the One reading without separating it from Five. Precision about the record is a Five's habit too, and principled people of every type keep promises. Some Enneagram teachers would say a healthy Five grows more decisive and outspoken, which could cover the activism. Fame creates demand. The home details come from a single visit. And his decade of silence at Google, including after Gebru's ouster, means the conduct standard binds him selectively, which weakens the One case as a description of everything he does even if it survives as an account of what he chooses to act on.

Type 3 has a case too: the household where he would be "a successful academic or a failure," the "enormous relief that I've succeeded," the humidifier hidden from Baidu. It ranks below Five because a Three guards the image, and Hinton's best-known public moments go the other way. He narrowed the Times's flattering whistleblower story the day it ran, and he later volunteered that the main reason he left Google was that he was 75 and wanted to retire.

[Type 6](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6) is the third serious candidate, in its contrarian, counterphobic form: the lone unbeliever at school, "I hate faiths of all kinds," science as "mostly anxiety," a company sold so his son would never be out on the street. He told Bartlett he spreads his money and his children's money between three Canadian banks in case a cyberattack takes one down. What tells against it is direction. A counterphobic Six tests itself by pushing against authority. Hinton's disagreements follow his model, and when the model sides with the institution, so does he; he defended Google on the day he quit. He treats his gloom as calibration, not dread. "I'm mildly depressed," he told MIT Technology Review in 2023, and when people estimate the odds of bad things happening, he said, the mildly depressed are "usually right."

## What the One reading leaves out

The Type 1 reading predicts a grim, self-critical man. The people who worked with him describe someone else.

"The excitement, the joy radiated out of his office down the hall," his former student Chris Maddison recalled in 2024 ([U of T](https://web.cs.toronto.edu/news-events/news/from-ai-to-atari-what-it-was-like-to-work-with-nobel-prize-winner-geoffrey-hinton)). "He was famous for bursting into a room and pronouncing that, he now finally, after all these years, understood how the brain worked." These are warm recollections gathered for a celebration, but they do not sound like "mostly anxiety."

He is also funny, in a dry, anti-authority way. He told the New Yorker that "'Geoff' is an anagram for 'ego fortissimo.'" He answered a long note from a Canadian intelligence agency with "Snowden is my hero." Rosemary Gartner, a retired sociologist who had been his partner for four years when the New Yorker visited in 2023, said of him: "I think he's the kind of person who always needs a partner."

About his life's work, at least, the inner critic is quieter than a One's usually is. When he quit Google, the Times wrote that "A part of him, he said, now regrets his life's work," and quoted him: "I console myself with the normal excuse: If I hadn't done it, somebody else would have." By that fall, the New Yorker reported, he had sent a journalist a link to Edith Piaf's "Non, Je Ne Regrette Rien." In 2025 he told Bartlett: "I don't feel particularly guilty about developing AI like 40 years ago because at that time we had no idea that this stuff was gonna happen this fast." The regret he does voice is about timing. After describing the risk of AI taking over, he said in the Nobel Week interview: "so I wish I'd thought about that sooner." Little guilt about the work, some regret about the timing. Duty without guilt is a real pattern, but it is not the self-punishing One of the textbooks.

Where the regret does show is closer to home. "I wish I'd spent more time with my wife. And with my children when they were little. I was kind of obsessed with work," he told Bartlett. He meant Jackie, who, he added, "was very supportive of me spending a lot of time working."

His grief is not guilt-free either. His second wife, Rosalind, was diagnosed with ovarian cancer and pursued homeopathic treatment, a path the New Yorker ties, tentatively, to her struggles with infertility and bad experiences with callous doctors. When she asked him to sell their house to pay for more of it, he refused for the children's sake: "I drew the line there." She died in 1994. Describing it in 2023, Rothman wrote, Hinton still seemed overwhelmed: "angry, guilty, wounded, mystified." Then Hinton made the connection to AI himself. "There are two approaches to A.I. There's denial, and there's stoicism," he said, comparing the urge to stop AI with the urge to cut out a cancer. "We can't be in denial," he said. "We have to be real."

That is the part the One reading predicts least. The lesson he took from loss is acceptance of what cannot be fixed, closer to stoicism than to correction.

## Half a Nobel Prize, and a promise from the 1980s

In the Nobel Week interview, Hinton explained where the money went. Half of his share went to a Canadian organization that trains people living in Indigenous communities to produce safe drinking water. His figure, in December 2024, was that 20 percent of Indigenous communities in Ontario lacked it. "It's ridiculous at this time in a rich country like Canada," he said. "That's just crazy." The reason he gave was personal: "I adopted a child in Peru and I was there for two months and you can't drink the tap water there."

The other half went back to the 1980s. He and Terry Sejnowski had built the Boltzmann Machine together, and they made a deal. "We had an agreement back in the 1980s which was that if they gave it to one of us and not the other one, we'd split it," Hinton said ([Nobel Week interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=66WiF8fXL0k&t=1040s)). When the prize came, partly for Boltzmann Machines, he asked Sejnowski where to send his half. Sejnowski declined, because the prize also covered later work he had not been part of. So they compromised. The money funds a prize in Sejnowski's name "for young researchers with crazy theories of how the brain works, like we were."

It was a promise two young researchers made about a prize neither had any reason to expect, kept forty years later when keeping it cost real money. Later that month, Hinton put his name to a legal fight over another organization's promises, arguing they should not be torn up "when it becomes inconvenient."

The prize carries the rest of him too. It is for the next person who looks, to everyone else in the room, the way he did for decades: wrong, and sure of it.
