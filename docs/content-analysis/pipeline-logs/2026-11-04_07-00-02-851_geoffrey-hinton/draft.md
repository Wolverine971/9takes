On May 1, 2023, _The New York Times_ reported that Geoffrey Hinton, the computer scientist often called the godfather of AI, had quit Google so he could speak freely about the risks of the technology he helped build ([NYT](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/05/01/technology/ai-google-chatbot-engineer-quits-hinton.html)). It was the biggest stage he had ever had for a warning.

The same day, he used it to correct the paper.

> "In the NYT today, Cade Metz implies that I left Google so that I could criticize Google. Actually, I left so that I could talk about the dangers of AI without considering how this impacts Google. Google has acted very responsibly."

That is his post on Twitter, as reproduced verbatim by [Notebookcheck](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Geoffrey-Hinton-leaves-Google-warns-about-AI-risks.712693.0.html). The version he corrected was the more flattering one, the whistleblower walking out on a powerful company. He did not want credit for a story that was not quite true.

Hinton, born in London on December 6, 1947 ([Nobel Prize facts](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2024/hinton/facts/)), spent about fifty years arguing that networks of artificial neurons could learn the way brains do, through long stretches when most of his field thought the idea was a dead end. In 2024 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physics with John Hopfield "for foundational discoveries and inventions that enable machine learning with artificial neural networks." He is back in the news because of what he has done since. On September 16, 2026, after a closed-door briefing for members of Congress organized by Sen. [Bernie Sanders](/personality-analysis/bernie-sanders), he told reporters lawmakers had "Maybe a year, but not much more than a year" to act on AI before it got away from them ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnewyork.com/news/national-international/geoffrey-hinton-congress-ai-regulation/6549094/)).

The usual picture of Hinton is a brilliant mind that wanted to understand the brain. That is true. But his costliest choices follow a second rule, and it shows up far from AI: when Hinton decides something is wrong, he cannot leave it alone.

## What is Geoffrey Hinton's personality type?

Geoffrey Hinton's personality type is most likely Enneagram Type 1, the Reformer. Ones live by an inner standard of how things should be. Hinton's most telling choices, from correcting the Times in Google's defense to keeping a 1980s promise with his Nobel money, follow that standard more than curiosity alone. Confidence is medium; Type 5 is the serious rival.

[Type 1](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-1) sits in the body triad, the three types organized around anger. Eights let it out, Nines go numb to it, and Ones convert it into correction: a quiet, steady sense that this is not how it should be, followed by the urge to fix it. In Hinton's record the heat shows up in odd places: contractors' mistakes that still enrage him, a fixable injustice he calls "just crazy," a rightness that feels to him less like belief than plain sight.

The read is held at medium confidence. Much of the record is Hinton describing himself after being proven right, which flatters a story about standing alone, and fame, not just temperament, puts him in front of lawmakers. The strongest rival, [Type 5](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-5), has real evidence in his own words: he defines himself by a question about how minds work. It gets a full hearing below, along with [Type 6](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6).

## A box marked "Not insects"

His father, Howard Everest Hinton, was an entomologist who set the bar out of reach. In the [New Yorker](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2023/11/20/geoffrey-hinton-profile-ai) in 2023, Hinton recalled him saying, "If you work twice as hard as me, when you're twice as old as I am you might be half as good." He told [60 Minutes](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/geoffrey-hinton-ai-dangers-60-minutes-transcript/) the same year that his father said a version of it every morning as he walked down the driveway to school.

"He knew a lot more about beetles than he knew about people," Hinton said. When his father died, Hinton went to his study at the university. The walls were lined with boxes of papers on different kinds of beetle. "And just near the door there was a slightly smaller box that simply said, 'Not insects,' and that's where he had all the things about the family."

The father also forbade him to study biology, the New Yorker reports, for ideological reasons: Howard Hinton was a committed Communist, and genetics cut against the belief that human nature could be remade. Remembering that, Hinton said: "I hate faiths of all kinds."

None of this proves a type; a father like that could produce a striver, a worrier or a perfectionist. What it shows is a split Hinton draws himself, between the work he had to do and the work he chose. "There was a lot of pressure to succeed academically, so I kind of knew from a very early age that I had to be a successful academic or a failure," he said in a [Nobel Week interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=66WiF8fXL0k&t=135s) in December 2024.

Woodwork was the other kind. "I've often wondered whether I'd have been happier as an architect, because I didn't have to force myself to do it," he told the New Yorker.

> "Whereas, with science, I've always had to force myself. Because of the family, I had to succeed at it—I had to find a path. There was joy in it, but it was mostly anxiety. Now it's an enormous relief that I've succeeded."

On the night of the Nobel banquet he put it more simply: "there was never any pressure with carpentry. I'm not very good at it, but I enjoy it" ([SVT interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qlEMrGwIWuM&t=65s)).

The science that changed the world, by his own account, ran mostly on should.

## "It was just completely obvious to me"

Ask Hinton where his independence comes from and he goes back to school. "My parents were atheists and sent me to a Christian school and I was the only person who didn't believe in God. As time went by, it turned out I was right," he told SVT. Being surrounded by people he thought were wrong, he said, "was very useful when I was studying neural nets."

Scott Pelley of 60 Minutes asked him when he realized he had been right about neural networks and most everyone else wrong. "I always thought I was right," Hinton said. Of the decades when the method barely worked, he told the New Yorker: "I was always convinced it wasn't nonsense. It wasn't really faith—it was just completely obvious to me."

His advice to young scientists, given in December 2024, is the same rule. If an idea seems right to you and differs from what everyone else believes, "don't give up on it until you've figured out why it's wrong," he said in the Nobel Week interview, adding: "you should just ignore what other people say. I'm very good at ignoring what other people say."

That could describe any stubborn genius. What makes it look like a One is the shape of the rightness. It has a "should" in it.

Hinton loved the Boltzmann Machine, the learning algorithm he built with Terry Sejnowski in the 1980s. "It should be true," he told the New Yorker. "If I was God, I'd make it true." Yann LeCun, his former student and collaborator, remembered the disagreement: "Geoff thought it was the most beautiful algorithm. I thought it was ugly." When Yoshua Bengio trained a Boltzmann Machine for his master's thesis, Hinton, an outside reviewer, wrote "something like 'This should not work.'" It worked.

Then the evidence went the other way, and Hinton moved with it. Backpropagation, the less elegant method, won. "It's so unsatisfying intellectually," he said. "Unfortunately, it just works better."

The standard is a picture of how the world ought to work, and it gives way to results. Hinton built his reputation on the algorithm he found unsatisfying.

## Notes written on the shelf

In 2023, the New Yorker's Joshua Rothman spent four days with Hinton on his island in Georgian Bay, Ontario. "On the island, he is always cutting down trees to create more orderly and beautiful tableaus," Rothman wrote. The contractors Hinton hired for the house "made needless mistakes (running a drainage pipe uphill, leaving floors half finished) that still enrage him today."

"Almost every room harbors a corrective mini-project," Rothman reported. Hinton had left notes for the next contractor, often written on the materials themselves. A baseboard propped in a bathroom: "Bathroom should have this type of baseboard (maple trim in front of shower only)." Masking tape along a closet shelf: "Do not prime shelf, prime shelf support."

This rests on one reporter's visit, and an engineer's precision about a building is not unusual. The tell is not the notes. It is the anger that outlives the mistake.

He corrects words the same way. "We should say 'confabulate,'" he told Rothman, when the subject turned to chatbots that make things up. "'Hallucination' is when you think there's sensory input."

Even his tenderness gets an edit. When his third wife, Jackie, was ill with pancreatic cancer and could no longer manage the cottage stairs, he built a small basket on a string so she could lower her tea to the floor below, where he would warm it in the microwave. Telling the story, he added: "I should've just moved the microwave upstairs."

## Room 731

In December 2012, Hinton ran an auction for a three-person company, DNNresearch, from room 731 of Harrah's at Lake Tahoe. The other two people in the company were his students Alex Krizhevsky and [Ilya Sutskever](/personality-analysis/ilya-sutskever), who had built AlexNet with him, a neural network that was, in Hinton's words, "very good at recognizing objects and images." Cade Metz reconstructed the week for his book _Genius Makers_, excerpted in [Wired](https://www.wired.com/story/secret-auction-race-ai-supremacy-google-microsoft-baidu/).

A back injury had long since stopped him sitting down ("I last sat down in 2005," he often said, "and it was a mistake"), so he watched the bids on a laptop set on an upturned trash can.

He gave his reason for selling in 2025, on [The Diary of a CEO](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=giT0ytynSqg&t=4272s): "I have a son who has learning difficulties and in order to be sure he would never be out on the street I needed to get several million dollars and I wasn't going to get that as an academic." He had tried teaching an online course for the money first. "There was no money in that."

Two details from Metz's account point in opposite directions. When a bidder from Baidu left his backpack in the room, Hinton and his students wondered whether to look inside for a hint of how high Baidu would go. "But knowing it wasn't the right thing to do, they didn't," Metz writes. Then, as the price hit $44 million, Hinton stopped the auction and sold to Google. In Metz's reading, "finding the right home for his research was ultimately more important to him than commanding the maximum price." Both lines are Metz's narration, not Hinton's words.

The second detail is less flattering. Afraid of getting sick in the dry mountain air, Hinton had rigged a humidifying chamber from a mattress, an ironing board and wet towels, and he had his students hide it whenever the Baidu executive came by. "I didn't want them thinking I was old and decrepit," he told Metz. "This is what vice presidents do," he told his students.

That is a man managing how he looks to buyers, a point for image (Type 3), not for Type 1.

## "It just feels wrong to me"

Hinton left Google in spring 2023, at 75. Days before the announcement he told [MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2023/05/02/1072528/geoffrey-hinton-google-why-scared-ai/): "I want to talk about AI safety issues without having to worry about how it interacts with Google's business. As long as I'm paid by Google, I can't do that."

Two years later he gave a fuller and less heroic account to Steven Bartlett. "The main reason I left Google was because I was 75 and I wanted to retire. I've done a very bad job of that," he said. He was also "making many more mistakes when I programmed, which is very annoying." Google, he said, had encouraged him to stay and work on safety.

> "You kind of censor yourself if you work for a big company, you don't feel right saying things that will damage the big company. Even if you could get away with it, it just feels wrong to me."

That sentence is the type argument in miniature. He had permission to stay. What he could not do was criticize an employer while taking its money, whether or not anyone would notice. The same instinct produced the correction of the Times: having left, he would not let Google be cast as the villain.

He calls what came next a duty. "So I feel I have a duty now to talk about the risks," he told Bartlett in 2025. On the day of his Nobel, at a University of Toronto press conference, he said: "I'm 76 and I'm not going to do much more frontier research, I believe. I'm going to spend my time advocating for people to work on safety." He used his Nobel banquet speech that December for a warning rather than a victory lap: "we now have evidence that if they are created by companies motivated by short-term profits, our safety will not be the top priority" ([Nobel banquet speech](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2024/hinton/speech/)).

His judgments of other people come out the same way, flat and moral. At that press conference he said: "I'm particularly proud of the fact that one of my students fired Sam Altman" ([U of T press conference](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H7DgMFqrON0&t=195s)). He meant Ilya Sutskever, then OpenAI's chief scientist, who helped lead the concerns behind the board's removal of [Sam Altman](/personality-analysis/sam-altman) as chief executive on November 17, 2023. Sutskever later apologized publicly for his part, and Altman was reinstated on November 22 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Removal_of_Sam_Altman_from_OpenAI)). Asked to elaborate, Hinton gave his own view: "over time, it turned out that Sam Altman was much less concerned with safety than with profits. And I think that's unfortunate."

What offends him most is a promise dropped when it becomes expensive. In December 2024 he backed a legal brief opposing OpenAI's move away from nonprofit control. "OpenAI was founded as an explicitly safety-focused nonprofit and made a variety of safety-related promises in its charter," he said in a statement quoted by [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/12/30/nonprofit-group-joins-elon-musks-effort-to-block-openais-for-profit-transition/). "Allowing it to tear all of that up when it becomes inconvenient sends a very bad message to other actors in the ecosystem."

He does change his mind when the facts change. In 2023 he declined to sign a petition for a six-month pause in AI research ("China's not going to stop developing it for six months," he told the New Yorker). In October 2024 he said, "I've never recommended slowing the advancement of AI because I don't think that's feasible." By September 16, 2026, after the briefing on Capitol Hill, he was telling reporters: "It is going to get out of control unless we do something. We need to slow down."

## Why not Type 5?

Asked on the morning of the Nobel how he would describe himself, Hinton said: "I would say I am someone who doesn't really know what field he's in but would like to understand how the brain works" ([Nobel Prize interview](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2024/hinton/interview/)). That is a Five's sentence: identity organized around a question, not a role. He theorizes even feeling. "I think feelings are counterfactual statements about what would have caused an action," he told the New Yorker. His fear of AI did not arrive as a mood. "The closest I had to a eureka moment was when a Google system called Palm was able to say why a joke was funny," he told Bartlett; that, coupled with seeing how much better digital systems are than brains at sharing what they learn, turned him toward safety.

He also admits mistakes without visible pain. In 2016 he predicted AI would be reading all the scans radiologists read. "That prediction was wrong. I was a bit overenthusiastic," he said in 2024. A [Type 5](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-5) treats a wrong model as information. So does Hinton.

On a first read, Five is the obvious answer. The difference is what happens after he decides something is wrong. The Five's characteristic move is to understand it and conserve himself, staying out of the fray. Hinton does the opposite, across decades and domains. He corrected the Times the day it ran, in Google's defense. He left a paid post because staying quiet "just feels wrong to me." He kept a forty-year-old promise with his Nobel money and condemned OpenAI for breaking one. He gave up retirement to brief lawmakers at 78. On his island, the contractors' mistakes still make him angry. Curiosity explains why he studied the brain for fifty years. It does not explain the heat.

The limits are real. Some Enneagram teachers would say a healthy Five becomes more decisive and outspoken, which could cover the activism. Fame creates demand. Plenty of principled people of every type keep promises. And the home details come from a single visit.

[Type 6](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6) is the third candidate. "I'm mildly depressed," he told MIT Technology Review in 2023. "Which is why I'm scared." He told Bartlett he spreads his money and his children's money between three Canadian banks in case a cyberattack takes one down. But a Six tends to test certainty against allies and authorities. Hinton's comes from inside ("I'm very good at ignoring what other people say"), and he treats his gloom as calibration, not dread. When people estimate the odds of bad things happening, he told MIT Technology Review, the mildly depressed are "usually right."

## What the One reading leaves out

The Type 1 reading predicts a grim, self-critical man. The people who worked with him describe someone else.

"The excitement, the joy radiated out of his office down the hall," his former student Chris Maddison recalled in 2024 ([U of T](https://web.cs.toronto.edu/news-events/news/from-ai-to-atari-what-it-was-like-to-work-with-nobel-prize-winner-geoffrey-hinton)). "He was famous for bursting into a room and pronouncing that, he now finally, after all these years, understood how the brain worked." Nick Frosst, another former student, told a story about Hinton playing the arcade game Asteroids until it damaged a finger: "It's that fever-pitched intensity, passion and playfulness that he brings to everything." Another, Kevin Swersky, remembered Hinton offering to put him up and go out to buy him dishes. These are warm recollections gathered for a celebration, but they do not sound like "mostly anxiety."

He is also funny, in a dry, anti-authority way. He told the New Yorker that "'Geoff' is an anagram for 'ego fortissimo.'" He answered a long note from a Canadian intelligence agency with "Snowden is my hero." His partner, the retired sociologist Rosemary Gartner, said of him: "I think he's the kind of person who always needs a partner."

The inner critic is quieter than a One's usually is. When he quit Google, the Times wrote that "A part of him, he said, now regrets his life's work," and quoted him: "I console myself with the normal excuse: If I hadn't done it, somebody else would have." By that fall, the New Yorker reported, he had sent a journalist a link to Edith Piaf's "Non, Je Ne Regrette Rien." In 2025 he told Bartlett: "I don't feel particularly guilty about developing AI like 40 years ago because at that time we had no idea that this stuff was gonna happen this fast." Duty without guilt is a real pattern, but it is not the self-punishing One of the textbooks.

Where the regret does show is closer to home. "I wish I'd spent more time with my wife. And with my children when they were little. I was kind of obsessed with work," he told Bartlett. He meant Jackie, who, he added, "was very supportive of me spending a lot of time working."

His grief has a line running through it, too, and he drew the connection to AI himself. By his account to the New Yorker, when his second wife, Rosalind, declined chemotherapy for ovarian cancer and asked him to sell their house to pay for homeopathic treatments, he refused for the children's sake: "I drew the line there." She died in 1994. Jackie, he said, "was incredibly brave and incredibly rational." Then, of AI: "We can't be in denial," he said. "We have to be real."

## Half a Nobel Prize, and a promise from the 1980s

In the Nobel Week interview, Hinton explained where the money went. Half of his share went to a Canadian organization that trains people living in Indigenous communities to produce safe drinking water. His own figure was that 20 percent of Indigenous communities in Ontario lack it. "It's ridiculous at this time in a rich country like Canada," he said. "That's just crazy."

The other half went back to the 1980s. He and Terry Sejnowski had built the Boltzmann Machine together, and they made a deal. "We had an agreement back in the 1980s which was that if they gave it to one of us and not the other one, we'd split it," Hinton said ([Nobel Week interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=66WiF8fXL0k&t=1040s)). When the prize came, partly for Boltzmann Machines, he asked Sejnowski where to send his half. Sejnowski declined, because the prize also covered later work he had not been part of. So they compromised. The money funds a prize in Sejnowski's name "for young researchers with crazy theories of how the brain works, like we were."

"I'm not complaining about that, but he could have been," Hinton said of Sejnowski's absence from the Nobel citation.

It was a promise two young researchers made about a prize neither had any reason to expect, kept forty years later when keeping it cost real money. Later that month, Hinton put his name to a legal fight over another organization's promises, arguing they should not be torn up "when it becomes inconvenient."

The prize carries the rest of him too. It is for the next person who looks, to everyone else in the room, the way he did for decades: wrong, and sure of it.
