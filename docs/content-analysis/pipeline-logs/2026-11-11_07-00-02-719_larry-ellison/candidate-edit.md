Larry Ellison has told the same story about his sister at least twice, and the key word changes.

In [a _60 Minutes II_ profile that CBS published in February 2004](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/the-worlds-most-competitive-man), he told it this way: "When I was a kid, my sister walked into my room and said, 'Larry, which is more important to you – to be admired or be loved?' And I said, 'Well, for me personally? To be admired.' She looks at me, smiles, 'Wrong,' and walks out." He added, "It took me a little while to realize that all of us want to be loved."

In September 2002 Playboy had published his telling of it to [David Sheff](https://davidsheff.com/larry-ellison). In that version his sister, whom neither interview names, is a psychologist, he is in his midteens, and her question is "What's more important to you: to be loved or to be respected?" He says he answered that it was easy: "To be respected." She told him he was wrong, and he got annoyed. Then, by his account, he saw it: "I'm concealing the fact that it's really more important to be loved."

Admired in one telling, respected in the other. It could be a slip of memory forty years on. It is also the open question about him.

This profile's reading of the difference, not his: a man who needs admiration arranges his life around how he looks, and a man who needs respect arranges it so that nobody can push him around. The two lives look alike until he is criticized, afraid or unobserved.

Ellison co-founded Oracle in 1977 and was its only chief executive for 37 years. Its database software, which large organizations use to store their records, made him one of the richest people in the world and put him in a long race with <a href="/personality-analysis/bill-gates">Bill Gates</a>, whose Microsoft was the one software company bigger than his.

## What is Larry Ellison's personality type?

Larry Ellison is best read as an <a href="/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-8">Enneagram Type 8</a>, the Challenger, at medium confidence. He names control of his surroundings as his motive, and under criticism he does not retreat. Type 3, the Achiever, is the strongest alternative, and his own words support it. This is an interpretation of public evidence, not a diagnosis.

Type 8 belongs to the Enneagram's body triad, whose core emotion is anger, felt as an instinct to push back against being controlled. Eights handle it by taking charge of their surroundings before anyone else can. At their best that makes them direct and protective; under threat it can become domination.

Ellison does not name anger as his engine. He names fear of failure. The fight shows in what he does. Asked in 1997 whether his attacks on competitors were acceptable, [he reached for the Gulf War](https://achievement.org/achiever/larry-j-ellison/#interview): "It was the job of our armed forces to defeat the enemy. It is my job to go out into the marketplace and win." In 2010, after Hewlett-Packard forced out its chief executive, his friend Mark Hurd, over expense-account abuses found by an inquiry that cleared him of violating its harassment policy, he [emailed the New York Times](https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna38637696) that its board had made the worst personnel decision since "the idiots on the Apple board" fired Steve Jobs.

The Type 8 reading is that fear becomes force and control before it registers as fear. That is an inference, and on core emotion alone the evidence is thin.

Most of the evidence is Ellison's own testimony from 1995 to 2024, which is exactly what a managed image would manage. Two biographies were not read for this profile: Matthew Symonds's _Softwar_ (2003) and Mike Wilson's unauthorized _The Difference Between God and Larry Ellison_ (1997). If either documents a habit of embellishing his story, an argument built on self-report weakens and Type 3 gains. Those gaps are why the confidence is medium.

## Why Larry Ellison says he started Oracle

By his own account, Ellison was born in New York City in 1944 to an unmarried 19-year-old who could not care for him. At nine months he went to her aunt and uncle on Chicago's South Side. He learned at 12 that he was adopted. In 1997 he named the core of his personality as "questioning conventional wisdom, doubting the experts, and questioning authority."

His adoptive father, Louis Ellison, is the figure he returns to. On CBS he recalled being told he would "never amount to anything," and described it, laughing, as "an alternative greeting as opposed to, 'Hi, how are you?'" He offered a guess at the reason: "I think, because I was constantly questioning authority, that he felt that would continuously get me in trouble as it had in school," he said.

That is one side of a relationship, told as a joke decades later. Louis Ellison's side is not on the record, so it stays a story Ellison tells and not a proven cause.

The reason he gives for founding Oracle does not change. In a [1995 Smithsonian oral history](https://web.archive.org/web/2020/https://americanhistory.si.edu/comphist/le1.html) he describes working under two bosses who, he says, ran their companies out of business. His conclusion: "I can run a company out of business all by myself. So I thought, next time, if the company was going to go down, I was going to be out front."

In 2016 he gave USC's graduating class the same reason, according to [a published transcript of the speech](https://singjupost.com/full-transcript-larry-ellisons-speech-at-usc-commencement-2016/): "That way I could completely control my work environment."

Twenty-one years apart, the motive he gives is the same. Neither account mentions wanting to be rich, or first, or famous. Both were polished for an audience, and the consistency still counts as evidence.

The 1997 interview holds the hinge. Asked about fear of failure, he did not deny it: "I think most overachievers are driven, not so much by the pursuit of success, but by the fear of failure." His first answer is work: "It drives me to work very hard." Then comes the sentence this profile leans on.

> "It drives me to make sure that my life is orderly, that I'm in control of my company, or in control of the airplane or boat or what-have-you, so that I'm not at risk of failure."

He ends on work again: "Whenever I feel even remotely close to being at risk of failure, I can't stop working."

Overwork as the answer to fear of failure is what a Three would say, and a One or a Six could have spoken the control clause. His answer to fear is to work harder and to get his hands on the controls. He says nothing there about how failure would look. What tips the reading is that control is also his reason for founding the company, given twice, when nobody had asked about fear.

## The trash investigation he defended in public

By Ellison's account, advocacy groups were arguing around 2000 that whatever hurt Microsoft hurt America, and he suspected Microsoft was paying them. "We found out, but the question was, how could we prove it?" he told Playboy in 2002. "That's why we hired private investigators."

In June 2000 the detective firm Oracle had hired was caught trying to buy the office trash of one of those groups, a Microsoft-funded trade association. Ellison says the method was news to him: "I didn't know they were going to go through Bill's garbage, but that doesn't bother me at all."

He did not shelter behind that. On June 28, 2000 he held a press conference. ["All we did was to try to take information that was hidden and bring it into the light,"](https://www.theregister.com/2000/06/29/ellison_offers_gates_all/) he said. "That's a public service." Then, joking by The Register's account, he offered Microsoft a trade.

> "We will ship our garbage to Redmond, and they can go through it. We believe in full disclosure."

That November he gave [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2000/11/13/the-next-richest-man-in-the-world) his summary of the method: "Unsavory, yucky—kind of. But legal." The Register's Thomas C. Greene pointed out what the full-disclosure line left out: Oracle financed front groups of its own. The type reading explains why Ellison went on offense. It does not turn buying a trade group's garbage into a public service.

He disclaimed the method, called it unsavory but legal, and defended the purpose without apology. He was as unbending about a friend. In the 1997 interview, listing people fame had let him meet, he came to Michael Milken, the financier who had gone to prison. "It's not terribly fashionable to be a supporter of Michael Milken, but I'm honored to have him as my friend," he said. He did not mention that in 1996 he and the Milken brothers had founded the education company Knowledge Universe with $500 million, [according to Education Week](https://www.edweek.org/ew/articles/1999/08/04/43univ.h18.html).

Nonconformity, he says, is method. "Not conforming caused terrible problems for me at school, but it was magic in business," he told Playboy. By his own account it is how he wins.

Each episode has a second reading. Milken was a business partner as well as a friend. The Hurd email was a shot at a competitor's board. A man with an outlaw's reputation can gain from owning a scandal loudly. Any one of these could be calculation. And no source here shows what any of it cost him. What the three episodes, from 1997 to 2010, establish is narrower: criticized, he restates his purpose and attacks.

## Why not Type 3?

The Type 3 case is strong, and most of it comes from Ellison.

<a href="/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-3">Threes</a> are organized around worth: the sense that love follows achievement, so you had better achieve. Here is Ellison in Playboy in 2002, asked whether he had been out to prove his father wrong: "If you don't get unconditional love early on, you have a great void in your life." He went on, "Depending on how you adapt, you can get very good at trying to earn the love of others. Bill Clinton is like me in that regard."

Sheff then asked whether the achievements fill the void. "Yes and no," Ellison said, and told the sister story as his answer. So the "respected" telling was a reply about earned love. He applied the description to himself, and it counts for Type 3 at face value.

The CBS profile was made during Oracle's hostile bid for a rival, PeopleSoft, which opened on June 6, 2003 and ended in December 2004 with a purchase for about $10.3 billion, [by The Register's timeline](https://www.theregister.com/2004/12/13/oracle_peoplesoft_history). CBS said the takeover "will potentially put thousands of people out of work," and has Ellison asking who he is winning for: "I admit to it, mea culpa. An awful lot of it is personal vanity."

Then there is rank. Fortune wrote in 2000 that he expected to pass Gates as the richest man in the world: "To some who know him well, this goal may matter even more to Ellison than his company's becoming the dominant software player on the Net." Ellison called the wealth ranking "kind of irrelevant" in 2002 and said, "what really means a lot to me is Oracle passing Microsoft." Wanting to be number one fits a Three at least as well as an Eight.

And his friend Andy Grove, Intel's former chief, told Fortune in 2000, "He works very hard at his bad-boy image." If the outlaw is a role, then the trash press conference and the Milken remark are performances too, and the Eight turns out to be a Three with unusual branding.

The objection is stronger than it looks. A Three courts the audience whose admiration he wants, and the audience for the man CBS called "The World's Most Competitive Man" admires a fighter who never apologizes. Every public episode above feeds that image. So does a story whose key word changes between tellings. And asked in 2002 about an accusation covered below, what he said he would never forget was the way a woman looked at him in an elevator.

On public conduct, then, the two readings cannot be separated. What keeps Type 8 ahead is evidence that does not depend on an audience, and there is less of it than a confident typing would want.

**The motive he gives when image is not the subject.** Control is his stated reason for founding the company in 1995 and 2016 and his remedy for fear in 1997. It is also the failing he named in 2002, after three marriages: "I shouldn't seek to control her anymore than I would want her to control me." These are self-reports, polished like the Type 3 ones, and they get the same weight. What they add is one motive turning up in four conversations.

**One private message.** In April 2022 <a href="/personality-analysis/elon-musk">Elon Musk</a> texted to ask whether Ellison wanted in on the Twitter purchase. The reply, disclosed later in court and [summarized by Fortune](https://fortune.com/2022/09/30/elon-musk-twitter-trial-text-messages-jack-dorsey-larry-ellison-joe-rogan/), was "of course," with an offer of "whatever you recommend." Musk suggested $2 billion "or more," and Ellison put in $1 billion. It was a private message with no audience expected, and it shows quick backing for someone inside his circle, at no cost to him.

None of this is conclusive. Grove knew him, and his remark is the best outside evidence that the persona is managed.

<a href="/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6">Type 6</a> deserves a hearing as well, because he names fear as his engine and, as a later section shows, trusts government protection. What separates the types is where trust goes when the stakes are his own. Ellison's remedy is his own hands on the controls, and when a rule bound him personally he fought it: the Playboy introduction describes a San Jose airport curfew he "routinely ignored" and then beat in court. That leans Eight. His statements about government lean Six, and the question stays open.

## The 1998 storm that found his limit

Ellison explains his risk-taking the same way whenever he is asked. In 1997 he said work, flying, sailing and chess were "all in pursuit of the same thing: self-discovery, the discovery of my own limits."

In December 1998 he got an answer. He was aboard his 80-foot yacht Sayonara when the Sydney to Hobart fleet sailed into a storm that killed six sailors on other boats. "We were at our absolute limits in this storm," he says in [CNN's documentary on the race](https://transcripts.cnn.com/show/cp/date/2002-01-01/segment/01). Sayonara was [the first boat across the line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1998_Sydney_to_Hobart_Yacht_Race).

The finish is not what he talks about in the documentary.

> "We had a lead in the race, and no one cared. But everyone cared about the morning, about the sunrise and how incredibly beautiful it was."

Playboy asked whether the storm made him more cautious. "Absolutely," he said. Asked if he would race it again, he had answered, "I'm not going to do this race again if I live to be 1,000." Then he thought about it and said, "No, if I live to be 1,000, I'll do the race again."

It is a joke. It also reads as an accurate self-portrait: a man who met a real limit, respected it, and still left himself an opening.

The opening turned out to be the America's Cup. His team won it in 2010 and defended it in 2013 on San Francisco Bay, a venue chosen at his insistence, according to the Academy's biography.

## What the type doesn't explain

The only deep fear he admits has nothing to do with business or weather. Asked in 1997 whether he had ever been deeply afraid, he said only once, at about six, when his adoptive mother, the only mother he then knew of, came home late from work: "That's the only time I can remember being deeply afraid." He remembered "making deals with God, if he would return her to me." She died during his second year at the University of Illinois, and he left, according to the [Academy of Achievement's biography](https://achievement.org/achiever/larry-j-ellison/#biography). It is one memory and should not be stretched into a theory of his childhood.

Sheff wrote that, most surprising to him, Ellison was "warm and self-effacing." He concedes fault more easily than the stereotype allows. "I wasn't a very good CEO," he said of Oracle's near-collapse in the early 1990s. The next section says what he was conceding.

An Eight at ease can be gentle, so some of this fits. A six-year-old afraid his mother will not come home fits every type equally. It is better left unexplained.

## What the control costs other people

Ray Lane, Oracle's second-in-command until Ellison pushed him out in 2000 and so no neutral witness, described the company on CBS in 2004: "Culture is win at any cost."

That culture nearly sank the company once. Oracle's staff "habitually overstated revenues," in the words of the Academy's biography, and in 1990 the company posted its first loss and its market value fell 80 percent. It later paid $24 million to settle shareholder suits and, in September 1993, agreed to a $100,000 fine to settle Securities and Exchange Commission charges of inflating revenue, without admitting or denying them, [according to a trade-press report that month](https://www.techmonitor.ai/?p=252198). The charges named the company.

The line most attached to Ellison is that "it is not sufficient that I succeed; everyone else must fail." He says it isn't his. He credits a Japanese executive who was citing Genghis Khan.

**Adelyn Lee.** In 1993 Adelyn Lee, a former Oracle employee in her twenties who had dated Ellison, sued for wrongful termination, alleging she was fired in retaliation for refusing him sex. Oracle settled for $100,000. Her suit leaned on an email that appeared to come from her supervisor, Craig Ramsey, to Ellison: "Larry, I've terminated Adelyn per your request." Ramsey said he never sent it, and prosecutors charged Lee with forging it. At her 1997 trial they showed that Ramsey's car-phone records put him on a call in his car when the email was sent and that its headers traced to an address near Lee's home. In reply her lawyer, Gordon Rockhill, argued that Ellison or someone working on his instructions had planted the email to undermine her. The jury convicted her on two counts of perjury and two of falsifying documents. She was sentenced to a year in jail and ordered to repay the $100,000, according to [a 2022 account of the case by forensics consultant David Kalat](https://thinkbrg.com/insights/publications/nervous-system-forged-email-carphone-woman-who-lost-job).

Ellison's response in Playboy was "I never forced her to have sex, and I never caused her to be fired." The conviction concerned the forged email. It did not rule on everything about a relationship between a chief executive and an employee, and the available accounts tell the story mostly from Oracle's side.

**The 2001 stock sale.** Ellison sold nearly $900 million of Oracle stock in January 2001, weeks before Oracle said it would miss earnings expectations. A shareholder suit alleged insider trading. The argument of plaintiffs' lawyer Joseph Tabacco, [as Computerworld reported it](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1588006/ellison-to-settle-insider-trading-suit.html), was the timing: Ellison sold at an average of $30.76 a share, and the day after the earnings news the stock traded at $16.98.

On November 24, 2004, Delaware's Court of Chancery granted Ellison summary judgment over the same trades. [Vice Chancellor Leo Strine wrote](https://law.justia.com/cases/delaware/court-of-chancery/2004/54350-1.html) that "no rational trier of fact could find" that Ellison held material nonpublic information when he traded, or traded because of it. The opinion records that his financial adviser had urged him to exercise 22 million options due to expire in August 2001.

A separate California case [settled in 2005](https://www.infoworld.com/article/2220877/court-oks-ellison-22-million-shareholder-settlement.html). He agreed to give $100 million to charity in Oracle's name and to pay about $22 million of the plaintiffs' legal fees. Neither report records an admission of wrongdoing.

An Eight's need for control explains how a workplace, a marriage or a sales culture ends up arranged around one man's judgment. It is an explanation of the pattern and no defense of any instance of it.

## The questioner of authority who trusts the government

Ellison says the authority he first questioned was his father's faith in the state. In 1997 he described Louis Ellison as a man who "never questioned the government's policies," one of the authority figures he called "wonderful examples of how not to be."

Five years later he was promoting a national identification standard. He granted the objection first: "Can such intelligence be abused? Absolutely. Everything can be," he told Playboy. His reason for going ahead was that the government "has a damn good record of using information." Then: "I would rather trust our government to protect us than rely on blind luck."

In August 2013, after the Snowden disclosures, he said of NSA surveillance, [as The Register reported from his CBS interview](https://www.theregister.com/2013/08/13/larry_ellison_on_google_and_nsa): "It's great, it's essential." In September 2024 he described AI-monitored cameras to Oracle's investors, starting with the police, [according to Business Insider](https://www.businessinsider.com/larry-ellison-ai-surveillance-keep-citizens-on-their-best-behavior-2024-9): "Every police officer is going to be supervised at all times, and if there's a problem, AI will report that problem and report it to the appropriate person. Citizens will be on their best behavior because we are constantly recording and reporting everything that's going on."

Three statements across 22 years, all close to the position he says he defined himself against.

The commercial explanation covers all of it and reaches back further. An early Oracle contract was a database for the CIA, and "Oracle" was that project's code name, according to the Academy's biography. The Register's Neil McAllister made the objection plainly in 2013: the U.S. government is a key Oracle market and the NSA a customer, so the conviction is also a sales position. That may be all it is.

The type reading sits beside that one and does not replace it. What Ellison has resisted since childhood is authority over him. Rules that bind other people have not troubled him the same way, and this is where the record leans most toward Type 6. Neither reading answers the civil-liberties objection.

## Politics, an island and the money

His politics drew a protest inside his own company. On February 19, 2020, a fundraiser for President Trump's re-election was held at his Rancho Mirage estate. The next day about 300 Oracle employees stopped work in a protest called No Ethics/No Work, [Bloomberg reported](https://fortune.com/2020/02/20/oracle-walkout-larry-ellison-trump-fundraiser/). Their petition argued that his public support for Trump violated Oracle's diversity, inclusion and ethics policies.

His answer came two months later, in a Forbes interview [quoted by TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/larry-ellison-top-gop-midterm-donating-15-million/): "I said President Trump could use the property. I was not here." And: "We only have one president at a time. I don't think he's the devil—I support him and want him to do well." The remarks hold no apology and no retreat.

In May 2022 the Washington Post reported, from an email in court filings, that Ellison had joined a call on November 14, 2020 with Trump allies about contesting the election result. The email, written by Catherine Engelbrecht, founder of the vote-monitoring group True the Vote, and [quoted in Engadget's summary](https://www.engadget.com/oracle-ceo-larry-ellison-joined-call-about-contesting-trumps-election-loss-002304636.html), names him among the participants. It records nothing he said. Engadget added that he had "seemingly never expressed doubts about the 2020 election results publicly."

Then there is Lanai. In 2012 he bought most of the Hawaiian island and, per the Academy's biography, announced plans to make it "the first economically viable, 100 percent green community." In [a critical 2026 feature in The Nation](https://www.thenation.com/article/economy/larry-ellison-skydance-paramount-hawaii-lanai/), Matthew Vickers argues that the plan gave way to luxury tourism while residents struggle with the cost of living. The piece notes early goodwill too and carries no response from Ellison. The record here shows a plan and a critique, not a motive.

His giving belongs on the record too. In a [2010 Giving Pledge letter](https://www.givingpledge.org/pledger/larry-ellison/) he wrote that he had put "virtually all" of his assets into a trust "with the intent of giving away at least 95% of my wealth to charitable causes." That is his stated intent. This profile did not audit the giving.

## What he kept and what he backed

In 2014 Ellison gave up the chief executive title and kept the chairmanship and the chief technology officer role. He still held both when Oracle [named two new co-CEOs on September 22, 2025](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2025/09/22/oracle-appoints-insiders-clay-magouyrk-and-mike-sicilia-as-co-ceos). Many founders do the same, so it shows only which part of the job he would hand over.

In December 2025 his son David's company, Paramount Skydance, was pursuing a hostile bid for Warner Bros. Discovery, the owner of CNN. The target's board had called the Ellison family financing "illusory" because the money sat in a revocable family trust. On December 22, [the company announced](https://www.fortune.com/2025/12/22/larry-ellison-personal-guarantee-paramount-skydance-warner-bros-netflix-deal-son-david) that Larry Ellison, then 81, had provided an "irrevocable personal guarantee" of $40.4 billion and agreed not to revoke the trust. He supplied what the other side said was missing. The takeover [closed on October 6, 2026](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/media/paramount-warner-bros-skydance-closes-rcna601833), and the combined company is called Skydance.

Fortune's headline had him coming "to his son's rescue," and a wealthy parent of any type might have signed. Like the PeopleSoft bid, the guarantee is consistent with the pattern and is not evidence of type.

In July 2026 a Paramount shareholder sued Larry and David Ellison in Delaware Chancery Court, alleging they had promised "illegal private benefits to President Trump in order to remove federal regulatory barriers," [Variety reported](https://variety.com/2026/film/news/david-larry-ellison-sued-paramount-side-deal-trump-warner-bros-1236812034/). The complaint says those benefits included settling Trump's legal claims against CNN. A Paramount spokesperson said "no commitments from either David or Larry Ellison have been made to any government body" about CNN or any other news property. These are claims in a complaint. As of November 2026, no ruling on them had been found for this profile.

In 2000, Fortune asked <a href="/personality-analysis/steve-jobs">Steve Jobs</a>, whom Ellison called his best friend, what drove his pal. Jobs chuckled, thought for a moment, and whispered one word into the phone: "Rosebud."

Rosebud is the dying word of the tycoon in _Citizen Kane_. Jobs did not say what he meant. Ellison's own version is the story about his sister. In both tellings the boy picks standing over love, she tells him he is wrong, and the man telling the story agrees with her. This is how he finished it in 2002.

> "Of course she's right. We've got to find that someplace in our lives. It's why my extended family is very important. And my relationship with my children is especially important to me."

No family member's account is part of this profile, so it cannot say how that has gone. Admired or respected: the word never settled, and the record does not settle which he wanted more. Criticized, he does not retreat. Afraid, he works and takes the controls. And the one reason he has given for building any of it was to control his own surroundings.
