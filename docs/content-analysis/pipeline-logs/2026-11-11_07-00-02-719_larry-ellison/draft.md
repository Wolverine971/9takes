Larry Ellison has told the same story about his sister at least twice, and the key word changes.

On [CBS's _60 Minutes II_ in 2004](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/the-worlds-most-competitive-man), at 59, he told it this way: "When I was a kid, my sister walked into my room and said, 'Larry, which is more important to you – to be admired or be loved?' And I said, 'Well, for me personally? To be admired.' She looks at me, smiles, 'Wrong,' and walks out."

Two years earlier he had told it to [David Sheff for Playboy](https://davidsheff.com/larry-ellison). In that version his sister is a psychologist, he is in his midteens, and her question is "What's more important to you: to be loved or to be respected?" He says he answered that it was easy: "To be respected." She told him he was wrong, and he got annoyed. Then, by his account, he saw it: "I'm concealing the fact that it's really more important to be loved."

Admired in one telling, respected in the other. It could be a slip of memory forty years on. It is also the open question about him.

A man who needs to be admired arranges his life around how he looks to other people. A man who needs to be respected arranges it so that nobody can push him around. From a distance the two lives look the same: the yachts, the fighter jet, the Hawaiian island, the long public war with <a href="/personality-analysis/bill-gates">Bill Gates</a>. They separate only when being liked and being in charge point in different directions.

Ellison's public record has several of those moments. They are the best evidence for what follows.

## What is Larry Ellison's personality type?

Larry Ellison is best read as an <a href="/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-8">Enneagram Type 8</a>, the Challenger, at medium confidence. When approval and control conflict, he repeatedly takes control and absorbs the disapproval. Type 3, the Achiever, is the strongest alternative, and his own words support it. This is an interpretation of public evidence, not a diagnosis.

Type 8 belongs to the Enneagram's body triad, the three types organized around anger and autonomy. Eights settle the autonomy question by taking charge of their surroundings before anyone else can. At their best that makes them direct, loyal and very hard to intimidate. Under threat, the same drive can become domination.

Most of the evidence here is Ellison's own testimony across four decades, tested against things he did when they cost him something. Two biographies that could shift the picture, Matthew Symonds's _Softwar_ and Mike Wilson's _The Difference Between God and Larry Ellison_, were not available for this profile. That gap is why the confidence is medium.

## Why Larry Ellison says he started Oracle

By his own account, Ellison was born in New York City in 1944 to an unmarried 19-year-old who could not care for him. At nine months he went to her aunt and uncle on Chicago's South Side. [He told the Academy of Achievement in 1997](https://achievement.org/achiever/larry-j-ellison/#interview): "I don't think my personality has changed much since I was five years old." He named its main feature as "questioning conventional wisdom, doubting the experts, and questioning authority."

His adoptive father, Louis Ellison, is the figure he returns to. On CBS he recalled being told he would "never amount to anything," and described it, laughing, as "an alternative greeting as opposed to, 'Hi, how are you?'" Asked whether it gave him drive, he said, "I had all the disadvantages required for success."

That is one side of a relationship, told as a joke decades later. Louis Ellison's side is not on the record, so it stays a story Ellison tells and not a proven cause.

He left two universities without a degree, drove to California and taught himself to program. "So I was a lousy student, but a good builder," he said in a [1995 Smithsonian oral history](https://web.archive.org/web/2020/https://americanhistory.si.edu/comphist/le1.html).

Then comes the detail that matters most for typing him. Ellison has explained more than once why, in 1977, he co-founded the company that became Oracle, and the reason does not change. In the 1995 oral history he describes working under two bosses who, he says, ran their companies out of business. His conclusion: "I can run a company out of business all by myself. So I thought, next time, if the company was going to go down, I was going to be out front."

In 2016, by then one of the richest people alive, he gave USC's graduating class the same reason, according to [a published transcript of the speech](https://singjupost.com/full-transcript-larry-ellisons-speech-at-usc-commencement-2016/): "That way I could completely control my work environment." He added that he never expected the company to grow beyond 50 people.

Twenty-one years apart, before and after the fortune, the motive he gives is the same. He does not say he wanted to be rich, or first, or famous. The 1995 version is warmer (he also wanted "an environment where I would enjoy working"), and founders do tend to retell their beginnings modestly. The consistency still counts as evidence.

The 1997 interview holds the hinge. Asked about fear of failure, he did not deny it: "I think most overachievers are driven, not so much by the pursuit of success, but by the fear of failure."

That sentence could come from a Three or an Eight. The next one is more specific.

> "It drives me to make sure that my life is orderly, that I'm in control of my company, or in control of the airplane or boat or what-have-you, so that I'm not at risk of failure."

His answer to fear is to get his hands on the controls. He says nothing there about how failure would look.

## Caught with Microsoft's trash, he called a press conference

In June 2000 it emerged that a detective firm hired by Oracle had tried to buy the office trash of a Microsoft-funded trade group. Ellison could have blamed the contractor.

He held a press conference instead, on June 28, 2000. ["All we did was to try to take information that was hidden and bring it into the light,"](https://www.theregister.com/2000/06/29/ellison_offers_gates_all/) he said. "That's a public service." Then he offered Microsoft a trade.

> "We will ship our garbage to Redmond, and they can go through it. We believe in full disclosure."

He did not back away later. That November he gave [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2000/11/13/the-next-richest-man-in-the-world) his summary of the method: "Unsavory, yucky—kind of. But legal." Two years on he told Playboy, "I didn't know they were going to go through Bill's garbage, but that doesn't bother me at all."

The Register's Thomas C. Greene, who covered the press conference, pointed out what the full-disclosure line left out: Oracle financed front groups of its own. The type reading explains why Ellison went on offense. It does not turn going through a rival's garbage into a public service, and he conceded the unsavory part himself.

What matters for the portrait is the direction of the move. He could look better or concede nothing, and he conceded nothing.

The same choice shows up elsewhere:

- **1997.** Nobody in the Academy of Achievement interview had asked about Michael Milken, who had served prison time for securities violations. Ellison brought him up anyway. "It's not terribly fashionable to be a supporter of Michael Milken, but I'm honored to have him as my friend," he said.
- **2010.** After Hewlett-Packard forced out his friend Mark Hurd, Ellison [emailed the New York Times](https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna38637696): "The HP board just made the worst personnel decision since the idiots on the Apple board fired Steve Jobs many years ago." HP disputed his account of the board's vote. Oracle hired Hurd the next month.

He describes this as policy. This is how he put it in 1997.

> "When fashion and the pursuit of love is in conflict with reason, too often fashion and the pursuit of love usually win. In my case, it didn't."

His 2002 version, to Playboy, was "Not conforming caused terrible problems for me at school, but it was magic in business."

Each episode has a second reading. Milken was a wealthy, well-connected ally. Hiring Hurd was a coup against a competitor. A man with an outlaw's reputation can gain from owning a scandal loudly. Any one of these could be calculation. Across four decades, though, the thing these choices reliably cost him is approval, and he keeps paying.

## Why not Type 3?

The Type 3 case is strong, and most of it comes from Ellison.

<a href="/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-3">Threes</a> are organized around worth: the sense that love follows achievement, so you had better achieve. Here is Ellison in Playboy in 2002, asked whether his life had been an effort to prove his father wrong: "If you don't get unconditional love early on, you have a great void in your life." A few sentences later he added, "You either learn how to get it or you try to get it through your achievements." He compared himself to Bill Clinton and Winston Churchill.

In the CBS telling of the sister story he chose "admired." Asked in the same interview who he was winning for, he said, "I admit to it, mea culpa. An awful lot of it is personal vanity." He called himself "addicted to winning." By his own account in the USC speech, his family wanted a doctor, and he tried and failed to become one.

And his friend Andy Grove, Intel's former chief, told Fortune in 2000, "He works very hard at his bad-boy image." If the outlaw is a role, then the trash press conference and the Milken remark are performances too, and the Eight turns out to be a Three with unusual branding.

Two things keep Type 8 ahead, on top of what he reaches for when afraid.

**The direction of the costly choices.** A Three under pressure usually adjusts to protect how he is seen. Ellison's record shows the reverse, and he says so. In the USC speech he described his first wife divorcing him after he wanted to buy a sailboat, which she took as proof he lacked ambition. [USC's own report](https://today.usc.edu/?p=36328) quotes his conclusion: "Their dreams and my dreams were different. I would never confuse the two of them again."

**The void passage is less of a confession than it looks.** He was answering a leading question and speaking in the second person. He named two routes to earned love, charm or achievement, without saying which was his.

None of this is conclusive. Grove knew him, and his remark is the best outside evidence that the persona is managed. Several of the unpopular stands also served Oracle commercially. The two unread biographies are the likeliest place to find testimony from people who knew him before the money, and if they show a habit of embellishing his own story, the Type 3 case gets stronger.

<a href="/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-7">Type 7</a> gets a look too, because the appetite for speed and novelty is real. But a Seven keeps options open, and Ellison stayed with one company for nearly fifty years.

## The 1998 storm that found his limit

Ellison explains his risk-taking the same way whenever he is asked. In 1997 he said work, flying, sailing and chess were "all in pursuit of the same thing: self-discovery, the discovery of my own limits."

In December 1998 he got an answer. His yacht Sayonara entered the Sydney to Hobart race and sailed into a storm that killed six sailors on other boats. Of the 115 boats that left Sydney, 44 finished, according to [CNN's documentary on the race](https://transcripts.cnn.com/show/cp/date/2002-01-01/segment/01). Sayonara won.

He had sailed the race in 1995 and entered again, he [told CNN in 2001](https://transcripts.cnn.com/show/sm/date/2001-06-10/segment/14), because he "wanted to see how much better I really was." Then the punchline: "Unfortunately, the weather got worse faster than I got better."

The win is not what he talks about in the documentary.

> "We had a lead in the race, and no one cared. But everyone cared about the morning, about the sunrise and how incredibly beautiful it was."

About the decision to sail at all, he says, "I guess we didn't listen carefully enough to the warnings that were there. I don't think it was arrogance so much as curiosity."

Then there is the vow. He told Playboy that when asked if he would race it again, he said, "I'm not going to do this race again if I live to be 1,000." Then he thought about it and said, "No, if I live to be 1,000, I'll do the race again."

It is a joke. It also reads as an accurate self-portrait: a man who met a real limit, respected it, and still left himself an opening.

## What the type doesn't explain

A portrait of pure force would miss a lot of him.

The only deep fear he admits has nothing to do with business or weather. Asked in 1997 whether he had ever been deeply afraid, he said only once, at about six, when his mother came home late from work: "That's the only time I can remember being deeply afraid." He remembered "making deals with God, if he would return her to me." It is one memory and should not be stretched into a theory of his childhood. It does sit close to what his sister was asking.

He is drawn to quiet. He told the Smithsonian interviewer that Kyoto "stunned" him and that Japanese culture seems to "intelligently pursue tranquility." CBS described his Woodside estate as a Japanese village inspired by that city.

Sheff wrote that, most surprising to him, Ellison was "warm and self-effacing."

He concedes fault more easily than the stereotype allows. "I wasn't a very good CEO," he said of Oracle's near-collapse in the early 1990s.

An Eight at ease can be gentle, so some of this fits. A six-year-old afraid his mother will not come home fits every type equally. It is better left unexplained.

## What the control costs other people

The people who paid for the pattern have described it. Ray Lane was Oracle's second-in-command for eight years until Ellison pushed him out in 2000, so he is not a neutral witness. This was his description on CBS in 2004.

> "Culture is win at any cost. It wasn't good enough for a salesman to make their quota. They had to make 200 percent of their quota."

The line most attached to Ellison is that "it is not sufficient that I succeed; everyone else must fail." He says it isn't his. In the Smithsonian history he attributes it to a Japanese executive who was citing Genghis Khan, and complains that the New York Times printed it as his own. He kept retelling the story, including on CBS in 2004.

Control reached into his marriages, by his own description. By 2002 he had been married three times. Asked what he had learned, he said, "I had an irrational standard of behavior for myself. If she didn't conform to the same standard, I would become annoyed." He ended with "I shouldn't seek to control her anymore than I would want her to control me." None of his former wives' accounts are part of this profile.

Two legal episodes belong on the record.

**Adelyn Lee.** In 1993 Adelyn Lee, a former Oracle employee in her twenties who had dated Ellison, sued for wrongful termination, alleging she was fired in retaliation for refusing him sex. Oracle settled for $100,000. Prosecutors then charged Lee over an email offered as evidence. At her trial, which began in January 1997, her lawyer, Gordon Rockhill, argued that Ellison or someone working on his instructions had planted the email to undermine her. The jury convicted her on two counts of perjury and two of falsifying documents. She was sentenced to a year in jail and ordered to repay the $100,000, according to [a 2022 account of the case by forensics consultant David Kalat](https://thinkbrg.com/insights/publications/nervous-system-forged-email-carphone-woman-who-lost-job).

Ellison's response in Playboy was "I never forced her to have sex, and I never caused her to be fired." He called the accusation worse than nearly dying at sea, and described its effect: "I'll never forget riding up the elevator at Oracle alone with a woman—the way she looked at me." The conviction concerned the forged email. It did not rule on everything about a relationship between a chief executive and an employee, and the available accounts tell the story mostly from Oracle's side.

**The 2001 stock sale.** Ellison sold nearly $900 million of Oracle stock in January 2001, weeks before Oracle said it would miss earnings expectations. A shareholder suit alleged insider trading. The argument of plaintiffs' lawyer Joseph Tabacco, [as Computerworld reported it](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1588006/ellison-to-settle-insider-trading-suit.html), was the timing: Ellison sold at an average of $30.76 a share, and the day after the earnings news the stock traded at $16.98. A Delaware court had already ruled in Ellison's favor over the same trades. The California case [settled in 2005](https://www.infoworld.com/article/2220877/court-oks-ellison-22-million-shareholder-settlement.html). He agreed to give $100 million to charity in Oracle's name and to pay about $22 million of the plaintiffs' legal fees. Neither report records an admission of wrongdoing, and Oracle did not comment.

An Eight's need for control explains how a workplace, a marriage or a stock sale ends up arranged around one man's judgment. It is an explanation of the pattern and no defense of any instance of it.

## The questioner of authority who wants more cameras

The man who calls "questioning authority" the core of his character has spent more than two decades arguing that the state should watch more.

In 2002 he was pushing a national identification standard and told Playboy, "I would rather trust our government to protect us than rely on blind luck." In August 2013, after the Snowden disclosures, he said of NSA surveillance, [as The Register reported from his CBS interview](https://www.theregister.com/2013/08/13/larry_ellison_on_google_and_nsa): "It's great, it's essential." In September 2024 he told Oracle's investors, [according to Business Insider](https://www.businessinsider.com/larry-ellison-ai-surveillance-keep-citizens-on-their-best-behavior-2024-9): "Citizens will be on their best behavior because we are constantly recording and reporting everything that's going on."

The Register's Neil McAllister made the plainest objection in 2013: the U.S. government is a key Oracle market and the NSA a customer, so the conviction is also a sales position. That may be all it is.

Read through the type, the contradiction shrinks. What Ellison has resisted since childhood is authority over him. Order enforced by systems he builds is something he has always liked. That reading accounts for 22 years of consistency. It does not answer the civil-liberties objection.

His politics drew a protest inside his own company. He called himself "a lifelong Democrat" in 2002. On February 19, 2020, a fundraiser for President Trump's re-election was held at his Rancho Mirage estate. The next day about 300 Oracle employees stopped work in a protest called No Ethics/No Work, [Bloomberg reported](https://fortune.com/2020/02/20/oracle-walkout-larry-ellison-trump-fundraiser/). Their petition argued that his public support for Trump violated Oracle's diversity, inclusion and ethics policies. The report records no response from him.

In May 2022 the Washington Post reported, from an email in court filings, that Ellison had joined a call on November 14, 2020 with Trump allies about contesting the election result. The email, written by True the Vote founder Catherine Engelbrecht and [quoted in Engadget's summary](https://www.engadget.com/oracle-ceo-larry-ellison-joined-call-about-contesting-trumps-election-loss-002304636.html), names him among the participants. It records nothing he said. Oracle did not respond to the Post.

Then there is Lanai. In 2012 he bought most of the Hawaiian island and, per the [Academy of Achievement's biography](https://achievement.org/achiever/larry-j-ellison/#biography), announced plans to make it "the first economically viable, 100 percent green community." It reads as the largest version of an environment he can completely control. [A critical 2026 feature in The Nation](https://www.thenation.com/article/economy/larry-ellison-skydance-paramount-hawaii-lanai/) argues the promise has not been met. It cites resident Solomon Pili Kaho'ohalahala telling Bloomberg that Ellison had done little to control the invasive deer or protect the reefs, and it also notes early goodwill, including a renovated community pool. Residents on both sides deserve a fuller hearing than one advocacy piece provides.

## At 81, the same move with a bigger number

In April 2022 <a href="/personality-analysis/elon-musk">Elon Musk</a> texted to ask whether Ellison wanted in on the Twitter purchase. The reply, disclosed later in court and [reported by Fortune](https://fortune.com/2022/09/30/elon-musk-twitter-trial-text-messages-jack-dorsey-larry-ellison-joe-rogan/), was "of course," with an offer to put in "whatever you recommend." He invested $1 billion, which for a man of his wealth was no sacrifice. But it was a private message, and it shows the same reflex as the public ones: quick, large, unhedged backing for someone inside his circle.

In December 2025 his son David's company, Paramount Skydance, was pursuing a hostile bid for Warner Bros. Discovery. The target's board had called the Ellison family financing "illusory." On December 22, [the company announced](https://www.fortune.com/2025/12/22/larry-ellison-personal-guarantee-paramount-skydance-warner-bros-netflix-deal-son-david) that Larry Ellison, then 81, had provided an "irrevocable personal guarantee" of $40.4 billion. He made no public comment in that report. The takeover [closed on October 6, 2026](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/media/paramount-warner-bros-skydance-closes-rcna601833), and the combined company is called Skydance.

It is the trash press conference at a different scale: a public doubt about his word, answered with force and no explanation. It was also the price of winning a contested deal.

That deal carries an unresolved allegation. In July 2026 a Paramount shareholder sued Larry and David Ellison in Delaware Chancery Court, alleging they had promised "illegal private benefits to President Trump in order to remove federal regulatory barriers," [Variety reported](https://variety.com/2026/film/news/david-larry-ellison-sued-paramount-side-deal-trump-warner-bros-1236812034/). A Paramount spokesperson said "no commitments from either David or Larry Ellison have been made to any government body" about CNN or any other news property. These are claims in a complaint. As of November 2026, no ruling on them had been found for this profile.

At Oracle he gave up the chief executive title in 2014 and kept the chairmanship and the chief technology officer role. When the company [named two new co-CEOs on September 22, 2025](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2025/09/22/oracle-appoints-insiders-clay-magouyrk-and-mike-sicilia-as-co-ceos), he stayed in both.
