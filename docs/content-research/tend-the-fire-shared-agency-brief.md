# The World Is Burning / Shared Agency Editorial Brief

**Created:** 2026-08-30
**Owner:** DJ Wayne
**Working blog title:** The World Is Burning. What Can You Actually Do?
**Working URL:** `/community/the-world-is-burning-shared-agency`

**Companion visual:** `9takes-statues-campfire-v2.png`

## The originating question

The image shows people gathered around a small fire, looking into their phones while a city burns
behind them. It captures a recognizable feeling: society appears to be breaking down, every crisis
arrives through the feed, and ordinary people do not know what meaningful role remains for them.

The post must answer the question the image creates:

> The world is burning. We are all watching it through our phones. What can I actually do?

This is not primarily a post about screen-time hygiene, loneliness, optimism, volunteering, or the
Enneagram. Those subjects can support the argument, but the central problem is the gap between how
much of the world a person can see and how little of it a person can affect alone.

## Thesis

> We have planet-scale awareness but individual-scale agency. The answer is not to care less or
> consume more. It is to turn attention into shared agency by learning to act with other people.

The image contains the answer as well as the problem. The people are already physically gathered.
The smaller fire in front of them is bounded and capable of being tended together. The community is
latent. It has not yet recognized its own agency.

## Reader

The primary reader is an ordinary person who:

- feels that society is deteriorating or that the world is lurching from crisis to crisis;
- stays informed through social media but feels worse and no more capable after doing so;
- cares about real problems but does not know where meaningful action begins;
- is not necessarily an activist, organizer, extrovert, or Enneagram expert;
- suspects community is part of the answer but needs a socially intelligent way to begin.

The post should not shame this reader for scrolling. Scrolling is framed as an understandable response
to receiving urgent information without a role, a plan, or other people with whom to act.

## Promise

By the end of the post, the reader should:

1. understand why the feed can produce attention without leverage;
2. recognize shared agency as the missing response;
3. see community building as a practice of listening and acting, not recruiting people into a
   predetermined mission;
4. know how to read a person or community using three heuristics: recurrence, context, and emotional
   stake;
5. know where to begin: tend to yourself, tend to your community, then tend to an outside community;
6. have one small action they can take within seven days.

## Vocabulary

### Core language

**Attention without leverage**  
Receiving a constant stream of problems without a meaningful role in addressing them.

**Shared agency**  
The belief and demonstrated ability that people can produce an effect by acting together.

**Read the vibes**  
Notice what a person or community repeatedly returns to, place it in context, and infer the emotional
stake. Treat the read as a hypothesis and verify it with a real question.

**Protect, repair, or build**  
The builder's three responses after understanding a situation: strengthen what is good, address what
is causing harm, or create what is missing.

**Tend to yourself → tend to your community → tend to an outside community**  
The inside-out progression. As distance from lived context increases, certainty should decrease and
listening should increase.

### Language to avoid

- “Phones are destroying society.” The phone is a delivery mechanism, not the entire cause.
- “People do not care.” The post argues that many people care but lack a shared role.
- “Just log off.” Reduced exposure can help, but disconnection alone does not create agency.
- “Find your tribe.” This can suggest similarity, exclusion, and echo chambers.
- “Fix an outside community.” Entering as a fixer contradicts the listen-first posture.
- “Community will save us.” Community can reproduce conformity and power imbalances.
- A long diagnostic checklist for reading a room. The guide uses a three-part heuristic.

## The vibe-reading heuristic

1. **Recurrence:** What keeps coming up?
2. **Context:** What is happening in this person or community that makes the topic significant?
3. **Emotional stake:** What do people appear to want, protect, fear, resent, or hope for?

The verification question:

> You keep coming back to this. What about it feels important to you?

At the community level:

> This keeps coming up. What does that tell us about what people here care about?

## Evidence map

### 1. News saturation is producing avoidance and unease

The Reuters Institute's 2026 Digital News Report surveyed more than 90,000 people across 48 markets.
It reports that 42% now avoid the news sometimes or often, compared with 29% when the measure began
in 2017. Its executive summary describes a widespread mood of unease and notes that people who rely
on social and video networks as their main news source are more negative about coverage of major
stories.

**Safe claim:** A large share of news consumers sometimes or often avoid the news, and the current
news environment is marked by overload, fragmentation, and unease.

**Do not claim:** Social media has made everyone hopeless or that news avoidance is caused by one
factor.

Source: [Reuters Institute, Digital News Report 2026](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2026/dnr-executive-summary)

### 2. Doomscrolling correlates with distress and pessimism

Recent doomscrolling studies associate compulsive negative-news consumption with distress, future
anxiety, lower well-being, and more pessimistic beliefs about human nature. Much of this literature
is correlational or crisis-specific, so causality must not be overstated.

**Safe claim:** Doomscrolling is associated with psychological distress and a darker view of the
world; it can be both a response to anxiety and a behavior that sustains it.

Sources:

- [Doomscrolling during COVID-19, _Applied Research in Quality of Life_](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35157484/)
- [Doomscrolling, existential anxiety, and pessimism, _Computers in Human Behavior Reports_](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S245195882400071X)

### 3. Social connection matters at individual and community scale

The 2023 U.S. Surgeon General advisory synthesizes research connecting social connection to health,
meaning, resilience, civic engagement, and community well-being. It explicitly treats connection as
an individual, relational, community, and societal issue, and recommends investment in local
institutions and environments that bring people together.

**Safe claim:** Social connection is an important contributor to individual and community health,
but it is shaped by environments and institutions as well as personal choice.

Source: [U.S. Surgeon General, _Our Epidemic of Loneliness and Isolation_](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf)

### 4. Connection becomes agency when people believe they can act together

Albert Bandura described collective efficacy as shared belief in a group's power to produce effects
through collective action. He linked perceived collective efficacy to motivation, resilience, and
performance.

Robert Sampson, Stephen Raudenbush, and Felton Earls operationalized neighborhood collective
efficacy as social cohesion plus willingness to intervene for the common good. In a study of 8,782
residents across 343 Chicago neighborhoods, higher collective efficacy was associated with lower
violence after controlling for several neighborhood and individual factors.

**Safe claim:** Connection alone is not the whole mechanism. Trust combined with willingness to act
for a shared good is a well-established form of collective efficacy.

Sources:

- [Bandura, “Exercise of Human Agency Through Collective Efficacy”](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/current-directions/1467-8721.00064/)
- [Sampson, Raudenbush, and Earls, “Neighborhoods and Violent Crime”](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9252316/)

### 5. Start with assets, not only deficiencies

The Asset-Based Community Development Institute begins with residents' skills, local groups,
relationships, and existing community strengths. Its public principles include starting with gifts,
valuing small actions, nurturing community-led action, believing in possibility, and leading by
stepping back.

This directly supports the builder posture: do not enter a community with a problem inventory and a
solution already written.

Source: [Asset-Based Community Development Institute](https://abcdinstitute.org/)

### 6. Community participation is not automatically beneficial

A 2023 rapid review of 27 studies found good indications that improved community agency and control
can improve community well-being through neighborhood connections, decision-making influence, and
community capabilities. It also found neutral or negative effects in 14 studies. Barriers included
power imbalances, structural issues, failure to address early losses, and weak sustainability.

**Safe claim:** Community agency can improve trust, belonging, and sense of place when residents help
identify priorities and act together. Poorly designed participation can also create frustration and
reproduce power imbalances.

Source: [What Works Centre for Wellbeing, _Rapid Review of Community Agency and Control_](https://whatworkswellbeing.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Rapid-Review-Community-Agency-and-Control.pdf)

## Search and competitive angle

Current results around “what to do when the world feels hopeless” mostly fall into two categories:

1. individual coping advice such as limiting news, journaling, mindfulness, or seeking support;
2. broad encouragement to volunteer, join an organization, or start local.

A directly competing post, “How to Build Community When It Feels Like the World Is Crumbling,” makes
the case for starting small and local but does not offer a perception framework for understanding a
community before acting.

The 9takes gap is the bridge between feeling and organizing:

> How do I accurately read myself, my community, and an outside community before deciding what action
> would actually help?

The distinctive contribution is the combination of:

- the two-fire visual;
- attention without leverage;
- shared agency;
- recurrence → context → emotional stake;
- protect → repair → build;
- the inside-out progression;
- “Answer before the crowd” as an offline listening method.

## Draft outline

### Opening: The people around the fire

Describe the image without explaining it too quickly. The city burns. The group is together. Every
face is turned toward a private screen. The small fire in the center is the visual clue.

Pose the reader's question: “What am I supposed to do about any of this?”

### 1. The problem is attention without leverage

Separate caring from capability. The feed expands awareness without supplying a role. Explain why
“just care more” and “just log off” are incomplete responses.

### 2. The missing thing is shared agency

Define shared agency in plain language. Distinguish an audience, a group chat, or proximity from a
community capable of acting together.

### 3. Think like a builder, not a problem hunter

Introduce the balanced posture: protect what is good, repair what is harmful, build what is missing.
Use asset-based community development to support the shift away from deficit-first thinking.

### 4. Read the vibes before proposing the plan

Teach recurrence, context, and emotional stake. Show how to verify a read without diagnosing people.
Explain why the recurring subject may be an elephant in the room or a source of positive energy.

### 5. Work from the inside out

- Tend to yourself.
- Tend to your community.
- Tend to an outside community.

State the humility rule: as distance from lived context increases, listening increases and certainty
decreases.

### 6. One small act within seven days

Give the reader a bounded practice:

1. Choose one community you already belong to.
2. Notice what keeps coming up.
3. Ask three people what it means to them.
4. Name one thing to protect, repair, or build.
5. Invite one small action that requires more than one person and can be completed quickly.

### Closing: Look up

Return to the image. Do not promise that the city stops burning. The first victory is that the people
around the smaller fire look up, recognize one another, and choose one thing to tend together.

Close with a real 9takes question:

> What keeps coming up in your community, and what do you think it is really about?
