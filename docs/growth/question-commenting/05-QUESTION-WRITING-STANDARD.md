# 9takes question-writing standard

## The standard

**Concrete scene, open interpretation.**

The question supplies the moment. The respondent supplies the meaning.

A strong question makes someone remember a behavior, person, sentence, or recent moment before they
start explaining themselves. It may feel exposing, but it should not tell the reader which personality
they have or which answer is emotionally correct.

Question #567 is the clearest current example:

> What's something you do every day to seem fine that nobody knows is costing you effort?

It works because it combines an ordinary behavior, a social mask, and a hidden cost. A respondent can
answer it in one sentence, but different answers still reveal very different emotional logics.

## Three useful question shapes

### 1. A hidden behavior

Ask for one thing the reader does, says, avoids, rehearses, notices, or pretends not to mind. The answer
should be a concrete example rather than a personal philosophy.

### 2. A small social moment

Give the reader a recognizable event such as a delayed reply, canceled plan, unwanted advice, forgotten
detail, awkward compliment, invisible chore, or disputed memory. Ask for the first thought or action,
not a framework for deciding what to do.

### 3. A remembered example

Ask for a particular person, sentence, mistake, compliment, fear, or childhood detail. Give the answer a
clear shape while leaving its emotional meaning open.

These are starting points, not fill-in-the-blank templates. Repetition of any one shape will make the
question bank feel generated again.

## Quality tests

A candidate must pass all five:

1. **Three-second answer:** Can a reader begin a real answer almost immediately?
2. **Specific noun or action:** Does the prompt create a moment instead of naming an abstract tension?
3. **Open meaning:** Could three sincere people answer differently without one sounding more mature?
4. **Premise check:** Does it avoid assigning the reader an achievement, wound, fixation, or type?
5. **Text-a-friend voice:** Would a person actually send this sentence without sounding like a coach or
   an AI?

The perspective-spread test happens before any Enneagram work: draft three short, non-type-labeled
answers. Reject the question if they collapse into the same moral, differ only in tone, or require
stereotypes to feel distinct.

## Automatic rejection patterns

- “How do you decide...”, “What guides your decision...”, or “How do you navigate...”
- “What is the true cost...”, “What does ... look like?”, or other coaching/philosophy language
- “Whether to...”, “would you rather...”, or a list of acceptable choices
- A premise involving unusual fame, profound knowledge, major achievement, great sacrifice, or something
  “significant” the reader supposedly built
- An Enneagram fear, desire, archetype, or celebrity biography disguised as a universal question
- Advice prompts, moral tests, and questions with one admirable answer
- More than one question, more than 22 words, or enough setup that it needs to be read twice

## Editorial workflow

1. Select eight ordinary scene seeds without using a profile's name, biography, occupation, or type.
2. Generate one candidate per scene plus three materially different sample answers for each.
3. Run deterministic rejection checks and a separate veto-editor pass. The veto editor rejects scene
   restatements and sample answers that differ in topic but not emotional logic.
4. Retain the full candidate slate, failures included, for review.
5. Have DJ choose or rewrite one exact question. Generated selection is never approval.
6. Publish one question, not a batch, and post the first real human answer with it.
7. Only after approval, generate the nine Enneagram-shaped takes from the exact public wording.
8. Use contribution rate and answer quality to decide what question shape to try next.

Question generation and veto review default to `anthropic/claude-sonnet-4`. The cheaper
`google/gemini-2.5-flash` remains the default only for drafting the nine takes after approval. In a live
comparison, Flash repeatedly treated different examples as different perspectives; Sonnet produced and
recognized differences in reaction, motive, and emotional logic.

## Unpublished calibration set

These are taste-calibration drafts, not approved questions and not database content:

1. What's something you say “I don't mind” about when you actually do?
2. What kind of compliment is hardest for you to believe?
3. What's something you rehearse before saying, even to someone you trust?
4. What do you do after sending a message that felt a little too honest?
5. What's a favor you say yes to, then resent later?
6. What do you say you're “too busy” for when that isn't the real reason?
7. When plans get canceled at the last minute, what's your honest first reaction?
8. What's the first story you tell yourself when someone doesn't text back?
9. What do you do when someone gets credit for an idea you shared?
10. What's a small mistake you still think about more than anyone else probably does?

DJ's reactions to this set should update the examples and rules before any replacement question is
published. A candidate that passes the automated gate can still be bland, leading, repetitive, or wrong
for 9takes.
