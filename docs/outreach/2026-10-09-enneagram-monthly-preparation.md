## Proposed article

**Working title:** What Public-Figure Typings Reveal—and What They Can't Tell Us

**Central argument:** Type patterns within the public figures covered by 9takes can generate useful questions about personality and public life. They also reveal the limits of the sample and the judgments used to assign types. The article should show readers both the observations and the plausible alternative explanations.

Aim initially for 1,200–1,500 words, one clear chart supported by a small editable table, source links, and a short author bio. This is our working editorial target; Jack has imposed no word limit.

Write for readers who already know the nine types. Spend the space on evidence, interpretation, and practical implications. The article should stand on its own; its value should not depend on visiting 9takes.

## Article outline

1. **Open with the patterns promised in the pitch.** Approximately 43% of the comedian profiles are typed as Seven and 37% of musician/artist profiles as Four. Put the sample label beside the figures. Verify final numbers against one fixed snapshot.
2. **Explain what was counted.** Describe how subjects were chosen, how categories were assigned, what public evidence supports a typing, and whether categories overlap. Distinguish editorial interpretations from a person's own stated type. Only describe methods actually used.
3. **Walk through three observations.** Comedians and Seven; musicians/artists and Four; technology founders and Five. Preserve the crucial distinction in the last case: Five may be overrepresented relative to the whole corpus even though Three is the group's most common assigned type.
4. **Test the interpretations.** Selection for coverage, selection into public prominence, professional performance, the public record, and editorial expectations may all shape the results. The corpus cannot isolate these effects or establish causality.
5. **Show where careful readers disagree.** Use one well-sourced public-figure example to explain why similar evidence may support different readings of motivation. Avoid presenting a public persona as direct access to private motives.
6. **Give readers a useful practice.** Keep the denominator and typing method attached to each statistic. Use patterns to ask better questions, while keeping individual typing open to correction.

## Proposed opening passage

In the public-figure profiles published on 9takes, roughly four in ten comedians are interpreted as Enneagram Sevens. Among musicians and artists, more than a third are interpreted as Fours. Those patterns invite familiar explanations: the comedian looking for another possibility, the artist trying to give a personal experience a form.

But before asking why those patterns appear, we need to ask what produced the numbers. These are people selected for coverage, examined through a public record, and assigned types through interpretation. Each step shapes what we see.

The useful question is how much these observations can support. They describe the profiles in this collection. They give us questions to investigate about personality, performance, and public attention. They cannot tell us the true distribution of types among all comedians or musicians, or establish what made any person choose a career.

This opening is provisional; insert the dated snapshot and verified counts before submission.

## Points to tighten before submission

- **Separate compilation from original research.** Describe the external data as published reports reviewed by 9takes. Describe the 9takes corpus separately as editorial classifications. Do not imply that DJ recruited the survey participants or measured their cultural values.
- **Qualify explanations.** The existing 9takes article says neither provider made a mistake. The comparison alone cannot establish that. Use wording such as: “Different samples and methods can produce different distributions; these reports do not tell us how much each factor contributed.”
- **Keep sample size and representativeness separate.** More observations do not automatically remove selection bias or validate a test's classifications.
- **Keep the corpus modest.** A profession pattern can reflect the people chosen for coverage, the available public record, or the editor's expectations as well as anything about the people themselves. State these alternatives beside the observation.
- **Avoid unsupported superlatives.** “Two published reports” is defensible here. “The only datasets” or “the largest available sample” requires a wider, current review.
- **Keep demographic language precise.** Preserve each source's category labels. Do not assume that a table of men and women establishes biological, cultural, or causal explanations.
