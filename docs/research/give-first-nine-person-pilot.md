# Answer Before the Crowd — Nine-Person Pilot

**Status:** Ready to recruit and run
**Created:** 2026-08-13
**Owner:** DJ Wayne
**Purpose:** Produce one real, unfakeable demonstration of how nine people can experience the same situation as different emotional emergencies.
**Message source:** [`docs/brand/messaging-hierarchy.md`](../brand/messaging-hierarchy.md)

## The question

> **When someone you trust is afraid of a decision you believe is right, what do you do?**

Optional context shown equally to every participant:

> Think of a real decision involving a partner, friend, family member, or teammate. You believe moving forward is right; they are worried about the consequences. What do you notice first, what feels at stake, and what do you do next?

Working slug:

`when-someone-you-trust-is-afraid-of-a-decision-you-believe-is-right`

## What this pilot can and cannot establish

This is a qualitative demonstration, not a statistical proof of the Enneagram or a causal test of the answer-first mechanic.

It can show:

- Nine real independent answers to one emotionally ambiguous situation
- Which stakes, alarms, and recommended actions differ
- Which responses participants could not have generated on their own
- Whether the reveal creates useful self-recognition or empathy

It cannot show:

- That all people of a type answer the same way
- That type caused a particular answer
- That nine participants represent a population
- That answer-first produces more diversity than a conventional comment section

Do not turn the pilot into a numerical claim. If the qualitative result is strong, use the controlled follow-on design at the end of this document.

## Participants

Recruit nine adults, one for each **self-identified** Enneagram type.

Requirements:

- They identify their own type; DJ does not assign it.
- They can answer from a real or readily imagined situation.
- They have not seen any other participant’s answer.
- They understand that public attribution is optional.
- They are not required to defend or perform a type stereotype.

Prefer participants with different genders, ages, relationships to DJ, and professional backgrounds. Type diversity is the organizing device; human diversity keeps the result from becoming nine caricatures.

## Consent language

Send this before collecting an answer:

> I’m running a small 9takes pilot with one self-identified person of each Enneagram type. Everyone will answer the same question independently before seeing the other responses. After all nine answers are collected, I’ll show you the set and ask what surprised you.
>
> You can participate anonymously, under a first name, or with full attribution. You can also choose whether your written answer, audio/video, and post-reveal reflection may be published. This is a qualitative demonstration, not a diagnosis or research claim. You may withdraw before publication.

Capture these permissions separately:

- [ ] Written answer may be published
- [ ] Enneagram type may be published
- [ ] First name may be published
- [ ] Full name/profile may be published
- [ ] Audio/video may be published
- [ ] Post-reveal reflection may be published

## Collection protocol

### Stage 1 — Independent answer

Send the exact question and optional context. Do not mention DJ’s marriage story until after the participant has answered.

Ask for:

1. A written answer of 75–250 words
2. What do you notice first?
3. What feels most at stake?
4. What would you want the worried person to understand about your response?

Optional: record a 30–60 second voice memo or phone video reading the answer naturally.

Rules:

- Do not show examples.
- Do not name likely type patterns.
- Do not coach an answer toward anger, fear, or shame language.
- Do not share completion counts or tell someone how “different” their answer is.
- Store every raw answer before editing.

### Stage 2 — Neutral edit

Edit only for obvious transcription errors, repeated filler, and private identifying details. Preserve the participant’s framing and vocabulary.

Return any material edit to the participant for approval.

### Stage 3 — Reveal

After all nine answers are locked, show each participant the complete set in randomized, unlabeled order first.

Ask:

1. Which answer surprised you most?
2. Which answer felt hardest to understand?
3. Did any answer change what you would do in the situation?
4. Which answer sounded most like your own, apart from the one you wrote?
5. What did another person notice that you initially missed?

Only after this reflection may the type labels be revealed.

### Stage 4 — Publication approval

Show each participant the final representation of their answer, type label, attribution, and reflection. Get explicit approval before publishing.

## Analysis rubric

Code each answer without using the participant’s type label.

| Dimension            | Question                                                                                           |
| -------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **First signal**     | What does the person notice before anything else?                                                  |
| **Primary stake**    | What do they believe could be lost or damaged?                                                     |
| **Alarm language**   | Where do anger, fear, or shame appear, if they appear naturally?                                   |
| **Trust request**    | What do they want the other person to trust them about?                                            |
| **Default action**   | Push, pause, question, plan, reassure, withdraw, negotiate, harmonize, correct, or something else? |
| **Time orientation** | Immediate action, future risk, past evidence, or relational continuity?                            |
| **View of care**     | What behavior counts as caring in this answer?                                                     |
| **Blind spot**       | What perspective or cost receives the least attention?                                             |

Use at least two readers for the coding pass if the output will be described publicly. Resolve disagreements in writing rather than forcing a neat pattern.

## Recruitment message

> I’m running a small 9takes experiment: one self-identified person of each Enneagram type answering the same relationship/decision question before seeing anyone else’s answer.
>
> It takes about 10 minutes. After all nine are in, you’ll see the complete set and tell me what you noticed that you would have missed alone. You can be anonymous, and nothing is published without your approval.
>
> Would you be willing to represent your own perspective—not your type as a stereotype?

## Public deliverable

The final artifact should have four layers:

1. **The question** — presented without the founder story
2. **The nine independent answers** — participant language first
3. **The reveal reflections** — what each person missed
4. **DJ’s synthesis** — the two-emergencies story and the limits of the pilot

Recommended title:

> **One decision. Nine different emergencies.**

This is an artifact title, not an addition to the master messaging hierarchy.

Recommended closing:

> The point is not that nine people produced nine mutually exclusive truths. The point is that each person noticed a real stake that others could overlook. Answer before the crowd, then become curious about what your answer cannot see.

## Success gate

The pilot is worth publishing when:

- [ ] All nine types are represented by self-identification.
- [ ] All nine answers were locked before any participant saw the room.
- [ ] At least seven participants approve public use of their written answer and type.
- [ ] At least five participants name a perspective they initially missed.
- [ ] The answers contain meaningful variation in stakes or actions without being edited into stereotypes.
- [ ] At least one answer complicates the expected type pattern.
- [ ] The final artifact states the limitations plainly.

## Controlled follow-on

If this pilot creates a clear signal, test the answer-first mechanism separately:

1. Recruit at least 90 participants.
2. Randomly assign half to answer the same question with no responses visible.
3. Show the other half three seeded answers before they respond.
4. Pre-register measures: phrase reuse, semantic similarity, number of distinct stakes, answer length, and self-reported confidence.
5. Analyze without using Enneagram type as the primary causal claim.

That study could support a defensible statement about anchoring. This nine-person pilot cannot, and should not be presented as if it does.
