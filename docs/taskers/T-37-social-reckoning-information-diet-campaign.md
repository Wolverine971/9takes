## 0. What and why

Build one evidence-backed pillar article and one focused social campaign around DJ's realization about _The Social Reckoning_:

> Social platforms do not merely show people different opinions about the same facts. They increasingly determine which facts, examples, people, and emotional cues enter each person's field of view. Each person receives a tailored information diet, then mistakes that sample for the whole world.

The central metaphor is food. A person who repeatedly chooses candy is served more candy. A person who pauses on outrage is served more outrage. A person who consumes one worldview is offered more of that worldview. The danger is not limited to false information. It also includes omission: facts, experiences, and interpretations that never reach the tray.

This idea is unusually close to the original 9takes mission. 9takes was built to challenge perspective bubbles by asking people to form their own answer first and then compare it with answers produced by different minds. The campaign should make that connection explicit without turning the article into a product advertisement.

**Editorial decision:** Use _The Social Reckoning_ as the timely doorway, but make the article evergreen. The movie is the hook. The lasting subject is personalized reality.

**Recommended article title:** `Your Feed Is Not Reality. It Is an Information Diet.`

**Recommended one-sentence promise:** Understand why sincere people can feel as if they live in different worlds, then audit what your own feed keeps serving and what it leaves out.

**Recommended target file:** `src/blog/community/your-feed-is-an-information-diet.md`

**Recommended campaign folder:** `docs/distribution-assets/information-diet-social-reckoning-2026-10/`

## 2. Thesis to develop

The article should distinguish three layers that are often collapsed:

1. **Personal filters:** temperament, fears, identity, experience, and attention make people notice different parts of the same event.
2. **Social selection:** people choose friends, creators, outlets, communities, and topics that feel relevant or rewarding.
3. **Algorithmic selection:** platforms rank a tiny portion of available content for each user based on predicted interest and engagement.

The distinctive 9takes insight sits where those layers reinforce one another:

> Your personality affects what you reach for. Your behavior teaches the feed what to serve. The feed repeats that selection until a personalized sample starts to feel like reality itself.

The strongest formulation is not, "the algorithm makes everyone believe lies." It is:

> The feed's deepest power may be deciding what gets a chance to enter your mind at all.

Two sincere people can reason carefully and still disagree because they are working with different menus of facts, anecdotes, authorities, threats, and examples. Each person then assumes the other has seen what they have seen.

### Important nuance

Do not romanticize the broadcast era as a period of complete, neutral, or "flat" truth. Older media diets were narrower and more shared, but they were still selective, commercial, and imperfect. The change worth exploring is the move from a more overlapping public information environment to individualized, continuously optimized feeds.

Do not claim that personality type determines feed composition. Personality is one plausible contributor to attention and selection. It is not the only one, and the article must not present an Enneagram hypothesis as established causal science.

## 3. Research requirements

Create a compact evidence log before drafting. Prefer primary sources, platform documentation, peer-reviewed research, and high-quality institutional reviews. Record URL, publisher, publication date, access date, exact supported claim, and limitations.

Research and separate the following questions:

1. How do Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, and X describe recommendation or ranking inputs in their current official documentation?
2. What does research actually show about algorithmic ranking, self-selection, selective exposure, echo chambers, and polarization?
3. What evidence supports the idea that repetition changes perceived importance, familiarity, credibility, or normality?
4. What evidence distinguishes misinformation from omission and uneven exposure?
5. What can responsibly be said about children and adolescents? Separate developmental vulnerability, repeated exposure, platform design, parental context, and demonstrated outcomes. Do not write a broad causal claim such as "different feeds produce different children" without strong direct evidence.
6. What did Facebook or Meta know internally, according to primary documents or attributed reporting, and what did Frances Haugen allege? Keep allegations, internal findings, and independently established facts clearly labeled.
7. Which claims belong to the real-world evidence, and which belong only to the movie's premise or marketing?

At minimum, obtain:

- one current official recommendation-system explanation from Meta;
- one primary or peer-reviewed source on selective exposure or algorithmic ranking;
- one source on repetition, familiarity, salience, or perceived consensus;
- one rigorous source on youth and social media that states both findings and limitations;
- the Sony film synopsis;
- the Haugen testimony or another primary document;
- one credible counterpoint showing where popular "filter bubble" claims overreach.

**Stop rule:** research is sufficient when every consequential causal claim in the outline has direct support, one credible counterpoint has been incorporated, and additional sources are repeating the same result rather than changing the argument.

## 4. Required article structure

### Opening: Two people, two trays

Open with a concrete scene. Two people unlock the same app in the same room. One receives outrage, institutional failure, and danger. The other receives aspiration, humor, and reassurance. Neither feed is wholly fabricated. Neither feed is the whole.

Land the thesis within the first 150 words:

> You do not see the internet. You see a menu selected from it.

### Section 1: A feed is a diet, not a window

Explain the metaphor precisely:

- available information is the full market;
- the recommendation system is the cafeteria and ranking process;
- clicks, pauses, follows, searches, shares, and skips are revealed preferences;
- the feed is the plate;
- repeated consumption shapes what feels important, normal, or common;
- missing foods represent facts and experiences that never enter awareness.

Avoid labeling one ideology as vegetables and another as candy. The nutritional categories refer to informational function, not political position.

### Section 2: The candy feedback loop

Explain the loop without pretending it is mind control:

1. Something captures attention.
2. The platform records behavior.
3. Similar material becomes more likely to appear.
4. Repetition makes the topic feel more prevalent or important.
5. The user engages again because the feed now fits existing interest.

Make clear that the person, their network, the creator economy, and the platform all participate in the loop.

### Section 3: The missing ingredient is often invisible

The most important harm may be omission rather than an obvious lie. A person cannot evaluate an example, event, or counterargument that never appears. This creates a specific form of overconfidence: mistaking a highly selected sample for a representative view of reality.

Use the line:

> The hardest blind spot to notice is the fact your feed never gave you a reason to search for.

### Section 4: Why reasonable people stop sharing a factual floor

Show how arguments fail when people assume shared exposure. They may use the same words while referring to different event histories, authorities, emotional examples, and base rates.

Do not imply that every disagreement is caused by feeds or that all claims are equally true. A common factual floor can be rebuilt through source checking, deliberate comparison, and exposure to missing context.

### Section 5: Children are still learning what counts as normal

Include this only after the evidence review. The section should focus on developing norms, salience, repetition, and the design responsibilities of adults and platforms. Avoid deterministic language and clinical claims.

The responsible framing is:

> A tailored feed is one influence in a child's information environment. Its effects depend on age, content, intensity, relationships, supervision, and offline life.

### Section 6: Why The Social Reckoning matters

Use the movie as a cultural catalyst, not as proof. Explain that the official premise follows Frances Haugen and Jeff Horwitz as guarded internal information becomes public. Connect this to the distinction between a platform's public story, its internal knowledge, and each user's individualized experience.

### Section 7: The 9takes response

Explain the design connection honestly:

- answer before reading others, which creates friction against first-frame capture;
- reveal multiple perspectives after contribution;
- add personality context to help explain why attention and interpretation differ;
- treat every take, including one's own, as partial rather than complete.

Do not claim that 9takes eliminates echo chambers. Say that it deliberately introduces comparison, independent response, and curiosity where conventional feeds tend to optimize continuity and engagement.

### Ending: Audit your tray

End with a practical seven-day audit:

1. Sample the last 20 recommended posts on one feed.
2. Label each by topic, emotional effect, source type, and whether it adds evidence or merely reinforces a feeling.
3. Identify the three subjects the feed repeatedly serves.
4. Identify one credible perspective or source class that never appears.
5. Deliberately seek a primary source and a good-faith counterargument.
6. Compare notes with someone whose feed looks different.
7. Write what changed in your view and what did not.

Primary CTA:

> What is something your feed makes seem normal that another person's feed might never show them?

Draft this as a 9takes question. Do not publish it without DJ's approval.

## 5. Voice and editorial guardrails

Write in DJ's voice: curious, direct, pattern-focused, personal, and willing to admit uncertainty. The article should feel like a realization unfolding, not a lecture delivered from above.

### Preserve this language

- `Your feed is not reality. It is an information diet.`
- `You do not see the internet. You see a menu selected from it.`
- `The feed's deepest power may be deciding what gets a chance to enter your mind at all.`
- `Two honest people can still be arguing from different sets of facts.`
- `A feed is a sample, not the world.`
- `The hardest blind spot to notice is the fact your feed never gave you a reason to search for.`

### Avoid these claims

- The old media environment delivered complete or neutral truth.
- Algorithms are the only cause of polarization.
- Platforms brainwash every user.
- Every person lives in a sealed echo chamber.
- Repetition makes a claim true.
- Political balance automatically equals factual accuracy.
- Children who see one kind of content inevitably develop one kind of outcome.
- Enneagram type determines what a platform serves.
- The movie proves what happened inside Meta.

### Style constraints

- No em dashes.
- No partisan examples in the opening or headline.
- No diagnosis of users, creators, executives, or actors.
- No scolding the reader for their consumption.
- No food or body language that moralizes eating behavior.
- Attribute disputed claims and distinguish evidence from inference.
- Use the movie title sparingly enough that the page remains useful after the release cycle.

## 6. Social campaign package

The campaign name is **Your Feed Is a Diet**. It belongs primarily to **Pattern Lab**, with one **Carousel-to-Reel Reach Loop** adaptation. Run one coherent campaign, not a pile of disconnected posts.

### Deliverable A: Eight-slide carousel

1. **Cover:** `Your feed is not reality. It is an information diet.`
2. **The selection:** `You do not see the internet. You see a menu selected from it.`
3. **The candy loop:** What captures attention gets served again.
4. **The familiarity effect:** Repetition can make a topic feel more common, important, or normal.
5. **The invisible gap:** The danger is also what never reaches your tray.
6. **The argument problem:** Two honest people can be reasoning from different sets of facts.
7. **The audit:** Inspect 20 recommendations. What is repeated? What is absent?
8. **CTA:** `What does your feed make seem normal? Read the full essay, then add your take.`

Every slide must make sense without the movie. Mention _The Social Reckoning_ in the caption and final distribution timing, not on every slide.

### Deliverable B: Thirty to forty-five second Reel

Required opening hook:

> Two people can argue about the same world while being fed different worlds.

Required beats:

1. You click what interests or alarms you.
2. The platform learns and serves more.
3. Repetition starts to feel representative.
4. Missing context stays invisible.
5. Audit the tray before calling it reality.

End on one question, not a generic follow request.

### Deliverable C: Text posts

Draft:

- three single-post variants;
- one five-post thread;
- one LinkedIn variant centered on institutional incentives and information environments;
- three Story prompts or polls;
- one email/newsletter intro that points to the pillar article.

At least one post should use the exact line:

> The most powerful thing your feed controls may not be what you believe. It may be what you get a chance to know.

### Deliverable D: 9takes participation prompt

Draft one primary question and two alternates. Recommended primary:

> What is something your feed makes seem normal that another person's feed might never show them?

Provide neutral context and avoid asking users to reveal political identity. The goal is to compare exposure, not provoke a partisan roll call.

### Deliverable E: Visual brief

Use a split cafeteria or tray concept. Two people sit at the same table but receive different plates assembled from headlines, faces, symbols, and emotional cues. Avoid literal platform UI, brand logos, grotesque food, or a simplistic good-food versus bad-food moral.

The visual should communicate selective abundance: both trays are full, but neither tray contains the whole kitchen.

## 8. Measurement

Choose one primary CTA for the whole campaign: article reader to 9takes question contribution.

Track:

- article entrances by source and campaign;
- engaged reading or scroll depth;
- clicks from article to the 9takes question;
- question-view to contribution conversion;
- carousel save and share rate;
- Reel three-second hold and completion rate;
- qualified comments that describe a missing or repeated part of someone's feed;
- email click-through rate;
- return visits to the article during the film's release week.

Use one consistent UTM campaign name, recommended: `information_diet_social_reckoning`.

Do not treat raw views, impressions, or follower growth as sufficient success. The useful signal is whether people inspect their own information environment, compare it with another person's, and contribute a specific example.

## 10. Risks and gotchas

- **Shared truth nostalgia:** A common broadcast diet produced more overlap, not perfect truth. Do not claim otherwise.
- **Algorithm monocausality:** Personal choice, networks, creators, advertisers, institutions, and recommendation systems interact.
- **Omission versus falsehood:** Missing context is the distinctive thesis. Do not collapse it into a generic misinformation article.
- **Children and causality:** This is the highest-risk section. Narrow or remove it if the evidence cannot support the proposed language.
- **Diet moralism:** The metaphor becomes weaker when it labels people or political positions as junk. Keep the focus on balance, repetition, provenance, and missing inputs.
- **Movie uncertainty:** The film is unreleased. Do not infer scenes, arguments, or quality from a synopsis.
- **Brand overreach:** 9takes creates useful friction and comparison. It does not guarantee objectivity or eliminate polarization.
- **Campaign drift:** Do not turn this into five simultaneous arguments about addiction, censorship, child safety, elections, mental health, and corporate malice. The spine is personalized exposure and mistaken completeness.
- **Parallel work:** Other agents and DJ edit this repo. Never stash, reset, or rewrite unrelated files.
