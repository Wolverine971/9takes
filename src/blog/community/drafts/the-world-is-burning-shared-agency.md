<p class="firstLetter">The city is burning behind them.</p>

Smoke swallows the skyline. Buildings glow orange. A group of people sits around a smaller fire in
the foreground, close enough to see one another's faces.

Every one of them is looking at a phone.

The obvious reading is that they are distracted while the world falls apart. That they do not care.
That if they would only look up, they would finally notice the emergency.

That reading is not wrong. But it is incomplete.

I think they know the city is burning. It is probably all over their feeds.

They have footage from nine angles. They have the breaking-news alert, the expert explanation, the
angry reaction, the counter-reaction, and the argument about whose fault it is. They know more about
the fire than any person standing that far away has ever known about a fire.

What they do not have is a shared answer to one question:

**What are we supposed to do?**

That is the feeling this image captures. The world appears to be breaking in public while each of us
processes it in private.

## We Have Planet-Scale Awareness and Individual-Scale Agency

Your phone can show you a war, a flood, an election, a shooting, a financial collapse, a public
scandal, and an angry stranger before you get out of bed.

That awareness is not fake. The events are real. Staying informed matters.

But the feed performs a strange trick. It places problems from every level of human life into the
same hand-sized frame. A broken traffic light in your neighborhood sits beside a humanitarian crisis
on another continent. Both demand an emotional response. Neither arrives with a clear description of
your role.

You receive the alarm without the agency.

The result is **attention without leverage**: you can see the problem, feel the problem, talk about the
problem, and still have no meaningful way to affect it.

This is not a niche reaction. The [Reuters Institute's 2026 Digital News Report](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2026/dnr-executive-summary)
found that 42 percent of respondents across its markets avoid the news sometimes or often, up from 29
percent when it began tracking the measure in 2017. [Research on doomscrolling](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S245195882400071X)
has also found associations with distress, future anxiety, and a more pessimistic view of human
nature.

That research does not prove that the phone causes despair. People who are already anxious may seek
more information. Real events can justify fear. The relationship runs in both directions.

But the loop is recognizable:

**Something feels dangerous → you search for more information → the information gives you more to
fear → you keep searching because you still do not know what to do.**

The problem is not that you care too much.

The problem is that caring has been disconnected from acting.

The [9takes Manifesto](/manifesto) begins with a related problem: the internet collapses nine
perspectives into one winning take. That architecture does not only flatten disagreement. It keeps
each person responding to a distant crowd instead of discovering what the people beside them
actually see. You can know what the internet thinks about a crisis and still have no idea what your
own community thinks it should do.

## “Just Log Off” Is Not an Answer

Putting the phone down can interrupt the loop. It can protect your attention and give your nervous
system room to settle.

But it does not answer the question in the image.

If the world contains real danger, private calm is not a complete response. You can meditate while
your neighborhood deteriorates. You can perfect your morning routine while the people around you
become more isolated. You can become extremely well regulated and still be alone.

The answer cannot only be to know less.

It has to include becoming more capable of responding to what you know.

And that ability rarely exists alone.

## The Missing Thing Is Shared Agency

Psychologist Albert Bandura used the term **collective efficacy** for a group's shared belief in its
ability to produce an effect through collective action. In plain English: _we believe we can do
something together, and experience has given us a reason to believe it._

That is different from being in the same place. It is different from following the same account,
sharing the same politics, or sitting in the same group chat.

A crowd can feel the same emotion without possessing shared agency. An audience can agree about a
problem without being able to act on it. A community becomes capable when people trust one another
enough to make and keep commitments for something beyond themselves.

[One influential study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9252316/) of 8,782 residents across 343
Chicago neighborhoods defined neighborhood collective efficacy as social cohesion combined with a
willingness to intervene for the common good. Higher collective efficacy was associated with lower
violence even after the researchers accounted for several individual and neighborhood factors.

The important part is the combination.

Connection matters. Willingness to act matters. Neither does the entire job alone.

The [U.S. Surgeon General's advisory on social connection](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf)
reaches a similar conclusion at a wider scale. Social connection affects individual health, but
connected communities also build resilience, safety, civic participation, and the ability to recover
from crises.

The opposite of doomscrolling is not optimism.

**It is shared agency.**

## Think Like a Builder, Not a Problem Hunter

There is a trap waiting here.

Once you decide to help, you can start scanning every person and place for what is wrong. If you are
a hammer, everything begins to look like a nail. The community becomes a pile of deficiencies. You
become the person with the plan.

Then you show up and tell everyone what they need.

That is not community building. It is control with friendly branding.

The [Asset-Based Community Development](https://abcdinstitute.org/) tradition starts somewhere else:
with the gifts of residents, the power of local groups, the relationships already present, and the
strengths a community can mobilize. Its public principles include starting with gifts, valuing small
actions, nurturing community-led action, believing in possibility, and leading by stepping back.

That does not mean pretending everything is fine. “Good vibes only” can become its own form of
denial. Some things are broken. Some people are being hurt. Some elephants stay in the room because
saying the obvious feels socially dangerous.

A builder sees the whole field:

- **Protect what is good.**
- **Repair what is causing harm.**
- **Build what is missing.**

The negative can create urgency. The positive supplies energy. A functioning community needs both.

## Read the Vibes Before You Offer the Plan

Reading the vibes is not mystical. It is pattern recognition.

Start with three questions:

1. **What keeps coming up?**
2. **What is the context?**
3. **What is emotionally at stake?**

People reveal what matters through recurrence. A friend repeatedly talks about leaving a job. Parents
keep returning to the same school policy. A neighborhood group cannot get through a meeting without
mentioning the empty storefront on the corner. A team keeps joking about how nobody knows who makes
decisions.

The repeated topic is a signal. It is not yet an explanation.

Context changes the meaning. The person talking about work may feel trapped, bored, ambitious,
afraid, or unseen. The empty storefront may represent safety to one neighbor, lost history to another,
and possibility to a third.

You read the pattern. You consider the context. Then you test your interpretation with a real
question:

> You keep coming back to this. What about it feels important to you?

For a group:

> This keeps coming up. What does that tell us about what people here care about?

That final question protects curiosity from turning into diagnosis. Your read is a hypothesis. The
person living the experience gets to correct it.

This is the 9takes way of entering a community: **questions over verdicts.** Understand the
perspectives in the room before you decide what the room should become.

## Work From the Inside Out

The phrase “change the world” is almost useless as an instruction. The world is too large to be your
first unit of action.

Start closer.

### Tend to yourself

What do you continually return to? Which story, fear, hope, or frustration keeps taking your
attention? What does it reveal about what you care about?

Then ask what is actually yours to do.

Tending to yourself is not retreating into self-improvement while everyone else suffers. It is
building the capacity to make a promise and keep it. You cannot create shared agency if your only
available energy is outrage followed by exhaustion.

### Tend to your community

Your community is the place where you already possess some context and accountability: your family,
friend group, neighborhood, workplace, church, club, or another group where people know you.

Do not begin by announcing a mission. Listen for what keeps coming up. Ask what it means. Notice what
people are already proud of, what they are quietly worried about, and where energy is already
gathering.

Then ask what people want to protect, repair, or build together.

### Tend to an outside community

An outside community is one whose experience you do not share from the inside.

The farther you move from your own lived context, the more your listening should increase and your
certainty should decrease.

Find the people already doing the work. Ask what they have learned. Understand the history behind the
recurring topics. Offer your skills without assuming that your preferred solution is the missing
piece.

Community agency disappears when outsiders keep the power to define the problem, set the agenda, and
judge the result.

[A 2023 review of community-agency initiatives](https://whatworkswellbeing.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Rapid-Review-Community-Agency-and-Control.pdf)
found encouraging evidence that participation can improve trust, belonging, neighborhood
connections, and people's influence over decisions. It also found neutral or negative results in
more than half of the 27 studies it examined. Power imbalances, unresolved losses, structural
barriers, and short-lived programs all got in the way.

Community is not automatically good because people gathered.

The people affected have to possess real influence over what happens next.

## What Can You Do This Week?

Do not begin by creating an organization, designing a logo, or writing a manifesto.

Try this:

1. **Choose one community you already belong to.**
2. **Notice what keeps coming up.** Do not decide too quickly whether it is positive or negative.
3. **Ask three people what the recurring subject means to them.** Listen for different emotional
   stakes.
4. **Name one thing worth protecting, repairing, or building.** Offer it as a read, not a verdict.
5. **Invite one small shared action.** Make it useful, voluntary, and small enough to complete soon.

If several people discuss the question together, have everyone write their answer before the first
person speaks. **Answer before the crowd.** It keeps the loudest or fastest person from defining what
the whole room is allowed to notice.

Maybe people keep talking about how nobody knows their neighbors. Do not launch a nonprofit. Invite
four people to a meal.

Maybe parents keep talking about the same dangerous intersection. Ask them to document what they see
for a week, compare notes before reading one another's answers, and decide together whether to contact
the city.

Maybe your friends keep saying they are exhausted and never see one another. Put one recurring date
on the calendar and share responsibility for keeping it alive.

The first action does not need to solve the entire problem.

It needs to prove something smaller and more important:

**We noticed something together. We understood it well enough to act. We made a promise. We kept it.**

That is how shared agency begins.

## Look Up

Go back to the image.

There are two fires.

One is consuming the city. It is enormous, distant, and beyond the control of any single person in
the circle.

The other is in front of them. It is small enough to tend.

The people do not need another angle of the skyline. They need to look up long enough to recognize
who is already beside them.

Looking up will not make the larger fire disappear. Building community will not solve every crisis or
protect us from every disaster.

But the first answer to “What can I do?” is no longer “nothing.”

It is:

**Find the people near you. Understand what keeps coming up. Choose one thing worth tending. Act
together.**

What keeps coming up in your community, and what do you think it is really about?
