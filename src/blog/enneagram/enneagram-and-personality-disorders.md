<QuickAnswer question="Is there a link between the Enneagram and personality disorders?">
<strong>A partial one, measured once, and no type causes or predicts a disorder.</strong> In the only clinical study we found, 117 outpatients in Istanbul took a nine-type scale adapted from the Enneagram. Type 4 had the strongest link to borderline symptoms, Type 2 to histrionic, Type 8 to antisocial, and Type 3 to narcissistic. Those links are weak to moderate, nobody has replicated them, and Enneagram teachers disagree about several of them. A type describes what you fear. A diagnosis comes from a clinician.
</QuickAnswer>

<p class="firstLetter">Your sister's moods swing so hard you've started reading about borderline personality disorder at 1 a.m. Your ex called you a psychopath on the way out the door. A coworker says the new hire is "so histrionic" and, in the same breath, "such a Two." Then the search narrows: which Enneagram type is most likely to have this?</p>

That question deserves a straight answer, and the straight answer has two parts. Researchers have tested it directly once that we could find, and the results are real enough to discuss and far too small to type anyone by. Two of the best-known Enneagram traditions also mapped the types onto personality disorders decades ago, and they don't agree with each other, or with the data, on every point.

This page lays out all three side by side: the study, the teachers, and the clinical definitions. It covers borderline (BPD), histrionic (HPD), antisocial personality and psychopathy, avoidant, dependent, obsessive-compulsive (OCPD), schizoid and paranoid. Narcissism has [its own page](/enneagram-corner/which-enneagram-type-is-most-likely-to-be-a-narcissist).

## What a personality disorder is, and what a type isn't

The two words sound related. They describe different things.

A personality disorder is a set of "pervasive, enduring patterns of thinking, perceiving, reacting, and relating" that cause real distress or get in the way of work and relationships ([MSD Manual, Zimmerman](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/personality-disorders/overview-of-personality-disorders)). About 9% of the general population has one. The DSM sorts the ten disorders into three clusters:

- **Cluster A, odd or eccentric:** paranoid, schizoid, schizotypal
- **Cluster B, dramatic, emotional or erratic:** antisocial, borderline, histrionic, narcissistic
- **Cluster C, anxious or fearful:** avoidant, dependent, obsessive-compulsive

An Enneagram type describes a motivation: the fear you organize your life around and the strategy you use to manage it. Every type has a healthy, functional version, and most people of every type never come near a diagnosis. A disorder describes how rigid and costly a pattern has become, whatever motive sits under it.

The teachers who did map the types to disorders put the match at the bottom of the scale. The Enneagram Institute describes nine levels of health for each type and attaches its DSM labels at Level 9, the most unhealthy. Its Type 8 page, for example, says the extreme Level 9 Eight "generally corresponds to the Antisocial Personality Disorder" ([Enneagram Institute, Type 8](https://www.enneagraminstitute.com/type-8)). Even in that framework, a type is the starting point and a disorder is a far end few people reach.

## What the research actually shows

The 2021 systematic review of Enneagram research covered 104 samples. It lists one study that compared the types with DSM personality disorders ([Hook et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1002/jclp.23097)). Everything below leans on it, so here is exactly what it was.

In 2014, Enver Demirel Yilmaz and colleagues recruited 117 outpatients at a university psychiatry clinic in Istanbul. Everyone had been diagnosed with personality pathology, and anyone with another psychiatric diagnosis or a chronic physical illness was excluded. Each person took the Nine Types of Temperament Scale, a 91-item questionnaire the authors adapted from the Enneagram. The researchers then ran the SCID-II, a structured diagnostic interview for personality disorders, and counted each person's symptoms for every disorder ([Yilmaz et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.5350/DAJPN2016290102)).

Every type scale correlated with at least one disorder. Here are the strongest links for each disorder.

<div class="scroll-table">

| Disorder             | Strongest type scales in the clinic (r) | What it means                                                                                                        |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Borderline (BPD)     | 4 (.53), 7 (.33), 2 (.30), 6 (.29)      | The strongest link in the whole study. Type 1 ran negative (-.22).                                                   |
| Histrionic (HPD)     | 2 (.50), 4 (.37), 7 (.35)               | The second-strongest link. Types 3, 6 and 8 were weaker but positive.                                                |
| Antisocial (ASPD)    | 8 (.36)                                 | The only significant positive link. Type 9 ran clearly negative (-.29).                                              |
| Narcissistic (NPD)   | 3 (.46), 8 (.27), 4 (.24)               | Covered in depth on [our narcissism page](/enneagram-corner/which-enneagram-type-is-most-likely-to-be-a-narcissist). |
| Dependent            | 2 (.45), 6 (.40), 9 (.24)               | Both Enneagram traditions below put dependent personality at Type 9 instead.                                         |
| Obsessive-compulsive | 6 (.40), 1 (.37), 5 (.28)               | Type 6 edged out Type 1, the type both traditions name.                                                              |
| Avoidant             | 6 (.38), 5 (.34), 9 (.23)               | Types 7 and 8 ran negative (-.25 each).                                                                              |
| Schizoid             | 5 (.50)                                 | Types 2 and 7 ran negative.                                                                                          |
| Paranoid             | 5 (.35), 6 (.33), 4 (.27)               | Type 6 is the tradition's pick; Type 5 slightly beat it here.                                                        |

</div>

### How much a correlation of .53 tells you

A correlation runs from 0 (no relationship) to 1 (a perfect one). Square it and you get a rough share of the variation one score accounts for in the other. The Type 4 and borderline link, the strongest in the study, works out to about 28%. That leaves more than 70% to everything else: history, trauma, temperament the scale didn't capture, and chance. The study's own scale called anything from .4 to .6 a "medium" correlation and anything from .2 to .4 "weak," which describes most of the table.

### Four reasons not to lean on it too hard

1. **One clinic, no replication.** We found no second study that gave Enneagram measures and a diagnostic interview to the same people.
2. **Everyone was already a patient.** The study compares symptom counts among people who all had personality pathology. It cannot tell you how often a Four in the general population has BPD.
3. **The scale isn't a standard Enneagram test.** It's the authors' own temperament model built from the Enneagram, scored on a three-point "yes, sometimes, no" scale.
4. **Some overlap is built in.** The paper describes its Type 6 as, among other things, "obsessive," "anxious, having paranoid touchiness, pessimistic, distrustful." When a type's description already uses a disorder's vocabulary, finding that type correlated with the disorder is partly a check on wording.

One more source belongs on the list. A 2023 doctoral project at Florida Tech surveyed 891 people in psychotherapy with a short Enneagram inventory and reported "several significant associations" between types and DSM-5-TR diagnostic categories ([Huber, 2023](https://repository.fit.edu/etd/1520)). We couldn't access the full text, so we can't tell you which types or whether personality disorders were among them, and we haven't used it for any claim below.

## Where the Enneagram teachers put each disorder

Long before anyone ran the numbers, two traditions mapped the types onto the DSM. Claudio Naranjo, the Chilean psychiatrist whose _Character and Neurosis_ matched each type to clinical character descriptions, did it chapter by chapter. Don Riso and Russ Hudson's Enneagram Institute does it at Level 9 of each type page.

<div class="scroll-table">

| Disorder             | Naranjo                            | Enneagram Institute (Level 9) | Clinic data (strongest) |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ----------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Borderline           | 4                                  | Not linked to any type        | 4                       |
| Histrionic           | 2                                  | 2 and 7                       | 2                       |
| Antisocial           | 8                                  | 8                             | 8                       |
| Narcissistic         | 7                                  | 3 and 4                       | 3                       |
| Dependent            | 9, and one variety of 6            | 9                             | 2, then 6               |
| Obsessive-compulsive | 1                                  | 1                             | 6, then 1               |
| Avoidant             | The self-preservation variety of 6 | 4 and 5                       | 6, then 5               |
| Schizoid             | 5                                  | 5 and 9                       | 5                       |
| Paranoid             | 6                                  | 6                             | 5, then 6               |

</div>

Read the table by rows. Where all three columns agree, on antisocial, histrionic and schizoid, you have the most support this subject has to offer. Where they split, on narcissistic, dependent and borderline, anyone who tells you "it's obviously Type X" is choosing one tradition and ignoring the others.

Two notes keep the agreement honest. First, our read is that the three columns aren't fully independent: the clinic's scale was built from the Enneagram, and the Enneagram's clinical vocabulary traces partly back to Naranjo's own mapping. Second, Naranjo found one type that never shows up. He noted that the Type 3 disposition was the only one not included in the DSM-III, and wondered whether that was because it had been the modal personality in American society since the 1920s ([Naranjo, ch. 7](https://www.claudionaranjo.net/pdf_files/not_catagorized/character_and_neurosis_nine_types_english/character_neurosis_7_english.pdf)).

## Which Enneagram type is most likely to be a psychopath?

Nobody has measured psychopathy across the nine types, so any confident answer is a guess. Here is what can be said.

**Psychopathy isn't a DSM diagnosis.** The closest formal one is antisocial personality disorder (ASPD): "a pervasive pattern of disregard for consequences and for the rights of others." It requires signs such as deceitfulness, aggression, recklessness and lack of remorse, plus evidence of conduct disorder before age 15, and it affects an estimated 2% to 5% of people over their lifetime ([MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/personality-disorders/antisocial-personality-disorder-aspd)).

The two labels aren't interchangeable either. Robert Hare, who built the Psychopathy Checklist-Revised (PCL-R), put it plainly: most psychopaths meet the criteria for ASPD, but "most individuals with ASPD are not psychopaths" ([Hare, 1996](https://www.psychiatrictimes.com/view/psychopathy-and-antisocial-personality-disorder-case-diagnostic-confusion)). Psychopathy adds personality traits on top of the behavior, which is why it's scored by a trained evaluator and not a quiz. Our [Dark Triad guide](/pop-culture/dark-triad-meets-enneagram) covers how it's measured.

**If you force a pick, the evidence points at Type 8.** The Type 8 scale was the only one with a significant positive link to antisocial symptoms (r = .36, about 13% of the variation). Naranjo called antisocial personality "a pathological extreme and a special instance of ennea-type VIII" ([ch. 4](https://www.claudionaranjo.net/pdf_files/not_catagorized/character_and_neurosis_nine_types_english/character_neurosis_4_english.pdf)), and the Enneagram Institute links its Level 9 Eight to the same disorder. At the other end, the Type 9 scale ran negative (-.29).

**Why that pick misleads more than it helps.** The Enneagram Institute gives the Eight's basic fear as "being harmed or controlled by others." An Eight's aggression, in that reading, is organized around protection: it's loud, it's aimed at threats, and Eights are commonly described as fiercely loyal to the people inside their circle. The ASPD criteria point somewhere else: lack of remorse after the harm. That part is invisible during a fight, so a forceful, blunt Eight can look the part while a quiet, charming person of any type is the one who never feels bad about anything.

<InsightBox title="Watch the after, not the type" tone="info">
	<p>Intensity during a conflict tells you about temperament. What happens after tells you more. Does the person register what the harm cost you? Do they ever change the behavior once it's pointed out, with you or anyone else? A pattern of harm with no remorse, repeated over years and across relationships, is the thing clinicians look for. It can sit behind any of the nine types.</p>
</InsightBox>

## Which Enneagram type is most likely to have BPD?

Borderline personality disorder is "a pervasive pattern of instability and hypersensitivity in interpersonal relationships," with unstable self-image, extreme mood swings and impulsivity. A diagnosis requires at least five of nine criteria, including frantic efforts to avoid real or imagined abandonment, an unstable sense of self, chronic emptiness and intense anger. The estimated median prevalence is 2.7% ([MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/personality-disorders/borderline-personality-disorder-bpd)).

**The data and one tradition point at Type 4.** The Type 4 scale's correlation with borderline symptoms (.53) was the strongest in the whole study. Naranjo got there first, writing that "the 'borderline' diagnosis in practice is made in terms of ennea-type IV traits such as these: variability of mood, self-condemnation, impulsivity, rage" ([ch. 3](https://www.claudionaranjo.net/pdf_files/not_catagorized/character_and_neurosis_nine_types_english/character_neurosis_3_english.pdf)). Types 7 (.33), 2 (.30) and 6 (.29) followed at a distance.

**The other tradition doesn't link BPD to any type.** The Enneagram Institute's Level 9 Four "generally corresponds to the Avoidant, Depressive, and Narcissistic personality disorders" ([Type 4](https://www.enneagraminstitute.com/type-4)). None of its nine Level 9 descriptions mentions borderline.

### The fear of abandonment, and the type it doesn't fit

The internet usually explains the BPD and Type 4 link through fear of abandonment. The Enneagram's own basic fears complicate that story. Here is how the Enneagram Institute words the three that sound closest:

- **Type 2:** "Of being unwanted, unworthy of being loved"
- **Type 6:** "Of being without support and guidance"
- **Type 9:** "Of loss and separation"

The Nine's fear is the nearest match to abandonment on paper. In the clinic, the Type 9 scale had essentially no link to borderline symptoms (-.04).

The Four's basic fear is different: "That they have no identity or personal significance." That lines up with a different BPD criterion, the unstable sense of self.

Our read, which no study has tested: sharing a fear doesn't make two patterns the same, because what someone does with the fear matters more. A Nine's usual strategy with the dread of separation is to go quiet, merge and keep the peace. The BPD criterion describes "frantic efforts" to stop the loss. The same alarm can drive opposite behavior. Where the Four overlaps with BPD, the overlap may run through identity at least as much as through abandonment.

That distinction matters if you love someone with BPD, or with an intense Four, Two or Six temperament. "They're scared of being left" is often true of both. The difference shows in whether their sense of who they are, and how they feel about you, holds steady across a hard week.

### What helps

BPD has a reputation for being permanent. The long-term data says otherwise. In a 16-year study of 290 people first assessed as hospital inpatients, 78% to 99% reached sustained symptomatic remission, depending on how long the remission had to last. Full recovery, with good work and social functioning, was harder: 40% to 60% ([Zanarini et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2011.11101550)).

Psychotherapy is the main treatment. A 2020 Cochrane review found that therapies built for BPD, such as dialectical behavior therapy (DBT), beat usual care on all of its main outcomes, with the clearest improvement in BPD severity ([Storebø et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD012955.pub2)).

## Which Enneagram type is most likely to have HPD?

Histrionic personality disorder is "a pervasive pattern of excessive emotionality and attention seeking." The criteria include discomfort when not the center of attention, rapidly shifting and shallow emotions, theatrical expression, and treating relationships as more intimate than they are. It affects an estimated under 2% of people ([MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/personality-disorders/histrionic-personality-disorder-hpd)).

**Type 2, one of the few calls all three sources share.** The Type 2 scale correlated with histrionic symptoms at .50. Naranjo titled his Type 2 chapter "Pride and the Histrionic Personality" and wrote that "in the DSM III, ennea-type II is found under the label of 'Histrionic Personality Disorder'" ([ch. 6](https://www.claudionaranjo.net/pdf_files/not_catagorized/character_and_neurosis_nine_types_english/character_neurosis_6_english.pdf)). The Enneagram Institute links its Level 9 Two to the same disorder, and adds Type 7. In the data, Types 4 (.37) and 7 (.35) came next.

The overlap makes sense at the level of motive. A Two's attention goes to being wanted, and the HPD criteria describe a person whose sense of being okay depends on being noticed. Our read on where they part: a healthy Two's attention points outward, at what someone else needs. The histrionic pattern keeps pulling the room back to the self.

Use this one with care. Older reports found HPD mostly in women, but the MSD Manual now notes that "the prevalence in females and males is similar," and that the earlier skew may reflect who ended up in hospital-based studies. Calling a warm, emotional friend "histrionic" is a misuse of the word, whatever her type.

## The rest of the list: avoidant, dependent, OCPD, schizoid and paranoid

These five get fewer searches, but the pattern is the same: some overlap, no prediction.

### Avoidant personality disorder: Type 6 and Type 5

Avoidant personality disorder means avoiding social contact that carries a risk of rejection, and it affects about 2.1% of people ([MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/personality-disorders/avoidant-personality-disorder-avpd)). The clinic found Type 6 (.38) and Type 5 (.34) on top, while the assertive types, 7 and 8, ran negative.

The traditions split. The Enneagram Institute names Types 4 and 5. Naranjo tied avoidant personality to the self-preservation variety of Type 6 ([ch. 8](https://www.claudionaranjo.net/pdf_files/not_catagorized/character_and_neurosis_nine_types_english/character_neurosis_8_english.pdf)). The useful line is the MSD Manual's: people with schizoid personality isolate because they are uninterested in others, while people with avoidant personality isolate because "they are hypersensitive to possible rejection or criticism." A Five who withdraws to recharge is doing neither.

### Dependent personality disorder: Type 2, Type 6 or Type 9

Dependent personality disorder is "a pervasive, excessive need to be taken care of," shown in things like trouble making daily decisions without heavy reassurance and difficulty disagreeing for fear of losing support. Fewer than 1% of US adults are estimated to have it ([MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/personality-disorders/dependent-personality-disorder-dpd)).

Here the data and the teachers disagree outright. The clinic found Type 2 (.45) and Type 6 (.40) on top, with Type 9 a distant third (.24). Naranjo wrote that "of the DSM categories, the one most congruent with ennea-type IX is 'dependent personality'" ([ch. 9](https://www.claudionaranjo.net/pdf_files/not_catagorized/character_and_neurosis_nine_types_english/character_neurosis_9_english.pdf)), and the Enneagram Institute agrees. If you've read that "dependent personality is the unhealthy Nine," know that the only data we have points at Twos and Sixes first.

### Obsessive-compulsive personality disorder: Type 1, and Type 6

OCPD is a preoccupation with order, perfectionism and control that gets in the way of finishing things. It's one of the more common personality disorders, with an estimated median prevalence of 4.7% ([MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/personality-disorders/obsessive-compulsive-personality-disorder-ocpd)). It isn't OCD. People with OCD are often distressed by compulsions they can't control. In OCPD, the need for control feels like who they are.

Both traditions name Type 1. Naranjo calls Type 1 "the obsessive personality proper" while describing a rigid variety of Six that resembles it ([ch. 8](https://www.claudionaranjo.net/pdf_files/not_catagorized/character_and_neurosis_nine_types_english/character_neurosis_8_english.pdf)). The clinic data reflects that tension: Type 6 (.40) narrowly beat Type 1 (.37). Our [mental illness guide](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-and-mental-illness) covers the Type 1 perfectionism spiral in more detail.

### Schizoid personality disorder: Type 5

Schizoid personality disorder is "a pervasive pattern of detachment from and general disinterest" in relationships, with little emotional expression. The estimated median prevalence is 0.9% ([MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/personality-disorders/schizoid-personality-disorder-scpd)).

This is the third row where everything lines up. Naranjo wrote of the Five, "In DSM III we find our type in the 'schizoid personality disorder'" ([ch. 2](https://www.claudionaranjo.net/pdf_files/not_catagorized/character_and_neurosis_nine_types_english/character_neurosis_2_english.pdf)). The Enneagram Institute links Level 9 Fives (and Nines) to it, and the Type 5 scale correlated at .50.

The difference that matters day to day: a Five is commonly described as guarding time, energy and privacy so they can engage on their own terms. The schizoid pattern is a lack of interest in close relationships at all, including family. Wanting a lot of alone time is a preference, and on its own it isn't a symptom.

### Paranoid personality disorder: Type 6

Paranoid personality disorder is "a pervasive pattern of unwarranted distrust and suspicion of others," such as unjustified suspicion that people are exploiting or deceiving you. The estimated median prevalence is 3.2% ([MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/personality-disorders/paranoid-personality-disorder-ppd)).

Both traditions name Type 6. Naranjo also warned that "the DSM III definition of paranoid character is narrower than ennea-type VI," because the type includes several different ways of handling fear and only one of them looks like the disorder ([ch. 8](https://www.claudionaranjo.net/pdf_files/not_catagorized/character_and_neurosis_nine_types_english/character_neurosis_8_english.pdf)). In the clinic, Type 5 (.35) narrowly beat Type 6 (.33). A Six who runs worst-case scenarios before a big decision is doing what Sixes are commonly described as doing. Paranoid personality is when the suspicion stays fixed even after the evidence comes in.

## Why "most likely" is the wrong question

Suppose the clinic study were confirmed tomorrow by a large one, and Fours turned out to have twice the average rate of BPD. With a median prevalence of 2.7%, about 19 in 20 Fours still wouldn't have it. The same math holds for Twos and HPD, Eights and ASPD, and every other pair above. A type can make a pattern slightly more likely. It can't tell you whether the person in front of you has it.

The search usually starts with a specific person and a specific fear: whether they're safe, whether this is going to get worse, whether it's your fault. A number can't answer any of those. Behavior over time can.

The Enneagram earns its place on this subject by working the other way around. Under most of these patterns sits a recognizable fear: being left, being nobody, being controlled, being unloved. Seeing the fear doesn't excuse harm, and it doesn't replace a diagnosis. It does help you stop mistaking someone else's alarm for a defect, and it gives you better questions to bring to a professional than "which number is she?"

## If this is you or someone close to you

- **If you or someone you love is in danger of self-harm,** call or text 988 in the US, or chat at [988lifeline.org](https://988lifeline.org/). It's free, confidential and open 24/7, and it takes calls from people worried about someone else too.
- **If someone you love has BPD or BPD traits,** BPD Alliance (the National Education Alliance for Borderline Personality Disorder) runs [Family Connections](https://bpdalliance.org/family-connections/), a free, evidence-based program for family members.
- **If these criteria read like your life,** take them to a clinician who can run a proper assessment. - **Don't diagnose a partner, parent or coworker from a list,** this one included. Describe the specific behavior and what it costs you. That works whatever their type turns out to be.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is there a link between the Enneagram and personality disorders?

A weak to moderate one, measured once. In a 2016 study of 117 psychiatric outpatients in Istanbul, every scale of a nine-type temperament model adapted from the Enneagram correlated with symptoms of at least one personality disorder. No large or replicated study exists. No Enneagram type causes or predicts a personality disorder, and only a clinician can diagnose one.

### Which Enneagram type is most likely to be a psychopath?

No study has measured psychopathy across the nine types. Psychopathy is not a DSM diagnosis; the closest one is antisocial personality disorder. In the one clinical study, the Type 8 scale had the strongest link to antisocial symptoms (r = .36), and two major Enneagram traditions also connect Type 8 with antisocial personality. Most Eights are nothing like that, and most people with antisocial personality disorder are not psychopaths.

### Which Enneagram type is most likely to have BPD?

In the one clinical study, the Type 4 scale had the strongest correlation with borderline symptoms (r = .53), followed by Types 7, 2 and 6. Claudio Naranjo also tied the borderline picture to Type 4, while the Enneagram Institute does not link any type to BPD. A correlation from one clinic does not mean Fours are likely to have BPD, and BPD is treatable.

### Which Enneagram type is most likely to have histrionic personality disorder?

Type 2 is the most consistent answer. The Type 2 scale correlated with histrionic symptoms at r = .50 in the one clinical study, and both Claudio Naranjo and the Enneagram Institute connect the unhealthy Two with histrionic personality disorder. Types 4 and 7 came next in the data. Histrionic personality disorder affects under 2% of people, so most Twos do not have it.

### Can the Enneagram diagnose a personality disorder?

No. The Enneagram describes motivations and fears. A personality disorder is a rigid, long-running pattern that causes distress or impairment, and it is diagnosed by a qualified clinician from a person's history, usually with a structured interview. An Enneagram result cannot screen for one.
