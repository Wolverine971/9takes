<p class="firstLetter">Brooke Monk was sixteen the first time she was allowed to own a phone. On September 4, 2019, she used it to post a TikTok about how to decorate a phone case.</p>

Two months later she delivered the news to her parents on her way to bed. "After two months, I was like, 'Hey, I hit a million followers, goodnight,'" she told People in 2025. Her parents said, "Huh?"

What grew on that account was comedy about high school. Hallways, crushes, group chats, the specific dread of being called on in class. Brooke had never been in a class. She was homeschooled from the start and graduated in May 2020. Forbes put it flatly in 2026: "She became famous making videos about a world she'd never lived in."

Today she sits at No. 38 on the Forbes 2026 Top Creators list, up from No. 43 the year before, with roughly 47 million followers on TikTok as of mid-2026. The videos still look like a girl goofing around in her bedroom before lunch.

That look took six years to build.

## What is Brooke Monk's personality type?

### Brooke Monk is an Enneagram Type 3

She said it herself before anyone typed her. "When you're a content creator, it's like you are the product," she told Elite Daily in 2023.

She offered it the way a restaurant owner tells you the kitchen is the business, a flat description of how the thing runs. Then, in that same 2023 Elite Daily interview, she finished the thought and it stopped being flat: "If you're unhappy with it, your brain turns, 'This product sucks, I hate it,' into 'You suck. I hate you.'"

There is the whole wiring in one sentence. A number about a video arrives, and somewhere between her eye and her stomach it converts into a verdict on the person. Take away the output and the question of what remains has no obvious answer, which is why the output never stops.

The plainest version came later in that same Elite Daily conversation. "Sometimes I feel like I'm just a little machine that's pumping out content for people."

The behavior is documented and dated. She posts by 1 p.m. daily and named the only two things that override it on the Forbes Top Creators Show in 2026: "if I'm not posting by 1:00, then I'm either traveling or … I'm super sick. That's the only reason I wouldn't post at 1:00 p.m."

She also films until the video is right. "I will film as many takes as needed to make the video perfect. I'm neurotic about it," she told Elite Daily in 2023. "If it's not perfect, I have to do it again and again." And on that same 2026 Forbes show she told newer creators to find whatever the analytics favor and, in her word, "lean into that."

So the perfectionism has a direction. It tracks whatever the numbers reward, and it moves when they move.

Two other types have a case here. Her openness about anxiety and her fixed daily ritual can look like Type 6, and her 2023 Elite Daily line, "I hate, hate people disliking me," can look like a Helper's hunger for approval.

The tiebreaker is what she does with a bad comment. On the 2026 Forbes show she described people telling her a bit was annoying, then added: "it's also what kept you watching the video." She left the annoying part in. The metric got the final vote.

Type 3 is the house type of this business, and plenty of creators share the wiring. What is unusual about Brooke is that she will narrate it to you, on camera, while it is running, and she will tell you where it started.

## Why Brooke Monk's childhood made the internet inevitable

Six kids. One house. School at the kitchen table.

"When you have a bunch of sisters and you're home all the time, you kind of fight for your own identity," she told Elite Daily in 2023. In the same interview she gave the reason she started posting at all: "I was [homeschooled], so I didn't have anyone to talk to."

Those two sentences describe one childhood with a very specific problem in it, and the problem was arithmetic rather than cruelty. There was no room in that house where being Brooke rather than a Monk girl registered as a separate fact.

There was also a rule about phones, and it held until she was sixteen. Her parents were strict, and the strictness had a shape. In a 2021 YouTube video she repeated the specific instruction she grew up with: her parents "told me to not show my face on the internet." She has said she dreaded telling them about the account, her father specifically. When she finally did, they had no frame for it. "I don't even think my parents have a TikTok, to be honest."

So the first audience she ever had was one her parents could not see, could not join, and could not grade.

She has described her motive at the time with no polish on it at all: "I was like, maybe I could relate to some people and make some friends online." That is a homeschooled sixteen-year-old looking for a room. What she found was the largest room in human history, and it ran on exactly the skill her house had trained: be distinguishable, quickly, or get passed over.

The high-school sketches make more sense in that light. She was doing the thing kids do outside a party they can hear through the window, which is imagine it in enough detail to perform it. Millions of people who actually went to high school watched a girl who never did and felt seen.

## The white wall behind Brooke Monk is empty on purpose

Building a high school she had never attended took work. So does looking like nothing was built.

Everything about her videos reads accidental. Bedroom, no crew, natural light, a girl in the middle of getting ready.

Here is how she describes the room, unprompted, on the Forbes Top Creators Show: "my background in my house is like so white. I keep the space very clean, so there's not a lot for people to think about outside of, you know, what I'm creating in the text above my head."

Nothing on the wall. Nothing on the dresser. Nothing that gives a scrolling thumb any reason to look anywhere except her face.

She then explains what the emptiness buys. Familiarity. "I would argue that even on a small scale, there is certain things about my videos that make people realize, 'Oh, that's Brooke. Oh, I've seen that before.'" The room got recognizable enough to work as a signature: when another creator films in her house, her comment sections fill with people who know that wall on sight. In a 15-second video, that flicker of recognition buys a second of extra watch time, and a second is the whole game.

There is a wrinkle in how she says it. "It's become, even accidentally, my background in my house is like so white." The blankness was deliberate. Its promotion to trademark was an accident she caught. She stripped the room to kill distraction, noticed years later that the absence had become the most recognizable thing about her, and locked it there permanently. Half of what looks engineered here began as luck she refused to waste.

The morning runs on the same logic. She looks for ideas while doing her makeup, which she admits makes the makeup take longer. And she is not scanning alone. "Sam will look for ideas same time," she says, "and literally by I want to say 10:30 or 11:00 we have a list ready to go of like what we think would be fun to film."

Sam is Sam Dezz, her partner since 2020. The relationship is almost exactly as old as the account. In her telling of the day he shows up the way a colleague shows up: on the morning shift, hunting concepts, hitting the 10:30 handoff. The relationship has outlasted most of the platform, and none of this is a knock on it. Bringing the people she loves inside the operation is what this type does, because the operation is where she lives.

Her manager, Devain Doolaramani, who signed her when she had just turned seventeen and has worked with her for five years, told the Founder's Story podcast in 2025 that she has "such a genius mind." He is talking about the analytics instinct. She also runs an active LinkedIn account, where she posts about the creator economy and the business behind the videos, which is a strange thing for a beauty-adjacent TikToker to do and a very logical thing for someone who wants a second arena to win in.

None of this is hidden. She will tell you the room is engineered, the schedule is engineered, the recognition is engineered. She has never once claimed the effortlessness. She just built a process that manufactures it before lunch, every day, for six years.

## Brooke Monk used the same word in 2023 and in 2026

She has explained every piece of the machine on the record. One thing she has never explained is a word she keeps reaching for.

It showed up in her childhood story. Here it is again three years later, different interviewer, unrelated subject.

> "When you have a bunch of sisters and you're home all the time, you kind of fight for your own identity."
>
> — Brooke Monk, Elite Daily, 2023. The subject is her childhood.

> "people are being funneled so many videos all the time that you really have to fight to be remembered."
>
> — Brooke Monk, Forbes Top Creators Show, 2026. The subject is the TikTok algorithm.

Same verb. Same stakes. The opponent got bigger and the prize stayed identical.

A skeptic gets one objection here and it is a fair one. "Fight for your identity" and "fight to be remembered" are ordinary idioms. You can turn either one up in a hundred interviews with a hundred people who share nothing else. Granted.

The tell sits underneath the word. Two unrelated questions, one about her family and one about a recommendation engine, pulled the same threat model out of her. She never described her childhood as lonely, or the algorithm as unfair, or either one as hard. Both times she described a crowded room and the live risk of not registering in it.

Nobody handed her that parallel and she has never drawn it in public. It surfaced twice because it is the sentence underneath her, and sentences like that leak.

The distance between those two rooms is the whole career. One had five siblings in it. The other has a recommendation engine and about a billion daily uploads. Same play in both: make something distinct enough that the room cannot scroll past you. She got very good at it. Forbes rank, forty-seven million followers, a company. The play just found a bigger house.

Which raises the question she has not answered, and possibly cannot. What happens to a person whose only known way of existing is to be the most noticeable thing in the room, on the day the room stops looking?

## Why Brooke Monk says she hates being disliked

A version of that verdict already exists in print.

In May 2026, after Brooke appeared at the Oxford Union, a student writer named Lucy Pollock used her as an example in The Oxford Blue. The Union, Pollock wrote, cares about sensationalism: "It's why they invite speakers with nothing to say, but high follower counts, like Brooke Monk."

The critique lands on something real. Her catalog is enormous and enormously consistent. Six years of daily short-form comedy builds a body of retained attention, which is not the same asset as a body of ideas, and Pollock is right that a debating society built for argument is a strange place to hand the microphone to someone whose craft is watch time.

But the short-form catalog is not the whole record, and Pollock's line only works if it is.

There is a second account carrying 11.5 million subscribers as of July 2026, and she runs that one on inverted rules. "With YouTube, I'm not as harsh about the perfectionist side of it," she told the Forbes Top Creators Show in 2026. Then she gives her reason, and it has nothing to do with reach. "I love long-form YouTube cuz it feels like people are there cuz they're interested in, you know, me or my personality rather than, you know, my content just being entertaining, which you know, I value both, but it's special when, you know, they're there for you."

Sit with the last four words. A woman with 47 million followers on one platform calls 11 million on another one special, because those are the people she believes came for her rather than for the product.

That is a Three saying the quiet part at normal volume. The achievement never quite answers the question. The only thing that would is being wanted with the machine switched off.

Then read what she told Elite Daily in 2023 about the job itself. "I don't think any human was meant to see millions of opinions about them every day." And: "I hate, hate people disliking me, which I know is a terrible mindset when you're on the internet." And: "I don't want to feed into something that's hurting me."

That is a person describing a wound she walks into every morning at one o'clock.

<p class="inner-thought">Someone typed that you're annoying. Do not feel it. Sort it. If they typed it, they watched to the end. If they watched to the end, the video worked. File it.</p>

That is the trick, and she explained it out loud to Forbes in 2026 without seeming to notice what it costs. A cruel comment gets reclassified as evidence of retention. "everything is kind of like a data point of what keeps people watching videos."

It is the only survivable arrangement available to her. You cannot hate being disliked and also stand in front of forty-seven million people every day, unless you build a machine that converts the hate into a number before it reaches you. She built the machine at sixteen and has run it ever since.

That does not make the output better than Pollock says it is. It explains why the output looks like that. She has already named the reason in the words she actually uses: "you really have to fight to be remembered." ## How Brooke Monk built Doting Beauty without outside money

There is an expiration date under her whole catalog, and she has reached it. She got famous between sixteen and nineteen playing high schoolers. She is twenty-three. The bit that built the account describes a life she has aged out of twice over, once by never living it and once by getting older than it.

Her audience aged with her, which is what makes this hard. The kids who found her in 2019 are in college or working now, and hallway comedy plays to them as nostalgia instead of recognition. The alternative is making it for the next fourteen-year-olds, which means a twenty-three-year-old performing a school she never attended for an audience that gets younger than her every year. Neither road runs another six years.

On January 23, 2026, she launched a lash brand called Doting Beauty. Four styles, reusable, and by her manager's account it took two years to get there.

The development story is unglamorous. On that same podcast, Doolaramani described the process as going from a Pinterest board to a full brand, working through factory samples, testing, adjusting, testing again. No outside funding. She paid for it. And on the question of whose company it is, he is unambiguous: "It's her brand. And it's her vision."

For a creator with her reach, the fast version of this was always available. License the name, take the check, post the link. She spent two years on lashes instead, which reads either as patience or as a hedge against the day the bedroom bit stops working.

She already talks about the operation like a company. On brands who sit on an approval for two weeks and then demand a 48-hour turnaround, she told Forbes in 2026: "I got a whole schedule, guys."

Nothing has stopped yet. She moved up five spots on the Forbes list this year. But when Elite Daily asked her in 2023 where she was headed, she did not name a platform, a revenue line, or a follower count. "I just want to be myself, and as I get older, I'm figuring out more of who that is."

It is the closest thing in the public record to a want the analytics cannot score. It is also a twenty-year-old admitting she does not yet know who she is with the camera off.

## What the internet takes from Brooke Monk

The internet had already answered that question for her. She read the answer out loud two years before that interview.

On October 18, 2021, eighteen years old, she opened a laptop on camera and searched her own name on Reddit for a YouTube video titled "Reacting to Reddit Fantasies About Me." She went in expecting style notes. "Maybe they give me a little fashion advice or something," she says at the top, "because it's always on the videos where I'm wearing like a cute outfit."

It was a subreddit of 28,000 members in October 2021, and it was not about outfits. She reads the comments out loud as she scrolls, and they get worse the longer she goes, ending somewhere that cannot be reprinted here. You can watch her face change in real time. Partway through she stops narrating and says it straight: "I am a stranger on the internet that you are fetishizing without my consent."

Then, still scrolling, she says something else. "I'm beginning to think this is the reason why my parents told me to not show my face on the internet."

That is the rule she grew up under and never had explained. She found out what it was for at eighteen, live, with the camera running.

And at the end of the video, still visibly shaken, she does the only thing she has ever learned to do with an experience. She pitches the sequel. "If you guys like this video as much as I hated that experience, we will go through the Reddit page with my boyfriend."

The violation is real, the distress is real, and inside the same minute it has been converted into a content plan gated on engagement. Cynicism has nothing to do with it. There is no other slot in the machine for it to go into.

Years later, a sexually explicit deepfake of her went viral. No outlet that covered it, The Mirror included, fixes a date, so when it happened is not knowable. What is knowable is that she had no part in making it and no way to unmake it, and that her answer took the same shape: a video, because a video is the tool she has. She screen-recorded the comment section and posted it to her backup account, lip-syncing to [Taylor Swift](/personality-analysis/taylor-swift)'s "The Man," Swift's four-minute list of things she would not have to explain if she were a man.

A deepfake accuses her of nothing and puts no words in her mouth. It takes the face, the white wall, the lashes, every signal the internet was trained over six years to recognize on sight as Brooke, and runs all of it without her inside.

She built that recognition on purpose. She stripped the room so nothing would compete with her face. She held a 1 p.m. deadline for years so the face would show up in the same place every day. She made herself into the most identifiable version of a girl in a bedroom that has ever existed, because a homeschooled kid in a house of six learned early that being scrolled past is the thing to fear.

Then someone fed the whole signature into a machine and got a girl who was not her.

The 1 p.m. rule has two exceptions, and she has named them both. Traveling. Seriously sick.

Neither one covers this.
