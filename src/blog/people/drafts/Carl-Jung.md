> "As a child I felt myself to be alone, and I am still, because I know things and must hint at things which others apparently know nothing of, and for the most part do not want to know." — Carl Jung, in his posthumous 1962 memoir Memories, Dreams, Reflections

<p class="firstLetter">In March 1959, the BBC sent John Freeman to a house on the shore of Lake Zurich to film a conversation with an 83-year-old man who had put words in all of our mouths. Introvert. Extravert. Complex. Persona. Archetype. Near the end of the interview, Freeman asked Carl Jung the question Jung's readers had spent four decades applying to everyone else. Had he concluded what psychological type he was himself?</p>

"Naturally I have devoted a great deal of attention to that painful question," Jung said, in the film the BBC broadcast that October.

Painful. He wrote the 1921 book the Myers-Briggs empire was later built on. He made the vocabulary common currency. And when the question finally pointed at him, he answered, on the 1959 BBC film, like a witness under caution: "The type is nothing static. It changes in the course of life." Then, carefully: he "most certainly was characterized by thinking," he "had a great deal of intuition too," and he "had a definite difficulty with feeling." His relation to reality, he added, "was not particularly brilliant."

Nearly seventy years on, the people who inherited that vocabulary still cannot close his file. The largest personality-voting site on the internet types him INFJ and Enneagram 5w4. Don Riso and Russ Hudson, authors of the most influential Enneagram books in print, typed him 9w1. The Myers-Briggs community splits him three ways. The father of personality typing is the type system's unsolved case.

This is the story of why. It runs through a pencil case in a Swiss attic, a thirteen-hour conversation in Vienna, and a red leather book that spent twenty-five years locked in a bank vault.

## What is Carl Jung's personality type?

### Carl Jung is an Enneagram Type 5

The 9takes verdict is Type 5 with a 4 wing (a wing is the neighboring type that colors the core), argued rather than assumed. Jung never heard the question: the personality Enneagram took shape through Oscar Ichazo and Claudio Naranjo after his death in 1961, so every typing of him is retrospective. And the retrospective verdicts disagree at the top.

Riso and Hudson put him at Nine with a One wing, alongside Lincoln and Walt Disney, reading his lifelong reconciling of opposites as a peacemaker's project. The internet's largest typing communities vote 5w4. Personality Junkie's A.J. Drenth, who has argued the question at more length than anyone, weighed 4, 9, and 5 and landed on 5w4. Each camp is looking at real evidence.

Here is the case for [Enneagram Type 5](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-5), and it rests on what Jung built when no one was watching. At ten, he carved a secret figure and hid it in a forbidden attic. At thirty-eight, mid-crisis, he poured his inner life into notebooks he showed almost no one. At forty-eight, world-famous, he began raising a stone tower for himself at the far end of the lake. The pattern held for eighty years: when life pressed hardest, he made a smaller, more sealed world, and studied his way back out of it.

The second pillar is how he treated knowledge itself. "The word belief is a difficult thing for me," he told Freeman in the 1959 BBC interview. "I must have a reason for a certain hypothesis. Either I know a thing, and when I know it, I don't need to believe it." That sentence is a working definition of the Five's relationship to safety: what you know is yours; what you merely believe can be taken.

And the third pillar is his own testimony, weighed for what it is. The self-portrait he gave the BBC in 1959 is self-report at 83, delivered in the vocabulary of his own system, and a Jungian function stack is not an Enneagram center; plenty of Nines lead with thinking. What the testimony does show is the felt goal. He named feeling as his weak point, treated knowing as his one solid ground, and then allowed, wryly, that Freeman now had "all the necessary data for the diagnosis." Riso and Hudson read his lifelong reconciling of opposites as a Nine keeping the peace, and their best fact is concrete: six years of deference to Freud before the break. When Jung himself explained the types book, in the same 1959 BBC interview, he said he wrote it "to find my own bearings," a sentence a Nine advocate can fairly hear as a search for equilibrium. This page reads it as the move he made all his life: when the ground shook, he went looking for something to know. The full case against this verdict, and it deserves its day, is in the Rabbit Hole below. The case for it begins with a boy and a pencil case.

## Carl Jung's childhood: the manikin in the pencil case

Kesswil, 1875. His father was a rural pastor whose faith was quietly failing and whose money already had. Jung wanted to be an archaeologist; he told Freeman flatly, "we had no money at all," so medicine it was. For his first nine years he had no siblings. "I was used to be alone," he said, "but I missed it, I missed company."

Company inside the house was complicated. When Jung was three, his mother spent several months in a Basel hospital, an absence he connected to trouble in the marriage. "From then on," he wrote in Memories, Dreams, Reflections, "I always felt mistrustful when the word 'love' was spoken." The portrait of her in the same memoir is itself split in two: "By day she was a loving mother, but at night she seemed uncanny... Archaic and ruthless; ruthless as truth and nature." On camera, at 83, he put it more simply: in the night, he had fear of his mother. Freeman asked why. "I have not the slightest idea."

Jung's answer to a house he could not predict was rooms no one else could enter. At about ten, he carved a two-inch manikin from the end of a school ruler, gave it a frock coat, a top hat, an ink-black finish and a little bed inside his pencil case, and hid it on a beam in the forbidden attic beside a painted stone from the Rhine. He visited it in secret and left it tiny scrolls written in a language he invented. He told no one for twenty-five years. "This possession of a secret had a very powerful formative influence on my character," he wrote. "I consider it the essential factor of my boyhood."

He was never a fragile boy. Set upon once by about seven boys, he told Freeman, he grabbed one by the legs, swung him as a weapon, and beat down four; the rest scared off, and afterward he avoided fights because he did not trust himself. At twelve came the episode that could have ended everything. A shove from another boy knocked him down, and afterward fainting spells arrived whenever schoolwork did. The spells went on for months, until he overheard his father tell a visitor: "I have lost what little I had, and what will become of the boy if he cannot earn his own living?" Jung wrote: "I was thunderstruck. This was the collision with reality. 'Why, then, I must get to work!'" He took out his Latin grammar and fainted. He kept reading and fainted again. And again. "'Devil take it, I'm not going to faint,' I told myself, and persisted in my purpose." The spells stopped. "That was when I learned what a neurosis is." The diagnosis he pinned on himself was harsher than any doctor's: "I saw clearly that I myself had arranged this whole disgraceful situation."

After the cure, he rose at five to study, sometimes at three. Around the same age, walking to school one morning, the fog inside him lifted: "Suddenly for a single moment I had the overwhelming impression of having just emerged from a dense cloud. I knew all at once: now I am myself!... Previously I had existed, too, but everything had merely happened to me. Now I happened to myself."

## Thirteen hours with Freud, then the letter that ended everything

Vienna, March 1907. "We met at one o'clock in the afternoon and talked virtually without a pause for thirteen hours," Jung wrote. "Freud was the first man of real importance I had encountered." Jung was 31, four years married to Emma Rauschenbach, the first two of their five children at home, a rising psychiatrist whose word-association experiments had given Freud's ideas their first laboratory legs. Freud was 50 and had found his heir. "If I am Moses," Freud wrote him in January 1909, "then you are Joshua and will take possession of the promised land of psychiatry, which I shall only be able to glimpse from afar."

That April, after a strange evening in Vienna, Freud wrote the line that named what he wanted: "It is strange that on the very same evening when I formally adopted you as eldest son and anointed you... as my successor and crown prince, you should have divested me of my paternal dignity."

An anointed son is expected to inherit, and inheritance requires agreement. Jung described the mismatch to Freeman with the mildness of sixty years' distance: "I soon discovered that when he had thought something, then it was settled, while I was doubting all along the line." In 1912 Jung published the book his doubt had been writing, a rework of the libido theory that removed sex from the center. On film in 1957 he gave that book its epitaph: "that cost me my friendship with Freud, because he couldn't accept it."

The letters turned raw. Emma had seen it coming: in the autumn of 1911 she wrote to Freud herself, privately, asking him not to think of Carl "with a father's feeling: 'He will grow, but I must dwindle.'" Jung, December 18, 1912: "You go around sniffing out all the symptomatic actions in your vicinity... Meanwhile you remain on top as the father, sitting pretty." Freud, January 3, 1913: "I propose that we abandon our personal relations entirely. I shall lose nothing by it, for my own emotional tie with you has long been a thin thread." Within eighteen months Jung had resigned the movement's journal and its presidency. "After the parting of the ways with Freud, a period of inner uncertainty began for me," he wrote. "It would be no exaggeration to call it a state of disorientation. I felt totally suspended in mid-air."

Eight years of that suspension produced Psychological Types. Asked by Freeman in 1959 what clinical experience prompted it, Jung corrected the premise: "It was a very personal reason, namely to do justice to the culture of Freud, also to that of Adler, and to find my own bearings." The book that seeded every four-letter test and every type fight on the internet is a rupture document. He built typology so that losing Freud could be a fact about structure instead of a fact about betrayal: two honest men, two attitude types, one inevitable collision.

## Jung No. 1 and Jung No. 2

Long before he typed anyone else, Jung had already split himself in two. "Somewhere deep in the background I always knew that I was two persons," he wrote of his school years. One was the pastor's son who went to class and did sums. The other was "old, in fact... skeptical, mistrustful, remote from the world of men, but close to nature, the earth, the sun, the moon, the weather, all living creatures, and above all close to the night, to dreams." He named them No. 1 and No. 2, and insisted the pairing was ordinary: "the play and counterplay between personalities No. 1 and No. 2, which has run through my whole life, has nothing to do with a 'split' or dissociation in the ordinary medical sense."

His whole life is legible as that ledger.

Most lives pick a side. Jung's method was to make each half study the other. No. 1 kept No. 2 from becoming a mystic adrift; No. 2 kept No. 1 from becoming a stopwatch. The tower was where the treaty was signed. "I have done without electricity, and tend the fireplace and stove myself," he wrote. "These simple acts make man simple; and how difficult it is to be simple!"

The two halves shook hands in public exactly once. Freeman asked whether he now believed in God. The old man paused. "Difficult to answer. I know. I don't need to believe. I know." The sentence made him world-famous one last time, and it is No. 2's content delivered in No. 1's grammar: even God had to arrive as knowledge. Three months after the broadcast he wrote to The Listener to correct the record, worried his most quoted moment had been misfiled: "I did not say in the broadcast, 'There is a God.'" He was 84, and still could not leave a sentence unguarded.

### Carl Jung's Wing: 5w4

The 4 wing shows in what his privacy produced. A 5w6 hoards knowledge toward systems and institutions; Jung walked out of the two institutions that claimed him (the church of his father, the psychoanalytic movement of his adopted father) and instead produced the Red Book: calligraphy, mandalas, illuminated paintings, a medieval art object made in secret. The 4 wing is the aesthetic, mythic, personal-meaning current running under the scholarship, the need for his knowledge to be his in style as well as substance.

### Carl Jung's Instinctual Subtype: sp/sx

Self-preservation first: Bollingen is the purest sp-Five artifact in this corpus, a hand-built stone shell with the comforts deliberately removed. Sexual second: his defining relationships were single all-consuming dyads, a thirteen-hour first conversation with Freud, the decades-long bond with his collaborator Toni Wolff, and he did his deepest work inside them. Social last: he accepted institutional roles, a journal, a presidency, and walked out of each the moment it crossed the inner work; the group as such never held him.

### Stress and Growth Arrows

Under stress a Five scatters toward Seven's frantic multiplicity, and 1913 to 1918 carries that signature. The visions arrived unbidden; the Seven-shaped part was the response, a man who privately feared for his sanity filling notebook after notebook because stopping felt more dangerous than continuing. In security a Five moves toward Eight's embodied command, and late Jung is full of it: the stone carving, the axe and the well, the blunt "I know" delivered to a television camera without a flicker of apology. The boy who once beat down four of seven attackers and scared off the rest had that Eight line available all along; health let him use it without fear.

### Counterarguments: Why Carl Jung Might Not Be a Type 5

The Riso-Hudson 9w1 case is serious. Jung's signature intellectual move was reconciliation: opposites held until a third thing emerges. He deferred to Freud for six years, anger surfacing only in eruptions, and his descriptions of Bollingen (merging with nature, simple acts, deep quiet) read like a Nine's homecoming. A 9w1 reading would cover the lifelong patience and the late serenity too. What it explains less well is the accounting he left: he described feeling as his deficit rather than his medium, treated knowing as the one ground he never doubted, and answered life's blows by sealing rooms and studying, which is a head type's defense. Drenth's field has a third chair, Type 4, and the epigraph's loneliness has a Four's ring to it. But he named feeling, the center a Four lives from, as his lifelong deficit; his crisis response was to record and understand rather than to be seen in the suffering; and when the most beautiful object he ever made was finished, he locked it in a cupboard. If new first-person evidence surfaced showing harmony, rather than knowledge, as the felt goal, the verdict here would have to move.

## Why the Red Book stayed locked in a bank vault until 2009

Start with what the flood was like from inside it. In October 1913, twice, the vision of Europe drowning arrived unbidden, and his first hypothesis was the clinician's: "I drew the conclusion that they had to do with me myself, and decided that I was menaced by a psychosis. The idea of war did not occur to me at all." War arrived the following August. The visions did not stop.

What he did next is the thing he was mocked for, for the rest of his life and after it. A practicing psychiatrist sat down, night after night for some sixteen years, and held written conversations with the figures in his own visions, then painted them: 205 folio pages of calligraphy and images in a red leather book. The Freudians took it as confirmation that the crown prince had left science for the séance room, and Freud had already filed his verdict in that final letter: a thin thread, long frayed.

Watch the behavior instead of the reputation. This was a man whose single trusted instrument, since the pencil case in the attic, was knowing. Now the unknowable was arriving from inside his own skull, and there was no Burghölzli stopwatch for it. So he did the only thing his machinery allowed: he treated the flood as data.

<p class="inner-thought">If I cannot stop this, I can record it. And if I can record it, I have not yet gone under.</p>

"The years when I was pursuing my inner images were the most important in my life," he wrote later. "In them everything essential was decided."

He also understood, with complete clarity, what the book would look like to everyone else. So the man who published twenty volumes locked this one in a cupboard at Küsnacht. His heirs kept it sealed after his death; when the Jungian analyst Stephen Martin asked Jung's son Franz about it, the otherwise genial man, by a 2009 New York Times account, "reacted sharply, nearly with anger." In 1984 the family moved it to a Zurich bank vault. It stayed there until October 2009, when it finally appeared in facsimile, sold out before publication, and reached number four on Amazon's overall list at 195 dollars a copy, as reported by the Jung Page. Its publisher, W.W. Norton, billed it as "the most influential unpublished work in the history of psychology."

He was right about the risk, and right about the value, and he managed both the same way he managed everything: alone, in writing, behind a lock.

## The shadow of the man who mapped the shadow

Jung gave the culture its word for the parts of ourselves we refuse to look at. The record shows he had his own.

The documented case in his private life is Toni Wolff. Deirdre Bair's biography traces the arc: Wolff arrived as his patient around 1910, became his collaborator, and then, through the crisis years and for decades after, a third presence inside the marriage, an arrangement Emma Jung ultimately accommodated. The historian Sonu Shamdasani states Emma's side of the ledger plainly: "Without Emma Jung, his work would not have been possible, and not just in terms of maintaining the household, raising the children, and so forth, but as a co-participant in his work." Emma analyzed patients from 1930, wrote her own psychology of the Grail legend, and died in 1955 with it unfinished. The Sabina Spielrein question, his Burghölzli patient who became a psychoanalyst and whose diaries surfaced in 1977, remains genuinely contested; Bair goes only as far as the word "intimate" and declines a verdict, which is exactly as far as the evidence goes.

A fair skeptic gets the next sentence for free: the man who told the world to integrate its shadow arranged his household so that his own was disclosed only on his terms. Nothing in the pages above excuses that, and the empathy his framework extends to everyone else is owed to the two women who subsidized it.

What the type lens adds is a mechanism, and only that. His entire safety system, from boyhood on, ran on controlled disclosure: decide what is known about you, and by whom. Confession hands someone else the file. He wrote the century's most famous argument for [facing your hidden material](/enneagram-corner/shadow-work-by-enneagram-type), and kept his in the same place he kept the Red Book.

The harder page of the record is public. In 1933 he accepted the presidency of the General Medical Society for Psychotherapy as it fell under Nazi-era coordination, and his 1934 essay "The State of Psychotherapy Today," published in its journal, contrasted Jewish and "Aryan" psychology; the Swiss psychiatrist Gustav Bally attacked the arrangement in the Neue Zürcher Zeitung that February, and the antisemitism charge has never left the essay. His defenders answer with the other column: he rewrote the society's statutes so that excluded German-Jewish doctors could keep individual membership, and he resigned in 1939. By Gershom Scholem's account, when Rabbi Leo Baeck confronted him in 1946, Jung's answer was three words: "I slipped up."

## Why every personality test you take is still arguing with Carl Jung

His secrets stayed home. His words did not. Order a coffee anywhere in the English-speaking world and you can hear a dead Swiss psychiatrist's clinical vocabulary used for small talk. "The world in general, particularly America, is extroverted like hell," he said in the 1957 Houston interviews. "The introvert has no place."

The typology was the least of what he claimed. Analytical psychology's larger architecture holds that beneath your private unconscious runs a collective one, stocked with recurring figures he called archetypes, and that a life's real work is what he named individuation: the slow integration of the parts you own and the parts you disown into one self. Individuation is his word for the process this profile has been tracing, No. 1 and No. 2 negotiating their treaty. It is also the closest cousin of what the Enneagram means by [growth](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-personal-growth): the work of becoming the whole of the type you already are.

Katharine Briggs and Isabel Myers built their indicator on Psychological Types, and every four-letter handle on a dating profile descends from it. The heirs still cannot settle his own four letters: the big voting sites file him INFJ, while the function stack he gave Freeman, thinking first and feeling last, reads closer to INTP or INTJ. The Enneagram's line runs elsewhere, through [Ichazo and Naranjo](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-influences), and its practitioners adopted Jung retroactively as their hardest case.

His least scientific idea became the decade's therapy trend: the #shadowwork hashtag had passed 2.3 billion views on TikTok by 2023, per trade coverage, while [Jordan Peterson](/personality-analysis/Jordan-Peterson) has spent a decade filling arenas with Jung's maps.

The Freeman broadcast had a sequel of its own. By Freeman's account, the flood of mail it brought, together with a dream of addressing a listening crowd, persuaded Jung to write Man and His Symbols, his one book for the general public, his own chapter finished roughly ten days before his final illness began. The excavation is still running too: the private Black Books reached print in 2020, Emma's writings in 2024, nearly seven decades after her death.

Which brings the question back around, one last time, to the only person who ever answered it from inside. In Houston in 1957, Richard Evans raised the popular idea that the world divides into introverts and extraverts. Jung's reply, preserved in C.G. Jung Speaking (1977), should hang over every typing thread on the internet, including this one. "You know how people are. They catch a word, and then everything is schematized. There is no such thing as a pure extravert or a pure introvert. Such a man would be in the lunatic asylum." His own system, he said, "is merely a sort of orientation," an instrument for the working psychologist and nothing grander. Then the verdict: "The classification of individuals means nothing, nothing at all."

Sixty-nine years later, we filed him anyway.
