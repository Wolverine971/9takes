"Like, who elected you and Sam Altman?" Anderson Cooper asked Dario Amodei on 60 Minutes in November 2025. Amodei had just said he was "deeply uncomfortable with these decisions being made by a few companies, by a few people."

His answer: "No one, no one. Honestly, no one."

That, he added, is one reason he has always advocated regulating the technology. ([60 Minutes transcript, November 16, 2025](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-warning-of-ai-potential-dangers-60-minutes-transcript/))

Amodei co-founded Anthropic, the company behind the Claude models, and runs it as chief executive. He will not stop building what he warns about, and his September 2026 essay on pacing AI says why:

> "Not building the technology deprives humanity of benefits or simply places AI in the hands of authoritarian powers, while building it too fast is reckless."

Anthropic's answer, the essay continues, is "a race to the top" that makes "safety something on which AI companies compete." ([darioamodei.com, September 2026](https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier)) The same essay committed Anthropic to host embedded outside evaluators with "Desks in our offices, access badges, and company laptops," free to publish findings "without editorial control by Anthropic." The next month, Anthropic filed publicly to sell shares on Nasdaq, with a voting structure its own prospectus says has "no direct public company precedent."

Those moves follow one pattern, and it runs through his whole career.

## What is Dario Amodei's personality type?

Dario Amodei is an Enneagram Type 6, the Loyalist. When danger rises, he moves toward people he has tested and proposes rules that would bind everyone, his own company included. When trust breaks, he leaves, and he takes his people with him.

[Type 6](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6) sits in the head triad, and the core emotion of the head triad is fear. That means fear as opposed to anger, which drives the gut types, or shame, which drives the heart types. Amodei doesn't move through the world on instinct or on image. He thinks his way through it, and what he thinks about most is what could go wrong.

What makes him a Six rather than another head type is what he does with the fear. A Six works it in four plain steps:

1. Scan for the threat before anyone else sees it.
2. Find something solid to stand on: people who have proven themselves, and rules that apply to everyone.
3. Test the people in charge, and say the disagreement out loud.
4. When trust in them breaks, leave, and bring the trusted group along.

The scanning started early. Richard Shapiro, his physics teacher at San Francisco's Lowell High School, told The New York Times that Amodei often thought he had underperformed on tests. Shapiro also remembered: "I'd yell at him, 'Dario, lighten up!'" ([The New York Times, via GV Wire, November 2026](https://gvwire.com/?p=278055))

Test nerves are common in teenagers, though, and they don't make anyone a type. Costly choices weigh more, and two of his came before he was famous. At OpenAI, by Kevin Roose's account, he kept a record of times he felt Sam Altman had misled him. At Anthropic's founding in 2021 he gave all seven co-founders equal equity, a decision he says drew "even more negativity" than the founding team's size ([Cheeky Pint, August 6, 2025](https://cheekypint.transistor.fm/5/transcript)).

He built his job the same way. As of June 2026 his only direct report was his chief of staff, and the rest of the executive team reported to his sister, Daniela Amodei, Anthropic's president. TechCrunch quotes him calling the setup "incredibly freeing" ([TechCrunch, June 10, 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/10/anthropics-dario-amodei-has-just-one-direct-report/)).

Asked by Bloomberg's Emily Chang how he handles the pressure, he named a second arrangement he finds freeing: talking, uncensored, to the whole company every two weeks. It builds trust, he said, and leaves him feeling that 3,000 people are on the same page with him. He thinks that is an advantage because he never feels alone. ([Bloomberg Originals, June 10, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v1wZwxY3CMg&t=1918s))

A Five guards his time so he can stand alone. Amodei's time is guarded by his sister, and he counts never standing alone as a strength.

The case for Type 5 gets its full hearing in the Rabbit Hole below.

## A cure that came "a few years" late

Amodei was born in San Francisco in 1983 and grew up in the Mission District. His parents, Elena Engel, a Chicago-born library project manager, and Riccardo Amodei, a leather craftsman from "a tiny town near the island of Elba," had moved there from Italy in the 1970s. ([Wired, March 28, 2025](https://www.wired.com/story/anthropic-benevolent-artificial-intelligence/))

His father had been sick for years and died in 2006, around the time Dario started graduate school at Princeton wanting to be a theoretical physicist. The loss pulled him toward biology, and later toward AI. "Maybe three or four years after he died," Amodei said, the cure rate for his father's disease rose from 50% to roughly 95%. ([Big Technology Podcast, July 30, 2025](https://pod.wave.co/podcast/big-technology-podcast/anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-ais-potential-openai-rivalry-genai-business-doomerism-451c32cc))

Jade Wang, who dated him in the early 2010s, told Big Technology what that timing meant ([Big Technology, July 29, 2025](https://www.bigtechnology.com/p/the-making-of-dario-amodei)):

> "It's the difference between his father most likely dying and most likely living, okay?"

In August 2026, [an investor](https://x.com/GavinSBaker/status/2088611616577253502) urged him on X to sound more positive before Anthropic went public. Amodei answered by naming the disease: "I lost my father to Hepatitis C only a few years before the development of direct-acting antivirals (sofosbuvir), which cure 95% of patients and probably would have cured him." ([X, August 15, 2026](https://x.com/DarioAmodei/status/2088758819304443967)) Sofosbuvir won U.S. approval in December 2013, about seven years after his death ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sofosbuvir)). A month later, the pacing essay disclosed that he had "survived an early-stage cancer that would not have been treatable even fifty years ago."

Both disclosures arrived as evidence inside arguments, not as confessions, and this analysis reads nothing further into them.

The lesson he draws from his father's death is that delay has a body count. That is his personal reason to keep building. The race to the top is his strategic one.

## "He would say it"

At Princeton, in Michael Berry's retina lab, he co-invented a better sensor for recording retinal signals, and his biophysics dissertation won the Hertz Thesis Prize. A lab colleague, Olivier Marre, told AFP: "I never saw someone engaged in a strong moral or ethical quest." ([AFP via France 24, October 2, 2026](https://www.france24.com/en/live-news/20261002-anthropic-s-dario-amodei-ai-s-most-visible-ceo-and-enigma))

After a Stanford postdoc he joined Baidu's AI lab, where an early team member, Gregory Diamos, had encouraged the team to hire him. Diamos has described the same habit twice. To AFP: "If he would disagree with ...the direction of the company, he would say it," until "the disagreement got to be so big that he felt like it was not worth continuing." To the Times: "Dario argued very aggressively at pretty much every opportunity."

That is the third step, testing the people in charge, done out loud.

At OpenAI, which he joined in 2016, he helped pioneer the human-feedback training used on GPT-2 and led GPT-3 with "50-60% of the entire company's compute." The man who warns about scaling did much of the scaling. GPT-2 "terrified him," Wired reports, and he urged a staged release:

> "I didn't know if this model was dangerous ... but my general feeling was that we should do something to signpost that."

Not everyone loved the style. In Alex Kantrowitz's account, some colleagues found his long memos inspirational, others "flag planting and heavy handed," and he "could be a bit abrasive, sometimes disparaging projects he didn't believe in."

## Staying on conditions

At OpenAI the sequence Diamos saw at Baidu took years. As safety issues grew more serious, he told Wired, "the company started moving in the opposite direction." He took his concerns to Altman, who, he says, would listen carefully and agree. Then nothing would change. (OpenAI declined to comment to Wired; its stance, the magazine noted, is that safety has been a constant.)

Kevin Roose, reporting for The Atlantic, fills in the rest. Amodei's "biggest concern was about Altman's character." He "recorded examples of times he felt Altman had misled him" and "had considered quitting before, but he'd always talked himself out of it." ([The Atlantic, September 29, 2026](https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/2026/09/openai-v-anthropic-inside-biggest-rivalry-tech/688819/))

What he kept a record of was not a technical disagreement. It was being misled.

The Times, citing people with knowledge of the company, adds an earlier test. After he and Daniela took over OpenAI's GPT project in 2018 and pushed back on co-founder Greg Brockman's attempt to help with it, Amodei "repeatedly told Altman that if he did not keep Brockman away from the work, he would leave." Altman refused, a person with knowledge of his thinking told the paper.

Roose also describes the endgame. Before he left, he offered Altman "a list of conditions under which he'd agree to stay," while "the group of seven formed a private Slack channel and called it BATNA," negotiators' shorthand for the best alternative to a deal.

When he finally said he was leaving, he told Wired, Altman made repeated offers for him to stay, and he realized he should have left sooner. He left at the end of 2020 with six other OpenAI employees, including Daniela. Within a few weeks, Wired reported, about a dozen more followed.

In a June 2026 interview, he told Chang that disagreement alone is not enough to leave, but not being able to trust someone makes staying very hard. ([Bloomberg Originals, June 17, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2VHFgyawPE&t=405s))

## People he trusts

Asked by [Lex Fridman](/personality-analysis/lex-fridman) in 2024 why he left OpenAI, he declined to judge anyone, then gave the advice he had followed himself ([Lex Fridman Podcast #452, November 11, 2024](https://lexfridman.com/dario-amodei-transcript/)):

> "You might think they're dishonest. Who knows? Maybe you're right, maybe you're not. But what you should do is you should take some people you trust and you should go off together and you should make your vision happen."

He learned to leave sooner. He did not learn to go alone.

Trust has been his organizing word since the company's first year. "The things that stand out to me are trust and common purpose. They're enormous force multipliers," he said of Anthropic's culture in March 2022 ([Future of Life Institute Podcast, March 4, 2022](https://futureoflife.org/podcast/daniela-and-dario-amodei-on-anthropic/)), before it had a product on the market. In 2026 he told podcaster Dwarkesh Patel that Anthropic holds together because "everyone has faith that everyone else there is working for the right reason." ([Dwarkesh Podcast, February 13, 2026](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/dario-amodei-2))

Daniela describes the bond on the record. "Dario and I have been fighting and getting over it for almost 40 years," she told a Stanford Graduate School of Business audience in 2026. "So we know how to work through conflict together, and there's no question we will love each other at the end of that, no matter what." Most of the other co-founders, she said, had reported to one or both siblings at OpenAI: "We'd like understood who we were, as people." ([Stanford GSB, June 29, 2026](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/daniela-amodei-says-curiosity-underrated))

For a Six, that is what solid ground looks like: a short list of people whose word has held for years.

## A company built to be checked

The Responsible Scaling Policy turns caution into "if-then" commitments that add safeguards once a model becomes dangerous, and Amodei says it "is checked by our long-term benefit trust," a group Wired describes as people with no financial stake in the company. He has said plainly that companies like his shouldn't be taken at their word:

> "I don't think you can trust these companies to adhere to these voluntary plans on their own."

He put Anthropic inside that principle, too, telling Fridman, "if there's nothing watching over us as an industry, there's no guarantee that we'll do the right thing."

Rules like that also bind his rivals, so they are strategy as well as conviction. The evaluators he promised in September 2026, with their desks and badges, are the same idea with furniture: a pre-commitment that binds the builder, plus an outsider empowered to check it.

Threat-modeling and calls for regulation are also an AI-safety chief's job, and critics read the whole program as strategy. When the pacing essay appeared, venture capitalist [Chamath Palihapitiya](/personality-analysis/chamath-palihapitiya) wrote that "Dario makes the case to stop open source and concentrate enormous technological and economic power with Anthropic" ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/09/12/anthropic-ai-amodei-pacing)). The essay never mentions open source, but it does ask governments "to require other frontier companies to match" Anthropic's commitment. AFP summarized the split: some see a humanist, others "a skilled operator willing to exploit collective fears about technology to serve Anthropic's interests."

He has answered on the record. Asked on 60 Minutes why people should trust Anthropic when some call its work "safety theater," he said: "So some of the things just can be verified now. They're not safety theater."

Strategy and milieu can explain much of his 2026 talk of trust. AFP reports he is considered a leading figure in the effective altruism and rationalist movements, though Anthropic told AFP he has repeatedly said he does not identify as part of the EA community. What strategy explains less is what came first: the record of being misled, the 2021 equity split and the 2022 "trust and common purpose" line, all from years when there were no shares to sell.

## Two fights with Washington

In February 2026 the Pentagon, which the Trump administration also calls the Department of War, demanded that Anthropic drop two limits on military use of its models: no mass domestic surveillance and no fully autonomous weapons. Amodei's signed statement said "frontier AI systems are simply not reliable enough to power fully autonomous weapons" and "we cannot in good conscience accede to their request." ([Anthropic, February 26, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/statement-department-of-war)) The Pentagon said it had no intention of mass surveillance or of removing humans from targeting decisions ([Fortune, February 27, 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/02/27/pentagon-brands-anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-a-liar-with-a-god-complex-as-deadline-looms-over-ai-use-in-weapons-and-surveillance/)).

The government answered on February 27. President [Donald Trump](/personality-analysis/donald-trump) ordered agencies to stop using Anthropic's technology, and Defense Secretary [Pete Hegseth](/personality-analysis/pete-hegseth) labeled it a supply-chain risk. Hours later, OpenAI announced its own Pentagon deal ([AP](https://apnews.com/article/anthropic-pentagon-ai-hegseth-dario-amodei-b72d1894bc842d9acf026df3867bee8a)). On CBS the next day, Amodei framed the fight as a question of oversight, "about who is holding the button and who can say no," and of loyalty ([CBS News transcript, February 28, 2026](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-full-transcript/)):

> "Disagreeing with the government is the most American thing in the world. And we are patriots."

Standing up to the most powerful authority in the country while insisting you're on its side is a Six's kind of defiance.

The private record was less composed. Within a few hours of the ban, he wrote a post to his staff that later leaked. On March 5 he apologized for "the tone of the post," which "does not reflect my careful or considered views," and repeated that Anthropic saw "no choice but to challenge it in court" ([Anthropic, March 5, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/where-stand-department-war)). It sued four days later.

The government had relied on two separate designations, and the courts split: a federal judge in San Francisco [ruled one designation unlawful](https://www.cnn.com/2026/08/27/tech/anthropic-pentagon-supply-chain-risk-unlawful-hnk) on August 27, and a divided D.C. appeals panel [upheld the other](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/25/pentagon-anthropic-ai-risk-appeals-court.html) on September 25.

The second front opened on June 12. A government export-control directive barred any foreign national, including Anthropic's own foreign-national employees, from its two newest models. To comply, the company switched them off for all customers ([Anthropic, June 12, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/fable-mythos-access)). Five days later, at a [working lunch with G7 leaders](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2026/06/17/trumps-anthropic-restrictions-rattle-us-allies-ai-leaders-gather-g-7/) in Évian-les-Bains, he told them to "resist the temptation to splinter," according to the Financial Times ([Bloomberg Law, June 17, 2026](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/artificial-intelligence/amodei-tells-g7-to-resist-temptation-to-splinter-on-ai-ft)). The controls were lifted on June 30, and Claude Fable 5 returned to users worldwide on July 1 ([Anthropic, June 30, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/redeploying-fable-5)).

When one authority proved unreliable, he reached for a bigger structure that no single directive could switch off. He had argued for exactly that, a "coalition of democracies," in his 2024 essay ["Machines of Loving Grace."](https://darioamodei.com/machines-of-loving-grace)

### Why not Type 5?

Until November 2026, 9takes typed him as a [Type 5](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-5), the Investigator, and that reading rests on real facts. In 2023, before he became one of AI's most visible executives, he told Dwarkesh Patel ([Dwarkesh Podcast, August 8, 2023](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/dario-amodei)):

> "I've deliberately tried to be a little bit low profile because I want to defend my ability to think about things intellectually in a way that's different from other people and isn't tinged by the approval of other people."

He thinks in long documents, like the nearly 14,000-word "Machines of Loving Grace," written, Wired reported, over "two frenzied days." Nathan Avery, who ran high school cross-country with him, told the Times: "He would often talk about the finiteness of time and how it was the one nonrenewable resource." Avery called him "ruthless with his time." Roose writes that he "had always tried to live below his means, and was slightly embarrassed by his new station."

Two more facts belong on the Five side. When he gave up on OpenAI in 2019, Roose writes, his first thought was to go work for DeepMind. Leaving as a group was Jared Kaplan's idea, and Amodei "was initially skeptical." He also told Chang in June 2026 that worrying isn't productive, and that studying people in history taught him to respond rationally and keep dangers in proportion.

That is a coherent Five portrait, and the 2023 quote is the strongest single line against this page's reading.

Here is the answer. Fives and Sixes share the head triad and its fear, and either one can write long memos and design safeguards. What separates them is what each leans on when the ground moves. A Five leans on his own understanding and a guarded store of time and energy, and would rather need less than depend on anyone. A Six leans on people and promises he has tested.

Amodei's costliest choices all run the second way. He kept a record of being misled, not of being overruled. He left OpenAI with a group, once Kaplan's idea had won him over. He split equity seven ways, handed his sister the executive team and told Bloomberg he never feels alone. A Six who thinks for a living can keep a Five's desk. A Five rarely builds his life around other people the way Amodei has.

### Why not Type 1?

[Type 1](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-1) has a case on standards and anger. At Princeton, Kantrowitz writes, his "strident sense of the way things should be" set him apart, and his parents, Amodei told him, "gave me a sense of right and wrong and what was important in the world." His anger runs hot when he feels misjudged. "I get really angry when someone's like, 'This guy's a doomer. He wants to slow things down,'" he told Kantrowitz. "You heard what I just said, my father died because of cures that could have happened a few years [earlier]."

A One's anger tracks what is wrong. A Six's alarm tracks danger and betrayal. His recorded anger mostly targets being misrepresented, which either would resent, so what tips it is how he argues. He called the Pentagon fight "the principle of standing up for what's right," the One reading's best line, but his stated reasons were reliability and who holds the button, not his own rightness.

And he bends where a purist would not. Kantrowitz reports he argued inside Anthropic that Gulf states' capital would help keep the company at the frontier, and in a Slack message obtained by Wired he wrote "Unfortunately," then: "I think 'No bad person should ever benefit from our success' is a pretty difficult principle to run a business on."

### Dario Amodei's wing: 6w5

The Five evidence above doesn't disappear under a Six reading. It reads as a 5 wing: the long documents, the guarded calendar, the 2023 wish to think "in a way that's different from other people." A 6w5 is the more private, more intellectual kind of Six, the one who studies the threat and writes it down before he takes it to his people.

## Seven votes

The hardest part of his record to type is control. Within OpenAI, Kantrowitz reports, some saw his safety focus "as a route to control the company altogether." The Times, citing people with knowledge of it, reports that in December 2018, when researcher Joshua Achiam pushed for an outside expert as chief ethics officer, "Amodei raised his voice and said no one was better suited for the position than him."

When Nvidia chief executive [Jensen Huang](/personality-analysis/jensen-huang) said in 2025, "He believes that AI is so scary that only they should do it," Amodei called it "an incredible and bad faith distortion."

From outside, securing a safe base and grabbing control look the same. One episode weighs against reading control as the point, as a Type 8 reading would: in November 2023, after OpenAI's board fired Altman, board members asked whether Amodei would merge the companies with himself as CEO, and he shut the discussion down. Wired's Steven Levy reported the approach in March 2025 ([Wired](https://www.wired.com/story/anthropic-benevolent-artificial-intelligence/)); Amodei chose not to comment on it.

In October 2026 his company filed to go public, and the filing is where the two readings finally come apart.

Anthropic's prospectus sets up a single share of a new Class F stock, issued as the offering closes, for a company owned by the seven co-founders. That share carries "50.1% of the total voting power" on matters put to shareholders, including the election of every director the company's trust does not pick. The trust, a body of five "financially disinterested Trustees," elects a majority of the board.

The filing warns that ordinary shareholders "generally will not be able to affect the outcome" of shareholder votes. ([Anthropic S-1/A, November 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026072484/anthropicpbc-sx1ax2.htm)) Morningstar's Michael Field told CNBC the open question was whether investors would "be fine with the lack of control implied" ([CNBC, October 30, 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/30/anthropic-ipo-s1-revenue-compute.html)).

That is real control, and critics are right to call it that. But look at who holds it.

Amodei doesn't hold it alone. The founders' share is voted by a majority of the seven, and "Each Continuing Founder will be entitled to one vote." If they can't agree, the share votes in line with the public shareholders. The prospectus, which he signed, explains the choice in a sentence that could sit under this page's type heading:

> "We believe that our seven co-founders, Dario Amodei, Daniela Amodei, Tom Brown, Jack Clark, Jared Kaplan, Sam McCandlish, and Christopher Olah, as a group, are distinctly equipped to be stewards of our mission."

When the biggest decision of his career arrived, he didn't take the control for himself, and he didn't hand it to the market. He gave it to the group he walked out of OpenAI with, one vote each, the way he split the equity in 2021, with an outside trust picking most of the board.

Whether the seven would vote against their own company when it counts, the record can't say yet. Asked by CBS in September 2026 whether America should slow down if China would not, he reached for a pact modeled on nuclear arms control "to put a speed limit on the rate of AI progress," one that would bind his company along with everyone else's ([CBS News, September 13, 2026](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-on-ai-risks/)).

"And honestly, I don't know if it's possible," he said, "but we should try."
