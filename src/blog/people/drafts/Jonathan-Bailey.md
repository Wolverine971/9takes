> "One thing that we're all born with is the sense of longing. Longing comes before anything else, doesn't it? ... And actually, if you long for someone, more often than not you don't think you are worthy of it. And that, to me, is a way into characters."

<p class="firstLetter">Jonathan Bailey was thirteen, in a leotard, and he had brought a friend to watch.</p>

The plan was a flex. A school friend was staying for the weekend, and before whatever they were actually going to do, Bailey wanted to swing by ballet class. His pitch, as he told [CBS Sunday Morning](https://www.cbsnews.com/video/here-comes-the-sun-jonathan-bailey-and-more/) in 2024: "I've just got to pop in and just take one photo with so many girls. It's going to be amazing. You're going to love it."

He had been the only boy in a leotard for fifty miles, by his own count, and had never been hyper aware of it. Then he looked up and found his friend's eyes across the room.

He stopped dancing that year.

Two decades later, Jon M. Chu cast him as Fiyero in *Wicked*. Of that first meeting with his director he told CBS in 2024: "we talked a lot about actually growing up and about dance and what it meant to play a character who literally dances through life now."

He turned thirty-eight in 2026. To most of the people reading this he is Anthony Bridgerton, the eldest brother on the biggest costume drama on Netflix, a man whose entire appeal is restraint failing in public. People named him its 2025 Sexiest Man Alive, a title NBC News and CBS News both reported as the first given to an openly gay man. He is the global face of Giorgio Armani's *I Will*, the brand's first new men's fragrance pillar in more than twenty years. Since June 2026 he has been The MARTINI Man, hired to sell *sprezzatura*, the Italian art of looking like you never practised.

He practised. Ballet, piano, clarinet, three seasons at the Royal Shakespeare Company before he was a teenager. The effortlessness is a manufactured good and he is the only one who knows the hours.

And he will not tell you who he loves, or whether there is anyone to name.

## What is Jonathan Bailey's personality type?

### Jonathan Bailey is an Enneagram Type 3

On the Enneagram he reads as a Type 3, the Achiever, most likely a 3w2 with a social-dominant instinct. The type carries one specific injury: the self keeps getting edited to match whatever the room reflects back.

Achievement is the visible symptom of [Type 3](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-3). What runs it is a nervous system permanently tuned to how a room is reading you.

Bailey has described that mechanism three times, unprompted, without any idea he was describing a personality pattern. Two of the three happened while he was hiding, and being closeted does most of that work on anybody, whatever their type. The third has nothing pressing on it at all, and that is the one that carries the type.

The first was the dance studio. His account of it, given to CBS Sunday Morning in 2024, is unusually precise about cause: "I just suddenly became aware of a story and a narrative that was put on something that I was so passionate about." Not that ballet became less fun. That a story arrived from outside and won.

The second was his twenties, closeted, working. "It was like a Tupperware box that had been locked down," he told [Attitude](https://www.attitude.co.uk/culture/film-tv/bridgertons-jonathan-bailey-talks-being-an-out-gay-actor-with-sir-ian-mckellen-304734/) in 2020. "It was affecting relationships, friendships, romantic relationships, because I felt like I was withdrawing." Then the line that matters most: "To be able to identify emotion seems to me completely parallel to being honest and authentic." He is describing a period where he could not locate his own feelings.

The third has no closet in it. At thirty-seven, safe and decorated, he talked himself out of a backflip he can still land, in case a failed one ended up on TikTok. Nobody was reading him. He read himself anyway.

The Three shows in what he did next. He did not argue with the reading, and he did not go looking for a better room to be seen in. He took one glance as data and turned it on himself. The recognition was instant, by his own account; the edit was partial. He dropped the dance and kept the acting and the singing.

Three decades of behavioural evidence, all of it sourced and all of it repeating, make the core type a safe call. His wing and his dominant instinct are not, and neither one is settled here.

## Jonathan Bailey's Childhood and the Crying Raindrop

Three decades is not a figure of speech here.

His grandmother took him to *Oliver!* in the West End when he was five. He came home and said that was what he wanted to do. She was ninety-five when he last told it, and he has checked the story with her, because he does not quite believe it either.

His first stage role came around the same time, at his village primary school: a raindrop in *Noah's Ark*. He had choreography. The boy playing Noah missed the cue for the rain, so the rain never fell, so the dance never happened. Bailey cried. At the end, once the applause had finished and the parents were reaching for their coats, they let him do it.

He tells this as a comic bit. "A crying raindrop in Noah's Ark," he told his *Crashing* creator Phoebe Waller-Bridge in Interview magazine in 2024. "It didn't rain. The bitch that played Noah forgot the cue." In the same conversation he described the boy who wept over it as "five going on 29."

Within a year, the Royal Shakespeare Company had scouted him, and at six he faced a choice between the RSC at the Barbican and playing Nipper in the very show that started it. He took the RSC. He had three seasons there before he was a teenager, and his parents refused to treat any of it as remarkable. "I didn't miss a day of school," he told People in 2025. "My parents were like, get back, tables first."

He is the youngest of four and the only boy, the little brother of three sisters who, in the same interview, "were like my heroes growing up." He wore a head brace for two years, which he remembers as "kind of awkward and painful actually," and describes the boy wearing it as "really enthusiastic, probably quite annoying, hard to calm down."

Then comes the line that carries the rest of his life, from the same People conversation: "I had the same passions as I did when I was sort of five. So I sort of knew who I was." He puts an expiry date on that certainty in a separate interview with British GQ in June 2025: "I remember at primary school being really clear and then it sort of gets murky as you get older."

Something happened between five and thirty that made a very clear boy murky, and he has never once described it as something anybody did to him.

## Three Times Jonathan Bailey Read the Room and Made a Decision

He has spent his working life responding to cues. The first one he ever waited for did not come. Here are three that did.

### Thirteen: the friend in the doorway

*Cue: a school friend looks across a dance studio.*

"I just remember clocking his eyes across the room and it was that one moment in me that I went, oh, this isn't right, this isn't actually the right thing I should be doing." (CBS Sunday Morning, 2024)

Nobody said anything. No incident, no confrontation, nothing to point at afterwards. He had chosen those classes over basketball, and one silent reading of himself was enough to overturn it. Then he removed the evidence.

<p class="inner-thought">He is watching this. This isn't the right thing I should be doing. I did not know that until now.</p>

### Thirty: the calls that went to his agent

*Cue: casting directors and publicists telephone his representation.*

"Oh, no, you can't come out. You shouldn't really do that." (Attitude, 2020)

The people advising silence were, he has said, mostly other gay men in the industry, some of them casting directors who rang his agent to make the point. He described the going rate plainly, in the same conversation: "They want you to be gay, but not too gay." This time the reading arrived loudly, from professionals, with a career attached to it. He came out publicly in 2018, the year he turned thirty. Five years later, with *Bridgerton* making him the most bookable romantic lead on television, he took the role the advice had been built to prevent: Tim Laughlin in *Fellow Travelers*, a devout young Senate staffer working through the McCarthy-era purge of gay employees while in love with a man neither of them can name out loud. He took the part of a man who cannot name what he feels, and got an Emmy nomination for it.

### Thirty-seven: the backflip he didn't do

*Cue: a magazine asks the world's sexiest man for a party trick.*

"I would hate for my back flip to go wrong and it be on TikTok. So I'm just slightly more cautious." (People, 2025)

He can still land one, mostly. He said so in the same breath, then noted that as long as your hands hit the floor the crowd goes wild anyway. Twenty-four years after the leotard, he is still pricing what a failed version would look like to strangers.

## Is Jonathan Bailey Dating Anyone? What He Will and Will Not Say

The same calculation runs on much bigger things than a backflip.

The honest answer to the search query is that it is not publicly established, and the record is openly contradictory. In December 2023 he told the Evening Standard he was seeing "a lovely man" and declined to name him. In July 2025, closing out an episode of *Chicken Shop Date*, the deadpan comic interview series where flirting with the guest is the running bit, he told [Amelia Dimoldenberg](/personality-analysis/amelia-dimoldenberg) he was available. Both statements are on the record. Neither has been reconciled, and the widely circulated name attached to him for years traces back to a single 2019 awards-show moment and has never been confirmed by him. In that same Evening Standard conversation he had already drawn the distinction he has held ever since, describing the life he keeps back as "private" rather than "secret."

Some people find the whole posture convenient, and they have a point worth stating at full strength. Ambiguity is commercially useful for a romantic lead. It is useful for a fragrance ambassador and it is extremely useful for a Sexiest Man Alive. He has never had to prove he would protect the privacy at a cost to himself, because so far it has cost him nothing. The noise arrives anyway. In November 2025, after a viral NYT Cooking video with his *Wicked* co-star [Ariana Grande](/personality-analysis/ariana-grande), a corner of the internet started speculating that he might not be gay. A much larger wave called that homophobic and said so loudly.

Here is the part the judgment misses. For the image type, being read by other people is not a social inconvenience. It is the mechanism by which the self gets overwritten. Bailey has one documented, catastrophic experience of it, at thirteen, and a second slower one through his twenties that he describes as a sealed container and a withdrawal from everyone who loved him. In that same December 2023 conversation he calls a private life "completely critical," and gives a reason that is operational rather than defensive: "I don't know if I would be able to be as confident to speak out on other things if I felt that my whole life was up for grabs." The fenced room is what pays for the speaking out. Remove it and the advocacy goes with it.

He is not shy about the rest of it. Waller-Bridge told him to his face in 2024: "You're like a chemistry machine. There's this incredible erotic energy that people are so excited about." He will give you the longing. He has built an entire career out of manufacturing it on demand. The one thing he keeps is the fact of who it is for, if it is for anyone.

## What Critics Said About Jonathan Bailey's Richard II

The fence shows up in the work too.

In February 2025, in the gap between the two *Wicked* films, Bailey went back to a London stage and did 103 performances of *Richard II* for Nicholas Hytner at the Bridge Theatre.

The reviews split along one seam. [Time Out's Andrzej Lukowski](https://www.timeout.com/london/theatre/richard-ii-10-review), who found the evening as a whole "okay but a bit stilted," granted in passing that "Jonathan Bailey is a great stage actor," gave the production three stars, and still found that "the final passages lose considerable poignancy through Richard's triviality." Matt Wolf, writing in [The Arts Desk](https://www.theartsdesk.com/theatre/richard-ii-bridge-theatre-review-handsomely-mounted-emotionally-muted) under the headline "handsomely mounted, emotionally muted," also gave three stars, granted that "Bailey, an infinitely warmer performer, certainly has the toolkit to chart the character's acquaintanceship with humility," and then asked the only question that counts in that play: "will you weep for this Richard? As of my recent visit, I'd say no."

They are describing a real thing. The charm arrives on time, every night, at full wattage. The grief takes longer to get there.

That is what an instrument built this way does. A performer trained from the age of five to read a house and give it the thing that lands has one blind spot, and it is precisely the register that cannot be calibrated to a house. Charm is a transaction with a room. Sorrow is not, and neither is love, which is the same border he polices offstage: the room gets what can be given to a room, and the rest stays behind the fence.

The obvious objection is that he does grief on camera and gets nominated for it. Anthony's panic in *Bridgerton* when Kate is stung by a bee. His father dying in a garden years before that. Eight hours of Tim Laughlin. All true, and it sharpens the claim: a scene built to deliver him there across a whole series is a different instrument from a nine-hundred-seat house that needs it on cue, nightly, from a standing start. His own method starts with what a character is reaching for, which is a beautiful way to build a person and also a way of approaching grief from the outside, through appetite. Wolf himself allowed that the depth might arrive later in the run. That is the whole question about Bailey in one clause: the toolkit is not in doubt, the arrival time is.

## Did Jonathan Bailey Quit Acting? What the Time Off Was Actually For

Seven months after he opened as Richard, in September 2025, he was cycling between Rome and Pisa on his way to a friend's wedding, on a bad line to a British GQ journalist. What came out the other end, and what he has since called "an unfortunate misquote," was this: "I've been working solidly for about three years now, which has been amazing and mind-blowing. But with everything happening in the world right now, I'm going to stop acting for a bit next year and just focus on The Shameless Fund."

It went around the world as Jonathan Bailey quits acting. His sister rang him. "Wow, is everything OK? You've given up?" He spent two months correcting it. What he had meant to say, he told [Esquire](https://www.eonline.com/news/1424863/jonathan-bailey-clarifies-acting-hiatus) in November, was "Next year I'll be back." He put the rest beyond misreading: "I'll definitely be back to work next year," and "I'm not taking the whole of 2026 off." His takeaway from the episode was not indignation: "I learnt at that moment that you can't control it."

The Shameless Fund is a UK grantmaking nonprofit he founded in June 2024, and the reason he gives for it is a single number.
