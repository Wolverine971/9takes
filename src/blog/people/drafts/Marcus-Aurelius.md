> — Marcus Aurelius to his tutor Fronto c. 146 AD, Loeb Classical Library vol. 1, 1919

<p class="firstLetter">In 162 AD the emperor of Rome took four days off at Alsium, a seaside town up the coast from the capital. When he got back, he wrote to his old tutor and refused to say how he had spent them. Both letters survive in the Loeb Classical Library edition of the Fronto correspondence, 1920.</p>

"In what holiday-wise we have kept our holiday at Alsium I will not put on paper, that you may not be yourself troubled and scold me, my master."

Fronto scolded him anyway. His 162 AD reply, in the same Loeb Classical Library volume, drops the mock innocence fast.

"It were easier to reconcile you to a polecat than to pleasure. Tell me, Marcus, I beseech you, have you repaired to Alsium only to fast with the sea in sight? What, could you not wear yourself out at Lorium with hunger and thirst and doing business?"

The man being teased in that letter, one of the funniest documents to survive from the second century, is the one whose face now sells composure on a million book covers. His private notebook, **Meditations**, is one of the most durable self-help texts in the Western canon, and the quality everyone buys it for is calm.

His own teacher could not talk him into a beach.

The easy version of this story would be hypocrisy, and it is not available. Marcus Aurelius taught nobody, built no school of his own, published no doctrine, and addressed his notebook to a single reader. The discipline ran inward, with no audience to impress and no rule he could be caught breaking.

So the question is what it was aimed at.

## Who Marcus Aurelius Was, and How Rome Picked Him Twice

He was born in Rome on 26 April 121, into a wealthy senatorial family, and chosen for the throne before he was old enough to want it. In January 138 the dying Hadrian named Antoninus Pius his heir on one condition: that Pius adopt the sixteen-year-old Marcus, and a boy named Lucius with him. Two men arranged it over his head, and both were dead before he had to use it.

He got twenty-three years to prepare, schooled in rhetoric by Marcus Cornelius Fronto, the most celebrated Latin orator of the age. Somewhere in there the training took a turn Fronto never fully forgave. Marcus found the Stoics through a different teacher, Junius Rusticus, and thanks him for it in the notebook: "I am indebted to him for being acquainted with the discourses of Epictetus, which he communicated to me out of his own collection."

Antoninus Pius died on 7 March 161. Marcus was thirty-nine, and his first act as emperor was to refuse the job as offered: he would not take office unless his adoptive brother Lucius Verus received equal powers. Nothing obliged him to do it, and Rome had never had two emperors at once. He split supreme power for eight years with a man who liked chariot teams and long dinners.

The reign that followed was one long emergency. War broke out in the east almost immediately, and the army that won it brought a pandemic home. The Antonine Plague, which began in 165 or 166, is estimated to have killed five to ten million. Germanic tribes crossed the Danube while the empire was still burying them. Verus died in 169. In 175 a general in Syria declared himself emperor on a false report that Marcus was dead.

He died on 17 March 180, aged fifty-eight, on the northern frontier. Ancient sources disagree about whether it was at Vindobona, modern Vienna, or at Sirmium, and the dispute has never been settled.

Cassius Dio, close enough to the events to know people who were there, put the reign in one sentence: "he did not meet with the good fortune he deserved, for he was not strong in body and was involved in a multitude of troubles throughout practically his entire reign."

## Two Archives, One Emperor: Marcus Aurelius at 22 and at 54

That is the outside view. Almost no historical figure leaves two first-person records of himself three decades apart. Marcus did, and the second was never supposed to exist.

The first is the Fronto correspondence, lost for a thousand years and recovered in the nineteenth century from a reused manuscript. It catches him young and unguarded, and a day without a letter from his teacher is unbearable: "To me this day seems, and will seem, longer than a spring day, and the coming night more tedious than a night in winter." It runs hotter than that in places, into the language of physical longing, and scholars have argued for two centuries over how to read such language between a Roman student and his tutor. What matters here is the temperament the discipline was applied to.

The second is the notebook, written in Greek, in his fifties, in army camps on the Danube.

## What is Marcus Aurelius's personality type?

### Marcus Aurelius is an Enneagram Type 1

He grades his own conduct against a standard he can state and cannot reach. Praise is acknowledged and changes nothing. Failure draws penance rather than excuse. And the correction goes down on paper, privately, over and over, for a decade.

Plenty of people hold high standards. What marks a [Type 1](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-1) is the machinery underneath: the standard measures whether you are good at the root, and the marking continues in an empty room. With Marcus the room was always empty.

Every Stoic marked himself in an empty room, which is the objection this typing has to survive. The exercise Rusticus handed him answers it. The version Epictetus drilled into students, at Discourses 3.10 in George Long's 1877 translation, ends "blame what is wrong, in what is right rejoice." Marcus ran the blame half for thirty years. The rejoicing half he wrote out as Book 1, and assigned every item in it to somebody else.

The Ariston letter carries the argument on its own. He was twenty-five, apologising to Fronto for an exercise he had not written, and the penance list in the epigraph, printed at page 217 of the 1919 Loeb Classical Library edition, is the excuse: blushing, self-anger, sadness, comparison, self-starvation, no trace of self-pity.

Beside it, in George Long's 1862 translation, Book 5 carries an instruction he needed: "Be not disgusted, nor discouraged, nor dissatisfied" when he fails to act on principle, and, "when thou hast failed, return back again, and be content if the greater part of what thou doest is consistent with man's nature." Thirty years in, disgust was still the default, and the correction he kept reissuing was permission to settle for most of it.

One pressure test, because this typing has an obvious rival: the anxiety in his letters looks like fear, and fear is Type 6 territory. The 143 Senate letter is the test case, and it turns on what he is afraid of. A Six in that position is managing danger. His worry is not that something bad will happen to him. It is that he may not deserve his own teacher.

## Why Marcus Aurelius Could Not Enjoy a Holiday

That case ran privately. Rome noticed the joylessness anyway, and found it funny.

The **Historia Augusta**, a late and famously unreliable set of imperial biographies, records a habit easy to picture: "It was customary with Marcus to read, listen to, and sign documents at the circus-games; because of this habit he was openly ridiculed, it is said, by the people."

That source earns the suspicion it usually gets. What makes it stick is Fronto, complaining about the same behavior decades earlier with no reason to invent it. Around 146 he admits to gossiping about Marcus: "Time was I did this, when you went about in public gatherings with too serious a face, as when you used to read books either in the theatre or at a banquet." Two sources, a generation apart, watching him work through the show.

There is a harsher version. In a letter dated to his 175 revolt, in the Historia Augusta's life of him, Avidius Cassius complains that Marcus "philosophizes and meditates on first principles" and "takes no thought for the state," and "wishes to be called merciful and hence suffers to live men whose manner of life he cannot sanction." That collection puts speeches in mouths and its letters are generally judged forgeries, so read it as a charge someone found plausible. The same collection says his philosophy made him "seem harsh in his military discipline and indeed in his life in general," and that he "was bitterly assailed" for it.

Then there is the objection that outlasts those. In 177, Christians were tortured and killed at Lugdunum, modern Lyon, on his watch. How much he knew or ordered is contested. The detailed account is a letter the churches of Lyon and Vienne wrote at the time, preserved by Eusebius a century later, and in it the governor writes to the emperor and waits: "For Caesar commanded that they should be put to death, but that any who might deny should be set free." Against that: no other contemporary source records the episode, the letter names no emperor, Marcus was weeks' march away, and Tertullian, alive then, called him a protector of Christians, on the strength of a document widely judged spurious. What nobody disputes is that these deaths sit inside the reign of the philosopher on the book covers. It does not resolve, and it should not be smoothed over.

The smaller charges do resolve. Concede what the critics get right: the discipline made him worse company. Fronto spends a whole comic essay begging him to sleep, and ends it in exasperated affection: "Now if you have declared war on play, relaxation, good living, and pleasure, yet do sleep as a freeman should."

What they missed is sitting in the open in Book 1.

Book 1 is a list of debts. Seventeen entries, working through family, tutors and finally the gods, assigning every decent quality he has to whoever gave it to him. "To the gods I am indebted for having good grandfathers, good parents, a good sister, good teachers, good associates, good kinsmen and friends, nearly everything good." He credits himself with nothing. Not one line.

That is the explanation of the beach. A man who believes every good thing in him is borrowed experiences rest as spending money he did not earn, while the lenders are dead and cannot be repaid and the plague is killing millions he was handed. Working through the circus games reads to the crowd as contempt. From inside, it is a man who cannot locate the moment the debt would be settled.

That still made him hard to be around, and Fronto was right to push. Rome spent nineteen years reading the ledger as arrogance.

## Why Marcus Aurelius Kept Writing Reminders About Anger

Fronto was pushing at something real. The notebook returns to anger at a frequency no serene man would require.

Book 2 braces him for the day: "Begin the morning by saying to thyself, I shall meet with the busybody, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial." Book 5 argues him down from irritation at another person's body: "Art thou angry with him whose armpits stink?" Book 11 runs a numbered list of reminders for handling people who have wronged him.

Nobody writes a numbered protocol for a problem they do not have.

This is the least visible thing about Ones and the most important. Their fuel is anger, and it is the last thing they will name, because losing your temper is itself evidence in the case against you. So it gets renamed on the way out, into disappointment, or firmness, or a polite sentence delivered a few degrees cooler. What survives the renaming gets stored.

Fronto saw the residue and said so, in that same confession about gossiping, which he offers as proof that he loves him: "I would call you an austere and unreasonable, even at times, stung by anger, a disagreeable sort of person." He adds that he could not bear to hear it from anyone else. That is the person closest to him, teasing a young man about a temper he was learning to keep off his face, and the notebook is that management written down.

### Marcus Aurelius's Wing: 1w2

The <a href="/enneagram-corner/enneagram-wings-complete-guide">wing</a> call is 1w2, and it is the least certain judgment on this page.

The axis is direction. The 1w2 advocate aims the correcting impulse at people and leaves the bench to fix your case personally; the 1w9 idealist turns it inward and rarely corrects you at all. On that axis Marcus reads 1w9, and the concession has to be made plainly: his correcting ran inward. What pulls the call back is that his obligations ran to named people rather than to principles. He would not take the throne unless Lucius Verus got equal powers. Book 1 is an inventory of specific people he owes. And when Avidius Cassius raised an army against him in 175 and was killed by his own officers, Marcus refused the win: the Historia Augusta has him refusing to rejoice, ordering Cassius' head buried, and pushing the Senate to pardon the family.


### Marcus Aurelius's Instinctual Subtype: self-preservation

The dominant <a href="/enneagram-corner/enneagram-instinctual-subtypes">instinct</a> reads as self-preservation, showing up as bodily austerity used as a moral instrument.

Cassius Dio reports that "he took but very little food and that always at night" and that close study left him "extremely frail in body." The self-preservation One turns the standard on their own appetites first, and discharges worry through regimen. He had an empire he could have corrected instead, and kept starting with the body he was in.

### Stress and Growth Arrows

Under stress the One moves toward Four, and the Ariston penances run it in sequence: sadness and comparison arriving together as personal deficiency. The 143 Senate letter shows the same movement in miniature, where praise cannot dislodge the mood.

The growth line runs to Seven, faint but real. Book 3 has him noticing that bread splits at the surface when baked, that "figs, when they are quite ripe, gape open," that ripe olives are most beautiful near rottenness. A Seven finds that everywhere by default. Marcus reasoned his way to it and wrote it down like a discovery.

### Counterarguments: Why Marcus Aurelius Might Not Be Type 1

Type 5 is the rival worth taking seriously. The reading, the withdrawal, the private notebook, and the retreat into his own mind all point that way, and he builds the Five's ideal explicitly: "the mind which is free from passions is a citadel."

What weakens it is narrower than it first looks. Meditations does carry real cosmological argument, on flux, on the view from above, on the whole and the part. But the behavioral instructions, which recur throughout it, barely develop at all: the same short list, restated for a decade. A Five builds. This rehearses.

The second rival is Type 6, treated in the main analysis. His duty, his loyalty to Antoninus, and his visible anxiety all fit. What separates them is the content of the fear: danger for a Six, desert for Marcus.

The strongest objection is not a rival type at all. Ones are supposed to correct other people, and Marcus mostly declined to. He bore blame without returning it, and at twenty-two he asked Fronto to go easy on a courtroom opponent, in a letter whose plain sense is a plea for mercy. That narrows the type instead of breaking it: he held unlimited power to correct everyone in the empire and pointed the standard almost entirely at himself.

Type 1 is also among the rarest reads in the 9takes corpus ([corpus stats](/corpus-stats)). Ones look, from outside, like people who have their act together.

What would change our minds: evidence that the self-criticism was situational. The two archives close that door, three decades apart, saying the same thing.

## How Marcus Aurelius Paid for a War He Could Not Win Quickly

The same standard decided what he did with money. By 169 the treasury was empty, the plague was still running, and the Danube frontier needed an army Rome could not afford. Raising provincial taxes was the obvious move.

He held a yard sale instead.

The inventory is specific, which is why it has stuck for eighteen centuries: goblets of gold and crystal, flagons made for kings, his wife's silk robes worked with gold, and jewels he had found in a cabinet of Hadrian's that the biographer calls holy. When the war was funded, he told the buyers that any of them who wanted their money back could return the goods and have it.

Dio corroborates the auction independently, and records him telling the senate, on a later occasion, the plainest sentence he ever aimed at it: "As for us, we are so far from possessing anything of our own that even the house in which we live is yours." Dio also notes that people still faulted him in his own day "on the ground that he was not an open-handed prince."

The notebook works the same problem from inside. Book 6 carries a warning that only makes sense if he thought corruption a live risk: "Take care that thou art not made into a Caesar, that thou art not dyed with this dye; for such things happen." The name in it had belonged to [Julius Caesar](/personality-analysis/julius-caesar) before it became a job description, and Marcus treated wearing it as a stain risk. The next instruction says what he was afraid of. "Do everything as a disciple of Antoninus." At roughly fifty, running an empire, he was still marking his work against a dead man.

## Marcus Aurelius, Faustina, and the Children He Buried

Antoninus was not the only person he had buried.

He married Faustina the Younger in April 145 and stayed married to her for thirty years. Ancient sources record thirteen or fourteen children. Six were alive when their mother died: five daughters and Commodus.

Do the subtraction. He buried at least seven of his own children, most of them small, and at least five before he was ever emperor.

Book 1 carries a line that changes once you know that arithmetic. Thanking the gods for everything, he slips in this: "that my children have not been stupid nor deformed in body." That is a man counting what was left.

The **Historia Augusta** supplies the detail hardest to hold. In 169 his seven-year-old son Verus died after an operation on a tumour under his ear. "For no more than five days did he mourn him," the biographer writes, "and even during this period, when consulted on public affairs he gave some time to them." The source is unreliable and the number may be invented, and it gives its own reason four lines on: the games of Jupiter were running and he did not want them halted for public mourning. What that does not explain is the business he took during them, and that is what Fronto had been complaining about for twenty years.

When Faustina died in 175 in a village below the Taurus mountains, he had her made a god, founded a colony there, and endowed a fund for orphaned girls. Whatever he felt went into institutions.

The notebook handles it alone, in Greek. He turns Homer on the generations of men onto his own household, "Leaves, also, are thy children," and copies out a line of Epictetus on the only safe way to love one: "When a man kisses his child," it runs, "he should whisper to himself, To-morrow perchance thou wilt die." He did not have to imagine that.

## Marcus Aurelius and Commodus: The Correction He Never Made

Then there is the decision that will not resolve, and it concerns the son who lived. In 177 he made Commodus co-emperor, breaking a run of adoptive successions that had produced four competent rulers. Commodus went on to a reign ancient writers treated as a disaster, and one modern audiences know mostly through **Gladiator** (2000), which invented its own version of his father's death. The film is fiction; the question underneath is not.

Dio, otherwise an admirer, puts it as the single flaw in the ledger: "Just one thing prevented him from being completely happy, namely, that after rearing and educating his son in the best possible way he was vastly disappointed in him."

Whether Marcus had a real alternative by then is genuinely disputed. A living adult son had never been passed over for an adopted heir, and doing it would likely have meant the civil war he narrowly avoided in 175. But the path was set long before the argument got hard. Commodus was made Caesar in October 166, aged five, when nobody could assess his character at all.

The shape of it is clearer than the verdict. The man who audited himself daily for four decades, who wrote a warning against being changed by the purple, who could not take four days at the beach without expecting a scolding, applied none of that scrutiny to the one decision that outlived him.

The Historia Augusta claims he saw it coming and wished his son would die young rather than become another Nero. That is the sort of retrospective prophecy the collection invents. What the same source says of his final days is quieter and harder to dismiss: on the seventh day of his illness he admitted only Commodus, then sent him out of the room at once, in fear that the boy would catch what was killing him.

## The Book He Never Meant Anyone to Read

He wrote the notebook in Greek, a second language, in a camp at night, between administrative days that ran into the dark. Book 1 ends with a location note, among the Quadi on the river Granua, and Book 2 with another, this in Carnuntum. One of the most reproduced philosophy books in the world has a war for a return address.

Then it vanished for centuries. The first possible mention is Themistius in 364 AD, and scholars dispute whether he meant the text or the man's reputation. The trail goes cold until around 900, when a Byzantine bishop, Arethas of Caesarea, turns out to own a manuscript and recopies it. Its Greek title, *ta eis heauton*, means something close to *to himself*, and a later reader may have supplied it, seeing what the pages obviously were.

Everything you can buy at an airport with his face on it descends from that accident.

The reading it gets now inverts the pages. He is the calm one, the meme with the marble bust and the serene caption, quoted so relentlessly that he gets quoted saying things he never wrote. PolitiFact fact-checked one of the most viral of them in September 2019, a line about opinion and perspective that no one has been able to find in the book.

The real ones are harder to put on a poster. They are corrections, issued to a man who kept needing them, by the only person qualified to issue them and the only one who would ever read them.

He wrote for an audience of one for roughly ten years. Book 8, in the same 1862 George Long text, opens on the shortfall, plain "both to many others and to thyself," and then turns: "throw away the thought, How thou shalt seem to others, and be content if thou shalt live the rest of thy life in such a way as thy nature wills." He wrote down the verdict and the permission to stop caring who saw it, and he kept needing both.

He was fifty-four, and he had been at it since he was twenty-five, and he was still marking the same paper.
