<p class="firstLetter">Ms. Rachel asks a toddler if she can say "mama," and then she stops.</p>

One second. Two. Three. She holds the camera's eye with her mouth slightly open and her hands still, doing nothing at all, while somewhere past the lens a child's brain assembles a word.

That silence is the product. Speech-language pathologists call it expectant pausing and put the useful window at three to five seconds. It is why so many parents hand their toddlers a woman in a bow headband on YouTube instead of a cartoon. The songs on *Songs for Littles* are written and arranged by a Broadway associate conductor and recorded with real horn players. It is a well-made show. The pause is the technology.

Rachel Griffin Accurso learned it the hard way. Her son Thomas said his first word at two years and eight months old. It was "mama." She counts it in months, the way parents do when the waiting gets long enough to need a number.

Born in 1982 in Biddeford, Maine, she pointed a camera at her living room in 2019. By the time The Hollywood Reporter visited her studio in November 2025, the channel had 17.5 million subscribers, Netflix had called its version of the show the biggest kids launch in the platform's history, and she had become one of the most argued-about people on the internet, which is a strange outcome for someone whose entire job is holding still.

She gives that silence to every child on earth. She has never once taken it for herself.

## Why Ms. Rachel Stops Talking

**She asks.** Her pitch climbs about an octave above her speaking voice, her vowels stretch, her eyebrows go up. This is parentese. Exaggerated pitch makes the individual sounds of a word easier for an immature auditory system to pull apart.

**She stops.** Most adults cannot do this. Conversational turn-taking in English runs on gaps of roughly a fifth of a second, and every instinct a fluent speaker has says fill it.

**Nothing comes back, and she lets it.** The speech-language pathologists who teach this put the window at three to five seconds, ten to twenty times an ordinary conversational gap. A neurotypical toddler is still at the front of it. A child with a language delay has barely begun. Comprehension, retrieval and motor planning each take their own time, and none are fast at two.

**She does not rescue the moment.** Her face does not drift. She does not look away, glance at a mark, or soften into a smile that says never mind. The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association tells parents to pause after speaking, and the reason so few manage it is that several seconds of undivided adult attention, aimed at a child producing nothing, is uncomfortable to hold.

**She answers back.** As though the child had spoken. Whether or not the child did.

The sequence is easier to see with the sound off. Every light in the frame is on the kid, and the biggest children's educator on the internet is taking up no space at all. Her most characteristic posture is by now also a production technique with a Netflix license attached.

## What is Ms. Rachel's personality type?

### Ms. Rachel is an Enneagram Type 2

The evidence is not the gentleness. Gentleness is her job.

The evidence is what she does when someone tells her the gentleness is fake.

In April 2025, the advocacy group StopAntisemitism asked Attorney General Pam Bondi to investigate whether Accurso was being paid to post about Gaza. The group produced no public evidence, and no investigation has been reported opened in the sixteen months since. It was still a financial accusation, the kind of thing a lawyer answers with bank statements. Interview after interview, she answered it with something else.

"It's really painful," she told WBUR's *Here & Now* in June 2025. "And I have to remind myself that people don't know my heart, and people try to tell you who you are, but you know who you are."

On Democracy Now! in August 2025, asked about the same accusation: "That's really painful, because obviously it's not true." She kept going: "It is difficult to receive criticism, but I know who I am."

Asked about money, she talks about her heart. Asked about a funding trail, she says she knows who she is. That reflex is the best evidence in the file. [Type 2s](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-2) locate their identity inside the realness of their care, which means an accusation of insincerity does not land as a factual dispute. It lands as an attack on the self, and it gets answered like one.

She said the quiet part out loud in a trade profile about studio equipment. Talking to The Hollywood Reporter in November 2025 about lighting and edit software, she volunteered: "I can't fake being genuine. I just, I love children so much."

Nobody had asked whether she was faking.

The other tell is the amount. "I think it might be a little abnormal, the amount of empathy I have," she told The Washington Post in 2025. A surplus, measured out as a quantity, as though the difference between her and everyone else were volume, when it is the thing holding the whole structure up.

## Ms. Rachel's Maine Childhood and the Trolley She Tried to Stop

The surplus is older than the audience.

She grew up in Springvale, a village inside Sanford, in southern Maine. Local Maine reporting has her raised by a single mother. Past that the record thins, and the sites claiming to know more about the Griffins contradict each other.

On Democracy Now! in August 2025, describing [Mister Rogers](/personality-analysis/mr-rogers): "I grew up loving Mr. Rogers, and I used to go up to the TV and try and block the trolley from leaving because I didn't want the show to end."

A small girl gets out of her chair and plants herself against the glass, because the show is ending and she has decided to physically prevent it.

She spent roughly twenty years of adult life on the other side of that transaction, and none of it was glamorous. A summer program for children with disabilities. Music lessons for refugee kids through a Boys and Girls Club in Maine. A public preschool classroom in New York City. Two master's degrees stacked on the bachelor's, music education at NYU and early childhood education after that, in a field where nobody gets rich.

The pattern predates the platform by two decades. She was choosing the children other systems had the least time for when there was no audience, no algorithm, and no money in it.

"Before I did this," she told the Post, "there were so many 'Oh, now I've found what I'm meant to do!' moments."

## How Rachel Accurso Built Songs for Littles Out of a Living Room

The founding story is usually told as an inspiring one. The inspiring version leaves out the year of nothing.

Thomas was born in March 2018. When the speech delay was diagnosed, she went looking for material and found almost none. "A lot of things I teach are things I wish I had known for my son," she told HuffPost UK in early 2023. So she began doing the exercises herself, on camera, in the living room, editing in iMovie.

The format has barely changed since: hold up an object, name it, sing a short song built around the word, ask for the word back, then let a puppet supply it if the child at home does not.

She was talking to a child who was not talking back. Not for a week. For months, and then more months, and then the word came.

"I had waited for that for so long," she said.

The channel launched in 2019 into essentially no audience. Then in 2020 every parent in the developed world was locked in a house with a toddler, and the thing she had built for one boy turned out to be built for all of them.

Her husband, Aron Accurso, spent seventeen years on Broadway and was associate music director on *Aladdin* before leaving to put his hand inside an orange-haired puppet named Herbie. Herbie is the show's stand-in for the child watching, the one who answers Ms. Rachel so a toddler at home has somebody to answer alongside. A Broadway conductor gave up *Aladdin* to play the kid who talks back, because for thirty-two months, nobody had.

"I can be such a perfectionist," Aron told The Washington Post. "But we never would have gotten this off the ground if she hadn't been like, at some point, 'Who cares if it's not perfect? It's for the kids.'"

Everyone assumes the woman doing a forty-minute take of a nursery rhyme is the control freak in the marriage. Her husband, on the record, says she is the one who ships.

Tareq Hailat, who runs the treatment program at the Palestine Children's Relief Fund and has worked with her off camera, told the Post the same thing from outside the marriage: "She is in real life as she is on screen."

A company arrived with the audience. She signed with CAA in 2023, licensed the show to Netflix, put out a toy line and a doll, took a Penguin Random House imprint. The educator who spent two decades in rooms with no money in them was by 2026 running an operation with agents, lawyers and a merchandising calendar. Somebody in that building is very good at saying no. Nothing on the record establishes that it is her.

Then came the Cameo marathon of May 2024: 500 personalized videos at $100 apiece, the whole take going to a children's emergency fund covering Gaza, Sudan, Ukraine, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo.

"I wanted to make each video really long and special," she told the Post.

<p class="inner-thought">Three hundred down. The voice is going. This kid's parents paid the same as everyone else. Do the whole song.</p>

She lost her voice. The instrument the entire enterprise runs on, spent in a stretch, for $50,000, because a version where some children got less was not a version she could sit inside.

## Why Parents Find Ms. Rachel Unbearable

Millions of parents hear that instrument every morning, and an entire genre of adult complaint about it has nothing to do with politics.

It is about the voice. The stretched vowels, the enormous eyebrows, the relentless brightness. Parents joke about it in group chats the way people joke about a song stuck in their head, which is to say with real hostility. In June 2025 the actress Olivia Munn told Parents and People that kids' shows drive her "crazy" and named Ms. Rachel as one she does not allow. Accurso replied to the magazines about their framing, and Munn spent the following week being defended by her husband against strangers making violent threats.

Here is what that voice actually is. It is a register a mother held for one specific boy, into a silence that had already lasted two years. It sounds performed to an adult because adults have never had to sustain warmth into a silence that long. She can hold it for forty minutes on camera because she learned it in a house where nothing came back, and the alternative was letting the room go quiet on a child who could not fill it himself.

The thing that reads as saccharine performance is a person who has trained herself never to be the one who stops first.

None of which makes the criticism wrong.

The sharpest version of it is institutional. The American Academy of Pediatrics keeps a guidance page for doctors titled "Watching Ms. Rachel: How To Advise Parents of Infants and Toddlers," updated June 2026. Before 18 to 24 months, it says, infants cannot learn from a screen the way they learn from the people in the room, and from around 15 months the biggest factor in whether a baby learns from media is a caregiver watching alongside and explaining it. It notes that no published research on her videos exists at all.

Hold that against the pause. A recording cannot stretch for a slower child, cannot register that an answer arrived, and runs identically for every child sitting in it, because it happened once, years ago, in a room none of them were in. So the pause was always aimed at two people. It is a demonstration, held long enough for the adult on the couch to see what waiting looks like and copy it, which is the same adult the AAP is trying to recruit.

Jerrica Sannes, an early childhood specialist who founded Raise Wildflowers, presses harder: Accurso deploys genuinely research-backed techniques to hold the attention of infants, and the research on screens for that age band has never pointed anywhere except away from them. Sannes says she does not consider herself a screen-time expert. She does not have to be for the objection to land.

A woman whose stated decision rule is "Is this helpful for kids?" has built a business that requires very young children to look at a screen, and her unimpeachable intentions are exactly why the question is hard. Dr. Sanam Hafeez, the neuropsychologist who reviewed the content for PureWow in 2025, endorsed the method and added the caveat anyway: "Keeping screen time balanced with other activities is important."

The most patient teacher on the internet is still a teacher who arrives through a rectangle.

## Ms. Rachel's Controversies: Jules, Pride, and Gaza

In February 2023 a TikTok creator posted a video objecting to Jules Hoffman, the singer-songwriter who performed on *Songs for Littles* and who uses they/them pronouns. The show had never run a segment about pronouns; contemporaneous reporting traced the claim to Hoffman's own TikTok account. Within days there were thousands of comments and an organized push to make #cancelmsrachel trend.

Accurso did not answer it at all. On February 27 she posted a video saying she was stepping away from TikTok, captioned: "Hurtful videos and comments, no matter how much attention they get, will not bring you want you want. Only love can do that." She did not name the campaign, name Jules, or use the word pronouns, and she was back in about a week. Someone in her care was being attacked, and the woman whose whole identity is care went quiet and posted about love.

By the summer of 2024 she was losing two different constituencies in the same six weeks. In May 2024 she started posting about children in Gaza, and the account that had been a neutral utility in every American household stopped being neutral. On June 1 she posted a 25-second Pride video: "Happy Pride to all of our wonderful families and friends." Matt Walsh and a run of conservative accounts called for a boycott. She had seen that one coming and said so inside the video itself, telling anyone about to announce they could no longer watch the show "no worries and much love your way."

What she has said about Gaza, in her words: "My deep care for children doesn't stop at any border." In January 2025, on Kfir Bibas's birthday, she posted the Israeli baby's photo and prayed that he, his brother Ariel, and their parents Yarden and Shiri would come home.

Asked on WBUR in 2025 where any of it comes from, she did not give a political answer. "I also pray every day," she said. "'How can I help children?' is just my prayer."

What has been said about her: the funding referral of April 2025. On November 30, 2025 the same group put her on a shortlist for "Antisemite of the Year." On December 8 she answered in a register she had never used before. The group, she wrote on Instagram, "should not be able to try to ruin people's lives, cause them to receive threats and need security." Then she stopped arguing the charge and named the cost: "This has taken such a toll on me and my family and all I've wanted was to help kids." In December 2025 the incoming mayor of New York named her to his inaugural committee, the first time her name attached to a politician rather than a child, and in January 2026 she apologized for liking an antisemitic Instagram comment, saying she had tapped like while trying to delete it.

A personality framework cannot explain why anyone holds a political conviction, and this one will not try. What it can explain is what happened on the other side of the screen.

The objection that does not run on hurt feelings is simple: a channel made for children too young to have a politics became a venue for the most contested foreign-policy fight in American life, and a parent who pressed play on a nursery rhyme did not sign up for that. The inaugural committee makes it harder to wave away.

For five years, up to the spring of 2024, millions of parents handed her their toddler's morning on an unspoken premise: that this woman's warmth was for their kid. She never made that promise. The sentence underneath every episode is that a child is a child, which is the same sentence whether the child is in Ohio or Rafah. When she said it without a border, a large share of her audience experienced it as a betrayal, and what they had actually discovered is that the care they consumed had never been addressed to them in particular. They had mistaken a disposition for a relationship.

What the fight costs her is easier to see. ## The Apology Ms. Rachel Owed No One

Time is what she gives everyone else.

On January 9, 2025, she uploaded a video. Then she went quiet.

Weeks passed, then more weeks, and parents on the internet speculated. On March 21 she finally addressed it in an Instagram comment.

"I'm sorry. We've had some family things to attend to."

Her daughter Susannah was announced on April 8, born via surrogate. Accurso said publicly that she had not been able to carry the pregnancy for medical reasons, and said nothing else about it. Her son was seven. She had a business, a Netflix launch, and two years of the worst public accusations of her life behind her. And what she led with, to an audience of toddlers who cannot read and parents owed nothing, was an apology for taking a break.

The 2023 disappearance was a harassment campaign. This one was a baby. What the two have in common is only the return: after the Jules campaign she came back with a caption reading "Love > fear," and this time she opened with "I'm sorry."

This is the part of the pattern that does not [photograph well under pressure](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-types-in-stress). The care is real and the exhaustion is real. What sits underneath is not a missing permission slip. Being the one who supplies and never requires is the identity, so two months away does not register as rest taken. It registers as supply withheld, and debts get apologized for.

## Ms. Rachel's Emmy Loss and the Suitcase She Took to Congress

In March 2026 she went to the Children's and Family Emmys with the first two nominations of her career and lost Outstanding Preschool, Children's or Family Viewing Series to *Sesame Street*.

There is something almost too neat about that. The successor loses to the institution. She had spent years being called the Mister Rogers of her era, and the Academy handed the statue to the show that has been doing it since 1969. She had already refused the compliment. "I can't accept the Mister Rogers comparison," she posted in July 2025, "because he's my hero and a saint to me."

Three months later she was wheeling a black suitcase through the Senate office buildings. The Washington Post counted 535 packets of letters and drawings from children in immigration detention, delivered over two days to about ten Senate offices and House members of both parties, with a petition to close the Dilley processing center carrying 324,000 signatures.

Losing an award is the kind of thing a career answers with a campaign. She answered it with somebody else's children.

## Nobody Is Holding the Trolley for Ms. Rachel

A little girl in Springvale, Maine, once crossed a living room and put her body in front of a television, arms out, to stop a toy trolley from leaving.

She lost. The trolley left because the show was over, and shows are over. That is the part a small child cannot accept and every adult already knows.

Then she grew up and built the thing that does not end. A queue that does not run out at bedtime, or on a bad week, or when the woman making it has lost her voice. No child watching her has to get out of the chair and stop anything.

Nobody built her one. The queue never runs out for anyone except the woman who keeps filling it, and whether it has ever occurred to her to want one of her own is not something the record answers.

She is still standing in front of the glass with her arms out. She is just on the other side of it.
