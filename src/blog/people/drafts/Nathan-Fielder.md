## What is Nathan Fielder's personality type?

### Nathan Fielder is an Enneagram Type 6

Nathan Fielder's personality type is [Enneagram Type 6](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6), known as the Loyalist. Sixes are in the head triad, and their core emotion is fear.

The head triad means he meets the world by thinking it through first, rather than going on gut instinct or on how he feels. Business plans. Flowcharts of every way a conversation could go. A replica of the room before he walks into the real one.

Fear, as opposed to anger or shame, is what drives the thinking. The quiet kind: what if this goes wrong, what if they don't take me seriously, what if I'm being played. A critic found the same emotion in his work without the Enneagram. In The New York Review of Books in December 2022, Gabriel Winslow-Yost wrote that the show "is about the fear of fatherhood and of other people," and [named the emotion driving the show](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/2022/12/22/no-surprises-the-rehearsal-nathan-fielder/): "fear."

The Six pattern runs as a loop. Sixes picture the bad version before it happens. They don't fully trust their own judgment, so they look for something outside themselves to lean on: a plan, a credential, an expert, someone who will tell them it's fine. Then they test that too. And once a Six finally trusts someone, the same vigilance turns protective. That is where the name Loyalist comes from.

Fielder has described the loop himself. He told [WIRED](https://www.wired.com/story/nathan-fielder-tells-us-everything/) in 2026 that his "tendency that I have to want to, like, plan" helped inspire _The Rehearsal_. And at the New York Film Festival on September 30, 2026, as [Variety reported](https://variety.com/2026/film/news/nathan-fielder-elizabeth-holmes-you-can-see-everything-liar-1236893716/), he described meeting Holmes:

> "When I met her, I remember having this weird experience where she seems so genuine at times. But then I'm like, 'no,' and clocking and tracking my thoughts and being like, 'Okay, I know she's a liar.'"

Trust, then suspicion of the trust, then a check against what he already knew. Most people meeting a convicted fraudster would be wary. What is specific to him is that he was watching his own thoughts do it. He told WIRED in 2026 that he "kept having this thing where I was tracking my thoughts about how honest she was being."

## Why not Type 5?

The strongest argument against this read is [Type 5](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-5), the Investigator. The flat affect. The research. The watchfulness. His own account of struggling with conversation that has no purpose. Emma Stone called him "an observer of human behavior" in a [2023 Time tribute](https://time.com/collections/100-most-influential-people-2023/6269978/nathan-fielder/), which is about as Five as a sentence gets.

He even has a Five's origin story. He told WIRED that in grade six or seven a tutor guessed he knew fewer than 200 words, and he started naming words to prove otherwise, "because I was insecure that I didn't know as much as others or something." Fives fear not knowing enough. In the same answer he said not knowing is "probably why I hate you asking me questions," which is a Five guarding his own read.

The difference is where he takes the doubt once he has it. A Five's answer to not knowing is to withdraw and study until competent, alone. On the Holmes shoot, Fielder did the opposite. He moved into the subject's guest house. He put himself on camera after planning not to. And at the end of each day, he told WIRED, he checked his read against the crew's, in what he called "processing sessions," where he would arrive with what he thought was "a very clear idea of what was happening" and find that everyone else had seen something different. Those sessions, he said, were one reason they thought there was a film at all.

Asked by [W Magazine](https://www.wmagazine.com/culture/nathan-fielder-cover-interview-you-can-see-everything) in October 2026 what age he feels ("Twenty-two"), he added:

> "I always feel that everyone knows more than I do. So it's very easy for me to ask people questions."

A Five guards their energy from other people. A Six goes looking for other people's read when their own feels shaky, which is what those end-of-day sessions were.

## Nathan Fielder's Vancouver childhood, a magic shop and a business degree

He was born in Vancouver on May 12, 1983. His parents, Deb and Eric Fielder, were social workers.

Two social workers raising a boy who grew up to build elaborate interventions for strangers makes a tidy story. Nothing on the record ties their work to his.

From 13 to 20, he told W Magazine, he worked at a magic shop and did magic at birthday parties and bar mitzvahs. He quit because "if you don't stop, people will forever say to you, 'Show me a trick.'"

Then the degree. He took a Bachelor of Commerce at the University of Victoria, class of 2005, per the [university's alumni record](https://www.uvic.ca/alumni/read-and-explore/news-and-stories/feature-stories/2025-alumni-in-news.php), before Humber College's comedy program in Toronto. When _Nathan for You_ arrived on Comedy Central in 2013, its premise leaned on a credential fans often take for part of the joke: a real business graduate offering genuine strategy to struggling businesses. The strategy just happened to be deranged, like a frozen yogurt shop selling a poo-flavored product.

## Is Nathan Fielder's awkward persona real?

_The Rehearsal_ (HBO, 2022 and 2025) pointed the _Nathan for You_ apparatus at private life: people practicing a hard conversation on a full-scale replica of the room, with actors standing in for the people they love. _The Curse_ (2023), co-created with Safdie, cast Fielder and [Emma Stone](/personality-analysis/emma-stone) as a couple whose home-renovation show and marriage rot together. In the first two he performs under his own name, playing a version of himself that people cannot agree is a version. The honest answer is that the question is built wrong.

He [told GQ in 2014](https://www.gq.com/story/nathan-fielder-dumb-starbucks) that the show is "so much based on who I am really, and I just kind of exaggerate things." In a [second GQ interview](https://www.gq.com/story/nathan-for-you-season-3-qa) a year later, he guessed that the hardest part socially is "when there's nothing to actually talk about," and that a business pitch gives a conversation something to hold onto.

After an answer he worried hadn't made sense, he told WIRED's Katie Drummond:

> "This is not my skill set, like articulating with words, as a verbal storyteller."

People also ask whether he is autistic. He has never said so. [Rolling Stone reported in 2017](https://www.rollingstone.com/tv/tv-features/nathan-fielder-how-the-cult-comedian-rules-the-outer-limits-of-awkward-203950/) that he researched Asperger's syndrome while fine-tuning his TV persona but rejected the idea that the character is on the spectrum: "There's a lot of social disconnects that people experience all the time that have nothing to do with autism or anything." Asked whether he had ever wondered about a developmental disorder himself, he answered, "Please don't tell me this is the angle of your piece."

The people who work with him describe the opposite of a man caught unprepared. Emma Stone, who acted opposite him and was directed by him, called him "obsessively detail-oriented, but never loses sight of the big picture" in the same Time tribute.

Here is our reading: his social difficulty and his preparation are not layers you can peel apart. A man who has planned a conversation to the third decimal place is sharing a room with someone who has not. The gap between his preparation and everyone else's is itself the awkwardness. The preparation helps produce the encounter, and then the camera finds it.

The persona is the truth photographed at an angle he chose.

## Why Nathan Fielder got a real commercial pilot's license

That angle shows plainest in aviation. For the second season of _The Rehearsal_, in 2025, he aimed the apparatus at commercial aviation and trained as a pilot to make it. At a [June 2025 Q&A](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=70xdc3T3oqk) moderated by [Jack Black](/personality-analysis/jack-black), he gave two reasons.

He wanted to understand the work. And from the start, he assumed nobody would take a comedian's aviation investigation seriously, and that pilots would think it wasn't a real inquiry. He named the feeling:

> "I felt insecure."

<div class="iframe-container">
<iframe width="560" height="315" loading="lazy" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/70xdc3T3oqk?start=1110&amp;end=1190" title="Nathan Fielder on why he got a pilot's license, Q&amp;A with Jack Black, June 2025 (18:30)" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; web-share" referrerpolicy="strict-origin-when-cross-origin" allowfullscreen></iframe>
</div>

The license would let him tell a pilot, in the middle of a conversation, "hey, I'm a pilot, too." He did not rank the two motives.

Only the second speaks to type. His format already needs real credentials, so the license alone proves little. The insecurity is the evidence. When a Six feels exposed, credentials and proximity to competence start working as proof of safety: if I hold their qualification, they can't dismiss me. That is the Six under stress.

The season finale, which aired in May 2025, showed him flying a real Boeing 737 out of San Bernardino and back. On [CNN's _The Situation Room_ that month](https://transcripts.cnn.com/show/sitroom/date/2025-05-29/segment/02), he argued that communication between pilots needed more attention. John Goglia, a former NTSB member who appears in the season, called it "a little sliver that has fallen through the cracks," while crediting existing crew resource management training. The FAA told CNN it already required interpersonal training and wasn't seeing data to support the show's central claim. Fielder's reply, on air: "That's dumb. They're dumb."

None of this makes him right about aviation.

## The people inside Nathan Fielder's rehearsals

The serious objection to Fielder is that other people are inside the machine and did not draw the blueprints.

Daniel D'Addario made the case in [Variety in July 2022](https://au.variety.com/2022/tv/reviews/nathan-fielder-the-rehearsal-review-hbo-4203/), arguing that _The Rehearsal_ is "openly derisive of the people Fielder meets," above all Angela, a participant rehearsing motherhood, whose beliefs he argued made her easy to score points off.

The show's own answer is stranger than either side. The first episode ends with a verdict on Fielder: he is "an awful, awful person." The speaker is an actor Fielder hired to play Kor Skeete, the episode's participant, inside a rehearsal of the show itself. So the harshest thing said about Fielder in the premiere is a line he commissioned, staged and edited in. Whether that is accountability or the most elegant available way of avoiding it is the question the show declines to settle.

The real Skeete later interviewed Fielder for [GQ in December 2022](https://www.gq.com/story/gq-hype-nathan-fielder-men-of-the-year-2022). Of _Nathan for You_, he said he doesn't "like shows that make people look a little foolish," but that it "takes a great deal of talent." On _The Rehearsal_ he was warier: "I'm getting used to it." Fielder fished for a number ("Like seven out of 10?"), got an eight, and Skeete started a sentence he never finished: "I'm still conflicted about certain things, about the episode, the idea"

Fielder cut in: "Eight out of 10?" The sentence stays unfinished.

There is a more sympathetic reading of the same apparatus. Given his own account of struggling with unstructured contact, a rehearsal may be an offer of the method he finds reassuring: a script, a stated objective, another chance. The replica bar promises that nobody has to walk in cold. That makes the impulse understandable. It clears nothing about the method, and a participant still has to live with the encounter and the edit.

## How Nathan Fielder ended up living with Elizabeth Holmes

The film began with a magic trick. In 2023 Fielder took the documentary director Lance Oppenheim to the Magic Castle in Los Angeles, where he is a member, and a magician asked for a celebrity's name. Someone shouted "Elizabeth Holmes," Oppenheim told W Magazine. Holmes and her partner, Billy Evans, had already pitched A24 on documenting their life, and the talks had gone nowhere. Fielder called A24 and asked, as he put it to WIRED, "Well, could I meet her?"

Filming began 34 days before Holmes reported to federal prison for defrauding Theranos investors. About 20 days in, [the Associated Press reported](https://abcnews.com/Entertainment/wireStory/movie-review-shows-elizabeth-holmes-nathan-fielders-singular-137265447), he accepted their invitation to stay, in a guest house at the couple's beachside home in Del Mar, California. The crew worked up to 17 hours a day, W Magazine reported. He described the camera plan to WIRED: one wide lens and two long zooms at the far ends of the house, placed so that "if anything happened at any point in the house," they would catch it. He had planned to stay off camera himself. He ended up on it because the couple kept turning to him ("Nathan, what do you think?"), and because when something sounded like propaganda, he told WIRED, "someone has to push back."

In the teaser he asks her, "You're being real right now?" It is the question the internet asks about him. Her answer: "I'm always being real."

One turning point, he says, came when he asked her about honesty. Holmes told him she had never lied in her life, W Magazine reported. (Two critics, The New Yorker's Richard Brody and NPR's Linda Holmes, say that when pressed she allowed for white lies.) Fielder:

> "When she said she's never lied, she widened the gap so much. I realized there's more going on here than I thought."

What he expected to find is more revealing. "I guess I expected a little more outward anxiety and a desire to control," he told WIRED. She did not come across as controlling. She let the crew in without asking what their angle was. That unsettled him more:

> "Is that intentional? Or is there some design to this whole thing that I'm not aware of, and I'm in some hall of mirrors that I am not even conscious of?"

Ordinary wariness relaxes when the warning signs don't show up. A Six's vigilance does something else: the missing signs became the warning. Our read: he expected her to look like the anxious, controlling side of his own method, and when she didn't, he suspected a version of it he couldn't see.

"I felt conflicted at times, which was new for me," he told W Magazine. Elsewhere in the same interview: "In my life, I've never been so confused by a person."

## Did Elizabeth Holmes fool Nathan Fielder?

Not about the fraud. "In my mind, she was guilty," he told W Magazine of where he started, and he says he told her right away that he believed everything he had read about her. Whether she got to him as a person is where critics split.

When Holmes explains away the Theranos device, [Variety's Owen Gleiberman](https://variety.com/2026/film/reviews/nathan-fielder-surprise-film-telluride-elizabeth-holmes-1236853513/) wrote, Fielder "is not taken in by any of this." The [San Francisco Chronicle's Mick LaSalle](https://www.sfchronicle.com/entertainment/movies-tv/article/you-can-see-everything-review-22467557.php) watched the whole film and saw something else: "His edge goes missing." Fielder, who did magic from age 13 to 20, told WIRED that being around her "felt like watching a magician."

The second half of the film is where the question gets sharp (light spoilers from here). Fielder restages conversations with Holmes that were never filmed, with an actress playing her, and more than once the restagings tilt her way. Of the closing prison-visit scene, [Den of Geek's David Crow](https://www.denofgeek.com/movies/you-can-see-everything-ending-nathan-fielder-spoiler-amanda-seyfried/) wrote that "each time, though, Fielder ultimately takes the most positive and charitable spin on Holmes' responses." [Slate reported](https://slate.com/culture/2026/10/you-can-see-everything-elizabeth-holmes-nathan-fielder-documentary.html) that he rewrites one line because it makes Holmes look like a calculated manipulator. When the actress calls him "protective," as [Brody](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/10/19/you-can-see-everything-movie-review) described the scene, his answer, as [The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2026/10/23/you-can-see-everything/you-can-see-everything-language-elizabeth-holmes-nathan-fielder) quoted it, is "I'm just trying to be accurate."

The accusation is the "awful, awful person" move again: he scripted the criticism of himself and kept it in. The rewrite is harder to stage, and it is the Loyalist turn the loop predicts. Once a Six lets someone in, the vigilance that scanned for the lie starts guarding the person. Plenty of documentarians grow protective of their subjects, so this supports the read rather than proving it.

The skeptics have a strong case. [NPR's Linda Holmes](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/16/nx-s1-6001834/elizabeth-holmes-nathan-fielder-you-can-see-everything) wrote that Fielder "seemingly chooses to trust his gut and his own conversations with her over all else." The Theranos whistleblower Tyler Shultz, writing in [STAT](https://www.statnews.com/2026/10/19/elizabeth-holmes-theranos-whistleblower-tyler-shultz-insider-review/) on October 19, called it art, and added: "What it certainly is not is a documentary, and it's dangerous to pretend that it is."

Shultz also gave him this:

> "In Fielder's defense, he does cue the audience, sometimes subtly, sometimes not, that he is a compromised subject of his own documentary and may have omitted things in order to protect Elizabeth."

A jury settled whether she defrauded investors. What he couldn't settle is what goes on in her head. "Is it a lie if she believes it to be true?" he asked WIRED. His doubt kept that question open for as long as she kept talking. A Six's doubt is a defense against being fooled. Its cost is that it can hold a question open long after other people have closed it.

This complicates the read, and it should stay complicated. Most projects, he told WIRED, start with a pitch and "a plan to get the money." This one started with neither, only the exit clause. Asked whether he worried the couple were ahead of him, he said, "I didn't worry," and he told W that feeling conflicted was new for him. A Six is supposed to live in ambivalence. Yet the same man wondered aloud about a hall of mirrors. Our read: the fear moved, from what she could do to the film to whether he could see her at all. A skeptic can fairly call that the type bending.

What would change our minds: sustained evidence that he prepares as hard for things nobody will ever see or grade, with no audience to be credible to, would push this toward Type 5 or 1. So far the preparation keeps pointing outward, at a room.

Beyond Type 5, the rival worth naming is Type 9. A viewer might raise it, given the charitable, unworried Fielder of the film's second half. But a Nine smooths conflict over, and Fielder walks into it, films it and keeps it in the edit.

## What Nathan Fielder still can't make sense of

At the Telluride premiere on September 6, 2026, he told the audience, [per the Los Angeles Times](https://www.yahoo.com/entertainment/movies/articles/happened-inside-tellurides-secret-screening-070018247.html), "I really am struggling to this day to understand exactly what I experienced in this." He was excited, he said, "for this movie to come out and for people to help me understand what I experienced," [Vanity Fair reported](https://www.vanityfair.com/story/nathan-fielder-elizabeth-holmes-doc-secret-screening-telluride).

Then, the week before the film opened nationwide, his phone showed two missed calls from a prison. A third came in from the same number, and he picked up. It was Holmes, who had not called him in over six months. Near the end she said, "I trust you." They talked a little longer, about her kids, and as the call ended she said it again.

"And I don't know what she meant by that," he told [WIRED](https://www.wired.com/story/nathan-fielder-tells-us-everything/). He said he didn't have the words to even speculate. Then he described his own mind:

> "I don't want to create too much meaning to any given moment, but then my mind does try to figure those things out."

The Westwood story ends with an answer he could buy for twenty dollars. This one has no price. A woman who told him she had never lied told a man who cannot stop checking that she trusts him, twice, and left him to work out what that was.

"It hasn't ended, I guess," he told WIRED, in October 2026.
