<p class="firstLetter">In April 2026, a man who had spent more than a decade teaching strangers to go easy on other people filmed himself sticking his tongue out at Ivanka Trump.</p>

The video is a split screen. On one half she is describing what she took from [Marcus Aurelius](/personality-analysis/marcus-aurelius). On the other half Ryan Holiday smirks, rolls his eyes, and at one point gives a thumbs down while she is still talking. Mediaite transcribed the whole thing in May 2026, including the objection underneath the smirk: "She's totally right. That's exactly what Marcus Aurelius said... she's just not living or acting in accordance with it in any way."

She is right. That is the part that bothers him.

Two years before that video he posted his most-quoted line from Book 5 of *Meditations* to the Daily Stoic account, in February 2024: "Be tolerant with others and strict with yourself." He wrote his own gloss underneath it: standards for yourself high enough that you have to stretch, standards for everyone else low enough that you are pleasantly surprised when they are met. The Daily Stoic, his own company, has put it flatly: projecting strict standards onto people who never agreed to follow them is a waste of time.

Then he spent a week projecting.

He broke his own rule, in public, on camera, and the internet told him so at length. The part worth understanding is why a man builds a thirteen-book career on a rule he cannot keep.

## What is Ryan Holiday's personality type?

### Ryan Holiday is an Enneagram Type 1

Start with the exercise, because it is the cleanest evidence in his life.

Holiday runs, swims, or bikes every day. He calls it "a very underrated philosophical hack": "having something that gets you a win every day." On the Diary of a CEO podcast in 2023 he described the win: "I'm not training for a marathon. I'm not trying to set any records. I'm trying to make a promise to myself and keep that promise. And it doesn't matter how badly the writing goes, it doesn't matter what news I just got from my agent, it doesn't matter how my business is doing."

Read what he threw away there. No distance. No time. No record. A win with no opponent, no score, and no way for anyone else to place it. He stripped out every number that would let it be an achievement and kept a ruling. Each morning he assembles a small piece of evidence that he is still the kind of person who does what he said he would do. Then he goes to work, and by evening the evidence has expired, and he has to produce it again.

That is a [Type One](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-1) engine. The Achiever wants the record. The One wants the verdict.

His mentor described this out loud, to his face, and seems not to have realized he was doing it. In a filmed conversation about *The Laws of Human Nature*, [Robert Greene](/personality-analysis/robert-greene) reached for Holiday as his example of how everyone walks around carrying a self-opinion. He improvised Holiday's, and the testimony that fell out in 2018 is the most useful thing anyone has said about him: "You, Ryan, have an idea of yourself... I'm very self-reliant, I'm very disciplined, I'm a good person, I'm conscientious... I'm a good father."

Greene has known him since he was nineteen. Given a free hand to describe how Holiday sees himself, he reached for *conscientious* and *a good person*, and nothing in the list is about being ambitious or brilliant or successful. The man who spent his twenties manufacturing publicity carries a self-image built entirely out of moral vocabulary.

Then the shape of the work itself. Between 2021 and 2025 he published four virtue books, one for each of the cardinal virtues: *Courage Is Calling*, *Discipline Is Destiny*, *Right Thing, Right Now*, *Wisdom Takes Work*. Thirteen books in thirteen years, and the culminating project was a report card with four categories, written by the student.

**A note on the obvious objection.** Holiday would find this page tedious, and his reasons are good ones. His entire position is that character is a practice, that what you are is the thing you keep doing. He said so mid-tirade in the April 2026 video. "Stoicism is not a brand," he told the camera. "It's a practice. And it demands honesty, it demands self-awareness, it demands accountability." That is a real argument with a site like this one. We agree with him about the practice, and we are describing which practice he cannot stop performing. Something is asking him for that swim every morning, and it is not a publisher.

## Why Ryan Holiday dropped out of college at 19

His father was a police detective. His mother was a high school principal.

Two parents, two careers, one shared job description: holding other people to a standard and noticing when they fall short of it. He has said what it transmitted. In an open letter to his father in October 2020, he quoted the lesson back to him: "You told me that as a kid! That the bad prevail when good people do nothing."

He lasted less than two years at UC Riverside before quitting in his sophomore year, moving to Los Angeles, and talking his way into working for Robert Greene, author of *The 48 Laws of Power*. On its face this is the story of a kid escaping the rules.

Listen to how he described it in 2012, though, on Chase Jarvis's show, back when *Trust Me, I'm Lying* was his only book and he had no reputation to protect. "I look at my education as having really started the day I dropped out of school, cuz now it was on me. It was my full-time job and no one was going to demand that I turn in papers... It wasn't, you know, a 9:00 a.m. class and then a noon class and then a 3 p.m. class. It was all the time, every day."

He is describing an upgrade in supervision. School demanded papers on a schedule, which meant there were hours when nothing was being demanded. He left, and the demand became continuous.

This is the move the pattern makes when it works well. A One who resents an external authority rarely walks away from authority itself. They relocate it inward, where it can run without a bell schedule and without anyone to negotiate with. He did not flee the household of two enforcers. He dissolved the last one standing between him and the job.

He also gave the whole thing away in the same interview, in a sentence nobody picked up: "I'm good at quitting actually." He is, and he has quit everything except the demand.

## Two Sides: the essay Ryan Holiday wrote before anyone was reading

On July 5, 2011, a 24-year-old marketing director at American Apparel published a short essay on his personal blog. There was no Daily Stoic. No bookstore. No ranch. *Trust Me, I'm Lying* was a year from publication. He had no audience to manage and no brand to protect, so he wrote the truth, and then he left it online, where it has stayed ever since.

<p><b>Source:</b> "Two Sides," ryanholiday.net, July 5, 2011. Still live at the original URL as of August 2026.</p>
</div>

Read it as a document rather than a memory. Each line came first, and everything after it is the receipt.

> "I have always felt pulled in two conflicting directions. It has not been pleasant. It's made me feel miserable when I should feel happy, afraid when I should feel secure–a constant tension and uncertainty that takes an exhausting toll."

He is twenty-four and already exhausted. The phrase to hold onto is *when I should*. Even his description of his own misery is graded against a standard.

> "There is the part of me drawn to discipline. To work and do great things in this brief bit of time I have been given."

Eleven years later he published *Discipline Is Destiny*.

> "There is another part of me pulled towards recklessness. To simply live. To hate the things that most people seem to value."

This half rarely makes it into the public record. It is the half that dropped out, moved across the country, went to work for a clothing company famous for its sexualized billboards, and spent six years getting very good at lying to journalists.

> "The few moves I've made in my life that could be considered daring or even reckless–dropping out of school, packing up and moving across the country–were almost immediately overwhelmed by the responsible side."

*Almost immediately overwhelmed.* He knows the outcome of the fight. He has watched it resolve the same way every time.

> "All this is to say that there is a tension in me that I have trouble grappling with. It is the feeling of living in a house divided against itself."

And then the line that reorganizes everything he has published since:

> "And yet I think they are both equal parts fear, the fear of simply *just being*."

Eight years after writing that sentence, he published a book called *Stillness Is the Key*.

The man who would go on to sell the case for stillness to millions of readers told us, in advance and without being asked, what stillness did to him.

## Did Ryan Holiday quit manipulating the media, or repoint it?

The half he called reckless left a résumé, and by his own accounting it is bad. At American Apparel he manufactured outrage on purpose, planted stories with small blogs knowing larger outlets would pick them up without checking, wrote comments under invented names, and engineered controversy over his own client's ad campaigns. A One's anger tends to surface as its opposite, which is how a moral kid gets fluent at lying without revising his self-image. The enjoyment is what the type does not explain.

Then in 2012 he wrote a book explaining exactly how he had done it, mechanism by mechanism, and put the word *Confessions* in the subtitle.

A man who is simply good at publicity and wants to keep being good at it does not publish the manual. He keeps the edge. Holiday burned the edge in print at twenty-five, and when Chase Jarvis asked him about that subtitle in 2012 the answer arrived tangled. The book was "here's what I do and here's how you can do it." He declined to call the rest of it an apology. Then he landed on the phrase he wanted: "an admission of some guilt in some sense."

*An admission of some guilt.* He reached for that unprompted, while promoting a business book.

Which makes the swing from media manipulator to Stoicism's most successful evangelist stop looking like a swing. He had been keeping the ledger the entire time, and *Trust Me, I'm Lying* was the ledger going public. What that does not settle is the harder question: did he shut the machine down, or aim it at cargo he approves of?

*The Obstacle Is the Way* came out in 2014 and did not spread the way philosophy books spread. It spread through locker rooms. ESPN traced the route in a May 2020 feature: Mike Lombardi, a thirty-year NFL front-office executive coming off a season with the Patriots, handed the book to Seahawks general manager John Schneider at a pro day and told him it distilled the Patriot Way. Schneider passed it to Pete Carroll and the staff. "Soon, word gets out," ESPN wrote. "The two best teams in the league are reading the same book. Soon everyone else in the NFL is too." By August 2016 he was addressing the defending national champions at Alabama's football complex, with Nick Saban asking the first question.

A book about accepting what you cannot control became an operating manual for the most win-obsessed institutions in America.

One item keeps running into the question. In February 2018 he published *Conspiracy*, the story of [Peter Thiel](/personality-analysis/peter-thiel) secretly bankrolling Hulk Hogan's lawsuit until it put Gawker out of business, reported with the principals talking to him and told as tradecraft. The man who wrote the manual for manufacturing media outrage came back to the destruction of a media company as its chronicler.

That is the case for the prosecution and the case for the defense, and they are the same sentence. He learned exactly how attention gets manufactured, decided the machine was corrosive, and kept running it, at scale, for freight he believes in. Whether that counts as restitution or as laundering is not something the record settles, and he is still living inside the question.

## Why the Naval Academy canceled Ryan Holiday

The machine costs him, though, and the clearest bill came due in Annapolis. For four years running, Holiday had gone there to lecture midshipmen on the Stoic virtues. April 14, 2025 was to be the fifth, in front of the entire sophomore class, on the theme of wisdom.

His material centered on James Stockdale, the Naval Academy graduate whose Stoicism carried him through more than seven years as a prisoner of war, and whom the Navy sent to Stanford to read Marx and Lenin in the original, because wisdom requires exposure to the ideas you find repellent.

Roughly an hour before he was due on stage, the Academy called and asked him to drop the slide referencing the 381 books that Navy leadership had pulled from its Nimitz Library that month, complying with an order from Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth's office to strip out anything promoting diversity, equity and inclusion.

<div class="key-stat">
<span class="key-stat-number">381</span>
<span class="key-stat-label">books pulled from the Nimitz Library in April 2025. He was asked not to mention them, one hour before the lecture. Nearly all were back on the shelves within six weeks.</span>
</div>

He declined. The lecture was canceled, and so was a planned talk to the Navy football team, whose players had been reading his books for years, a sequence he laid out afterward in a *New York Times* op-ed. "The idea that there are topics that are off limits or that they can't handle is absurd on its face," he told ABC News afterward.

Jonathan Friedman, who directs PEN America's U.S. Free Expression programs, put the institutional version bluntly. "The Naval Academy is becoming known not only for banning books," he said, "but also for cancelling someone for planning to discuss the banning of books. This is a tragic new low."

Hold this episode next to the video that came a year later, because the same mechanism produced both. Something was wrong, in public, and Holiday could not agree to let it stand unremarked. In Annapolis that cost him the stage, and it read as courage, because a hard thing was done at real expense. The wiring does not distinguish between the two occasions.

## What Ryan Holiday does every morning before he writes

The rest of the time he holds, and the holding is daily work. He gets in the water. Nobody times him. Reconstructed, the logic of that morning runs something like this.

<p class="inner-thought">Nothing today has gone wrong yet. That is the problem with the morning. The record is clean, which means it is only available to be spoiled, and the swim is the one entry I can post before the day starts making its own case.</p>

The practice runs the same way. He writes his books on four-by-six note cards, thousands of them, the same system Robert Greene taught him at nineteen. He journals daily, and the questions he puts to the page are interrogations: "Why am I angry? What caused this? What can I do about it?... Why am I sad?"

Stephanie Ruhle asked him at the 92nd Street Y in December 2025 what he does with the pages. "Nothing," he said. "The page, doing them, is what I do." Does he keep them, then? "Yeah, I keep them because I struggle to throw anything away." Reading them back, he said, "is just extra to me."

He has built a business on the premise of letting go and cannot throw away a notebook. The pages are the record, and the record has to exist whether or not anyone consults it. Somewhere in Bastrop County, Texas, on forty acres with cattle and donkeys and goats, there is a growing archive of evidence in a case that will never come to trial.

The bookstore runs on the same rule. He and his wife Samantha put their life savings into a 140-year-old building on Main Street in Bastrop in 2021, and where a typical independent carries thousands of titles, The Painted Porch stocks what its site in 2026 called "a small collection of only our absolute favorite books." The policy was posted alongside it: "If it's on our shelves, it's because we read it and think that you should too. Period." A retailer in a business with almost no margin, refusing to sell anything he has not personally vetted.

### Ryan Holiday's Wing: 1w9

The Nine <a href="/enneagram-corner/enneagram-wings-complete-guide">wing</a> shows up in the geography. He left Los Angeles for forty acres outside Austin, filled them with livestock, and put his bookshop in a Texas town of roughly ten thousand people. His public affect is cool and unhurried, and his anger, when it surfaces, arrives chilled.

A 1w2 corrects you warmly, to your face, framed as help. Holiday's habitual mode is the essay, the newsletter, the book. He addresses the standard rather than the person, which is the Nine wing routing the correction away from direct confrontation and toward the system.

## The week Ryan Holiday could not be tolerant

The restraint he manages at home is the restraint that failed him in public. Back to the split screen, because this is where the sympathetic reading has to earn itself against the hostile one, and the hostile one is well argued.

Doug Bates, who writes the Stoicism newsletter *Ataraxia or Bust*, put it sharply in May 2026: "More than anyone else, Holiday has turned Stoicism into a brand," and "Holiday's real gripe with Ivanka appears to be that her admiration of Marcus Aurelius infringes upon that brand." The deeper problem Bates named is that the tradition Holiday represents is built on the discipline of leaving other people's choices alone.

Daily Stoic sells courses, a paid membership, journals, and memento mori medallions. One course is a ten-day guide to controlling your temper.

Concede the accurate parts, because most of them are accurate. He does run a brand. He projected a strict standard onto a person who never signed up for it, the specific error his own company has written newsletters warning against, and then he compounded it for days.

Now the part the hostile reading reaches without settling. Bates is describing what the anger is about, and territorial anger and sincere anger are the same anger, so the sincerity of the fury leaves his claim standing. What the video adds is a second act nobody has explained. Something in him gave, on camera, in a way he did not manage to edit out. Then he cut the two halves into a split screen, left the eye-rolls in, and posted it. Whatever went first, the publishing was a decision.

Here is what gave. For this pattern, an obvious wrong sitting in public with nobody naming it does not read as neutral. It reads as participation. Silence gets filed as endorsement, and endorsement gets filed as being the sort of person who lets things slide, which is the exact charge he has been defending himself against every morning in the pool since roughly 2011. Ivanka Trump quoting Marcus Aurelius put a wrong in front of him with a live camera on it. The composure he sells is real, and it is not native. It is a structure he built and maintains at cost, and that week the structure gave.

This does not make him right. He broke his own rule, and the rule was the good one. He has also passed this exact test before, on the record.

In March 2015 the *New York Times* ran an op-ed on Stoicism by Massimo Pigliucci, a philosophy professor at CUNY City College, and it climbed the site's most-emailed list. Holiday, who had by then made the subject his own, wrote down what he actually felt: "I also had an embarrassing human reaction: jealousy. Why did Professor Massimo Pigliucci get this opportunity and I didn't? Why are things so unfair?" Then he emailed the professor and published a long interview with him on his own site, because the article "almost certainly introduced tens of thousands of people to a topic I care about."

Nearly the same trigger both times. Someone else, in public, holding his philosophy. In 2015 he aimed the correction at himself and handed the other man a microphone. Eleven years later he stuck out his tongue. In between, he acquired a franchise to defend, which is what Bates says explains everything.

Which means the anger arrived first and the philosophy came second. Thirteen books of counsel about equanimity are not a report from a calm man. They are the working notes of someone who needed the equanimity badly enough to reverse-engineer it, write it down, and sell it to a list he built himself.
