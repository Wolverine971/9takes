<p class="firstLetter">There was an ambulance parked beside the soundstage, and it was there for her. Samara Weaving had the worst migraine of her life on the day Damien Chazelle shot her big scene in <i>Babylon</i>. She was throwing up. Someone put an IV in her arm. She sat in the back of the ambulance between setups, and when a crew member asked if she was ready, she said yes, walked onto the stage loaded with painkillers and steroids, and improvised a line Chazelle kept in the film.</p>

Here is the part she volunteers when she tells that story. The migraine helped.

"It kind of made me not nervous," she told Kevin McCarthy on _On Film_ in June 2026, "cuz all I had to do was like get through this scene."

She had taken anxiety medication for years. What settled her down was a medical emergency. The same thing had happened three years earlier on a different set, and it is why she has spent every year since screaming for a living.

## What is Samara Weaving's personality type?

### Samara Weaving is an Enneagram Type 6

The evidence is not subtle, because she narrates it herself. Asked on _Happy Sad Confused_ in 2019 when her anxiety started, she had no origin story: "I don't remember not being anxious, so I don't know." That is a baseline condition, reported without drama, as weather she was born into.

She built a professional filter out of it, and the filter is the [Type 6](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6) pattern in plain sight. "Especially choosing jobs," she told Kevin McCarthy on _On Film_ in June 2026, "I'm like, do I trust this filmmaker? Do I trust this person? Where I know that I'm going to work really hard, are they going to work just as hard?"

That is a hiring criterion. Most actors screen scripts; she screens people, deciding before she signs who will still be working as hard as she is at two in the morning on day twenty-six.

The second tell is what she says about the future. Talking to _Collider_ in June 2026, she named the strangest part of finally having leverage to say no: "Okay, let's just try and trust that something will happen. You will work again, which is hard to convince myself of, but it does pay off." She has three films out this year. She still has to argue herself into believing there will be a fourth.

The Type 9 reading has a case worth one paragraph. She is easygoing in interviews, allergic to friction, and told _The Independent_ in September 2025 that "fame wasn't something I was ever striving for. I wanted to be successful, but being really famous never appealed to me." But a Nine's peace comes from not looking too hard at what might go wrong. Weaving looks constantly. She audits directors, over-prepares to a fault, and reports that trusting the world takes conscious effort. The vigilance is the giveaway.

What the fear reading does not cover is the second half of her, and she has been saying that half out loud since she was eleven.

## Samara Weaving's childhood was four countries and no fixed address

She was two weeks old the first time she left the country she was born in. "I was born in Australia but I left it two weeks old and we grew up in Fiji, Singapore, Indonesia, a little bit in Italy, and then moved back to Australia," she told Josh Horowitz in 2019. Her father worked in film; her mother is a curator, hired by museums in whichever city came next.

The moving worked on her the way it works on a quiet kid: she stopped talking. "I wouldn't speak to other children for, you know, weeks," she told Horowitz.

A six-year-old walks into a classroom in a new country where every child already has a history with every other child.

<p class="inner-thought">There are eleven of them and one of me. They already know who sits where. If I say the wrong thing on the first day, that's who I am for the whole year. Better to find out how this place works first. I can start tomorrow. Or the day after.</p>

Her parents' fix was drama class at five. The goal was to get her to open up. What she found was a room where the words arrive in advance and somebody else is responsible for what happens next. "That's where I can just be a lunatic and don't feel judged," she told Horowitz in the same conversation.

That is the whole engine, installed at five. Give her the script and she will do anything in front of you. The Enneagram calls this the search for solid ground; Weaving's version is literal, since she grew up on ground that was scheduled to move.

The wanting came later, and not from the uncle everyone asks about. Her father had read the Lord of the Rings books aloud to her and her sister, so she sat down for the films expecting to be transported. "You're kind of lost in this beautiful magical world," she told McCarthy, "and then your uncle, silly uncle Hugo's got a wig on and pretending to speak Elvish, and you're just going, okay well I'm out now. This is so annoying."

What did it was a film with nobody she knew anywhere in it. She was eleven, in Malta, watching _Pirates of the Caribbean_, and she came out of the cinema angry.

"I left the theater and I was furious because I'd had almost a spiritual awakening," she told the _Tribune-Review_ in March 2026. "I had this feeling of, 'How dare they do this without me? I want to be Keira. I want to be Orlando Bloom. Why wasn't I invited?'"

That is not a frightened child looking for shelter. That is a child staking a claim. Both of them grew up, and any reading that tracks only the first one will keep getting her wrong.

The job came with a side effect nobody had prescribed.

## Why Samara Weaving stopped taking her anxiety medication

The line that made headlines in 2025 was cheerful and reckless. "Being able to scream and run and chase people and fight back kind of shook the anxiety out of me," she told _The Independent_ in September 2025. "After that, I noticed I didn't need my meds!"

The headline version leaves out what she made of it: "I was discovering this inner strength that I didn't realise I had."

It has happened twice on the record, both times reported with something close to surprise.

**Entry one. _Ready or Not_, shot late 2018.**
The job: a bride hunted through a mansion by her new in-laws. The work: screaming, sprinting, bleeding, fighting back, for a whole shoot.
The result: she came off the medication and stayed off it.
Her note on the mechanism: "Your body doesn't know that what you're doing is fake," she told _Coveteur_ in June 2026. "My limbic system has just been put through the ringer."

**Entry two. _Babylon_, shot 2021.**
The job: one high-pressure scene for Damien Chazelle.
The condition: the migraine, the IV, the ambulance idling off-stage.
The result: the nerves went quiet, for the reason she gave in the ambulance. One problem in front of her instead of all of them.

Both entries hold the fear constant. Weaving is frightened either way. What changes is the scope. Give her body one urgent, concrete, survivable problem and the diffuse background dread has nowhere left to live. She had spent years being told, in effect, that her alarm system was miscalibrated. Given a threat with an actual shape, it turned out to have a job.

Something else burns off in those shoots, and she names it exactly. "I find it quite strangely therapeutic," she told the _Tribune-Review_. "You get to do things that are so socially unacceptable, like scream and bash people up." By the time a horror shoot wraps, she said, "I don't have any anger left in me. It's all been burnt up."

Anger is the harder half. Fear she narrates, medicates and plans around; it is the subject of nearly every profile written about her. Aggression is the one she has no license for, because a Six who holds it becomes the thing in the room other people watch. Horror hands her a script that requires it and a crew that applauds when it lands. She goes home empty.

Anxiety [shows up differently in every type](/enneagram-corner/anxiety-and-enneagram-types-guide), and the version that runs on doubt is worst in the waiting room. Weaving found a career that is almost never the waiting room: a call sheet, a scene, a mark, a job for every pair of hands. It is also why she will not produce. She tried it once. "It's not for me," she said. Owning all the variables is the exact shape of the thing that keeps her awake.

A film wrap is not a treatment plan, and she has never claimed otherwise. What she reports is narrower, and it carries a bill. "Being terrified for weeks on end is just exhausting," she told _Coveteur_. "Being exhausted is useful for the character, but it's not useful for me."

### Samara Weaving's Wing: 6w7

Weaving reads as 6w7 with a wide wing. The core is fear management, loyalty and vetting; the outward strategy is comedy and a taste for the absurd. She gravitates to material that laughs at horror rather than wallowing in it. Radio Silence's note to her, in her retelling to <em>Collider</em>, was "You're a weird gal. Lean into the weirdness." A 6w5 in her position would retreat into technique. She retreats into being funny. See the <a href="/enneagram-corner/enneagram-wings-complete-guide">wings guide</a> for how the neighbors reshape a core type.

### Samara Weaving's Instinctual Subtype: sp/sx

Self-preservation leads. Her stated ambition has always been the working life rather than the visibility, and she says so in those terms. She married a writer she met on set, works repeatedly with the same directors, and describes the drought in survival economics rather than wounded ego. The sexual instinct runs second, in the intensity of her chosen pairs and the pull toward roles that put her body under real strain. The social instinct is the quiet one: the gumnut mafia functions as a small trusted unit rather than a network, and she does not work it. More in the <a href="/enneagram-corner/enneagram-instinctual-subtypes">instinctual subtypes guide</a>.

### Stress and Growth Arrows

The Six's <a href="/enneagram-corner/enneagram-connecting-lines">connecting lines</a> run to Three under pressure and to Nine in growth. The Three move is the decade she spent taking whatever was offered, which she summarizes as "anyone who wants to hire me, I'll do whatever." The Nine move is her recent vocabulary: show up, do the job, relinquish the rest. That is a Six arriving at settledness, and it belongs to the last two years rather than the last ten.

### Counterarguments: Why Samara Weaving Might Not Be Type 6

Type 9 is the strongest alternate, and the main body handles it: Nines achieve peace by declining to look, and Weaving audits every collaborator before she signs.

Type 4 gets raised because of the genre affinity and the outsider childhood. It does not survive contact with her own material: she has no romance about her suffering and no investment in being singular.

The honest limit of the type is not a rival type. It is appetite, and the vocabulary gives it away: powerful, autonomy, extremes, morbidly obsessed. No fear-management model predicts a woman who selects for the least stable job in the room. The _Azrael_ section below makes the case.

## The horror movie where Samara Weaving does not speak

Even so, she kept choosing the hardest available version of the job.

In March 2024, at SXSW, she premiered a film in which she never says a word.

_Azrael_, directed by E.L. Katz and written by Simon Barrett, is set in a world where nobody speaks. Weaving plays a woman escaping a devout community that intends to sacrifice her, hunted through the forests of Estonia, carrying the whole thing on her face and her body. It sits at 71% on Rotten Tomatoes, most reviews pointing at her performance as the reason to watch.

The girl who walked into each new school and said nothing to anyone for weeks grew up to headline a movie where speaking is not available and silence is the whole discipline.

It cuts against her method, too. Everything Weaving says about surviving a set runs through preparation: know the lines, know the accent, leave nothing to chance. _Azrael_ removed the thing she prepares. "At first it was scary because I thought, 'How am I going to communicate with an audience using just my face and limbs?'" she told _Screen Rant_ at the festival. "Instead of learning lines, I had to learn so much choreography."

She took it anyway, and the reason she gave _Screen Rant_ is the most useful sentence on record about how she decides.

"I was really afraid, which is a good sign for me to do something. Usually if I'm like, 'Oh, yeah, I can do this,' I shouldn't do it."

That is fear used as a compass, pointed at whatever she is most likely to fail at. Two years later, in plainer terms: "When I read a script and I get nervous because it's something I haven't done before or it feels challenging, I want to do it," she told _Coveteur_. "If I'm doing the things that I've done before, it's just not as fun at work."

The security model explains why she vets people before signing. It does not explain why someone organized around making the ground stop moving would go hunting for the least stable footing on offer.

What she describes is appetite, and she is specific about it. Horror, she told _Screen Rant_ at the same festival, is "where you can be one of the guys. I love that; it makes me feel really powerful, and I have autonomy over it." In 2026 she named the ceiling it breaks: "It's the only genre where you can really explore those extremes of the human experience," she told _The Wrap_ in April 2026, "unless you're a man, and they put you in a World War movie. As a woman, that's not going around."

Powerful. Autonomy. Extremes. Nobody managing a fear talks like that. Those are the words of someone who found the one room where the quiet kid gets to be dangerous, and who has not been able to leave it since. Ask what she actually wants and the answer is the film nobody sends her. Women in war movies. "I'm, like, morbidly obsessed with it," she told the _Tribune-Review_.

Off camera the older pattern still runs. "I think a lot of introverts have to sort of copy what extroverts do in order to fit in, survive, get jobs," she told _Collider_ in March 2026. The easy public affect is a job requirement she taught herself. _Azrael_ is the one where she did not have to perform it.

## The two years after Home and Away when nobody called

None of that machinery was there at the start.

She started working at fourteen and got the first two jobs she auditioned for: _Out of the Blue_, then _Home and Away_, where she played Indi Walker from 2009 to 2013 while still at school. She drew the obvious conclusion. "I think I had a very naive perception of how the industry works," she told _New Idea_ in 2021. "Oh, I got the two jobs I auditioned for. This is easy."

Then she left Summer Bay, and the phone stopped. "I didn't work for nearly two years," she said. "It was a real reality check in terms of the life of an actor."

For someone organized around the possibility that the ground stops being ground, two years is not a setback. It is proof. She had been handed a world where effort produces work, the world withdrew it, and the lesson she took was arithmetic, delivered in the same interview: "In the entirety of SAG, 1% of actors are working. It's hard."

The posture that came out of it lasted a decade. "When you're starting out, you're going, 'Anyone who wants to hire me, I'll do whatever,'" she told _Collider_ in June 2026.

The drought is doing quiet work in every decision she has made since. It explains the over-preparation, which she still describes thirteen years later in the language of blame avoidance. "I hate being underprepared. I hate trying to read the scene on the way to work and being stressed that I don't know my lines," she told _Collider_ in March 2026. Then, unprompted, she names the cause: "I never want to feel that ever again, so now I'm obsessively the other way." It explains why she has to talk herself into believing there will be another job. And it explains the move to Los Angeles in her mid-twenties, which she now describes with retrospective bewilderment.

"I'm very grateful for my 20-year-old brain that was like, I can do this, let's move, let's go!" she told _The Independent_. "I don't understand how brazen I was."

She is envying a version of herself who had not yet collected enough information to be frightened, and who got on the plane before anyone could hand her any.

What she assembled once she landed was small and closed and Australian. "I think we're called the gumnut mafia," she told _The Independent_, naming her circle after the expat pack around Olivia Newton-John in eighties Los Angeles. Hers runs to Teresa Palmer, Phoebe Tonkin and Lara Worthington. "I'm very grateful for all my ding-a-ling girls out here." People from the same island who already know what the drought feels like.

In late May 2026 she and Warden had their first child. She has kept nearly everything private, including the name, though the pronoun escaped to _Coveteur_ in June: "She's number one. As long as she's fine, everything's fine."

"It's the most magical. It's the most terrifying, most insane experience," she told the same magazine. "My heart's on the outside now."

Magical and terrifying, in that order, in the same breath. It is the pair she has used about her work for a decade, arriving now in the one part of her life she cannot prep for. The woman raised on ground that was scheduled to move is the one holding it still for someone else.

## Why Samara Weaving keeps making the same movie

That instinct to keep the circle small runs her career too.

In March 2026 she released _Ready or Not 2: Here I Come_, in which she again spends a night hunted by wealthy people and again screams a great deal. It landed at 73% on Rotten Tomatoes, down from the original's 89%.

The sharpest criticism went straight at the repetition. "Samara Weaving's guttural screams can cover a multitude of sins," wrote Zachary Lee in _The Wrap_, "but even her fully embodied performance and powers can't save a movie that mistakes stilted recurrence for high-octane throwback."

He is right about the film. The charge underneath it follows her everywhere: one register, endlessly returned to, a career built out of a single sustained scream.

She takes the label without argument. Asked at SXSW whether she minds being called a scream queen, she told _Den of Geek_ in March 2024: "Yeah, why not? 'Scream Queen' is great. I'll take it!"

What she does not accept is that she keeps choosing it. She has been trying to quit the genre for years, out loud, on the record. "I keep telling my team, 'I'm not doing any more genre movies,'" she told _Collider_ in April 2026. The same month, to _The Wrap_: "Okay, maybe I'm going to take a break from horror, but then something will come along, and I go, 'Damn it!'"

That is not brand management. That is a woman announcing an exit and discovering, every time the script lands, that she does not want to take it.

Part of what keeps her is arithmetic she names herself. "I think there's a reason why people work together a lot, because it's such an intense experience, filmmaking," she told _The Wrap_. Weaving picks jobs by asking whether she can trust the people running them, and that question takes a full production to answer honestly. Once a crew has answered it, that room joins a very short list where she already knows the floor holds. Matt Bettinelli-Olpin and Tyler Gillett answered it in 2018. Her husband, Jimmy Warden, answered it earlier, on _The Babysitter_, where they met, and later wrote her a lead and directed it himself. "I wrote it with her in mind," Warden told _People_ in 2025. "I put all of her into all of these scenes when they were conceived."

Going back to those rooms is what loyalty looks like when trust is scarce and slow to earn. The rooms that passed the audit are her whole inventory, and she will not burn one to prove she has range.

She is spending that inventory down on purpose now. Her rule, as told to _Collider_ in June 2026: anything short of a "fuck yes" gets a "fuck no." What she is correcting is specific. "I think before I could talk myself into a role or I could get convinced by other people, or I'd try and tell myself, 'This is good for me,' instead of sticking with my gut." She spent a decade handing the decision to whoever in the room sounded certain. The rule is a Six taking her own authority back.

The people in those rooms describe her as what holds the production together. "The linchpin that holds it all together is Samara," Bettinelli-Olpin told _Den of Geek_ in March 2026. Kathryn Newton, playing opposite her, put it in the terms that matter to Weaving: "She was so good to me. And it's her movie. It's her franchise, and she never made me feel like I had to not take up space," Newton told _Bleeding Cool_ that same month.

The woman who spent weeks not speaking at new schools now specializes in making the new person in the room comfortable.

And the fear she is actually managing is not the one on the poster. The scariest job of her career, by her own account, was a love story. "It's just the most vulnerable character. I haven't done a love story before, and so that was really scary in its own right," she said of _Carolina Caroline_ to _Screen Rant_ in June 2026. "It's scarier than just being like, 'Yeah, I'm going to run around and fight people.'" What got her through was the same instrument as always: "It was having to trust Kyle." A machete she can rehearse. Being known is the part with no choreography.

Then, in the last week of July 2026, the pattern broke. _Deadline_ and _Variety_ both reported that Marvel had cast her as Emma Frost in Jake Schreier's _X-Men_. Per the trades, finalists tested just after the July 4th weekend and her read was too hard to beat. Marvel declined to comment. She has said nothing publicly.

None of that job is available to the audit. You cannot interview a room that has not been assembled yet, and the fame she says never appealed to her is not an optional feature of playing a mutant in that franchise. It is the terms.

So the trust-inventory reading, honest as it is about the first fifteen years, does not cover the next one. The other engine does. Emma Frost walks into every room as the most dangerous mind in it, which is exactly what horror has been paying Weaving in for a decade, scaled up until nobody can miss it. Whether a woman who needs to know the floor holds can work on a floor nobody will let her inspect is now the open question of her career. The film is expected in 2028.

## The photograph that isn't Samara Weaving

About ten years before Marvel called, in an airport, a stranger walked up to Samara Weaving and told her she was [Margot Robbie](/personality-analysis/margot-robbie).

She could have said no. It would have taken a second and cost nothing.

"I just couldn't break it to them that I wasn't," she told _People_ in May 2026, "so I posed for a photo with the fan, saying that I was Margot."

She has thought about it since. She is not sure the fan ever worked it out. "They might have looked at it later and been like, that's not… that's some random lady."

Somewhere there is a phone, or a hard drive, or a shoebox, holding a photograph of Samara Weaving smiling next to a happy stranger, filed under another woman's name. She let it happen because in the half-second available to her, giving away her own name was the cheaper thing to do.

Ten years on, audiences buy tickets to watch her refuse to hand over anything at all.
