<p class="firstLetter">There was an ambulance parked beside the soundstage, and it was there for her. Samara Weaving had the worst migraine of her life on the day Damien Chazelle shot her big scene in <i>Babylon</i>. She was throwing up. Someone put an IV in her arm. She sat in the back of the ambulance between setups, and when a crew member leaned in to ask if she was ready, she said yes, walked onto the stage loaded with painkillers and steroids, and improvised a line Chazelle liked enough to keep in the film.</p>

Here is the part she volunteers when she tells that story. The migraine helped.

"It kind of made me not nervous," she told Kevin McCarthy on _On Film_ in June 2026, "cuz all I had to do was like get through this scene."

A woman who has taken medication for anxiety discovered that a genuine medical emergency settled her down. She had discovered the same thing three years earlier on a different set, and that discovery is the reason she has spent the years since screaming for a living.

## What is Samara Weaving's personality type?

### Samara Weaving is an Enneagram Type 6

The evidence is not subtle, because she narrates it herself. Asked on _Happy Sad Confused_ in 2019 when her anxiety started, she had no origin story to offer: "I don't remember not being anxious, so I don't know." That is the baseline condition, reported without drama, as a weather pattern she was born into.

What she does with that baseline is where the [Type 6](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6) pattern becomes legible. She built a professional filter out of it. "Especially choosing jobs," she told Kevin McCarthy on _On Film_ in June 2026, "I'm like, do I trust this filmmaker? Do I trust this person? Where I know that I'm going to work really hard, are they going to work just as hard?"

Read that again as a hiring criterion. Most actors screen scripts. She screens people. She is deciding, before she signs, who will still be working as hard as she is at two in the morning on day twenty-six.

The second tell is what she says about the future. Talking to _Collider_ in June 2026, she described the strangest part of finally having enough leverage to say no: "Okay, let's just try and trust that something will happen. You will work again, which is hard to convince myself of, but it does pay off." She has three films out this year. She still has to argue herself into believing there will be a fourth.

The Type 9 reading has a case, and it is worth taking seriously for one paragraph. She is easygoing in interviews, allergic to friction, and told _The Independent_ in September 2025 that "fame wasn't something I was ever striving for." But a Nine's peace comes from not looking too hard at what might go wrong. Weaving looks constantly. She audits directors, over-prepares to a fault, and reports that trusting the world takes conscious effort. The vigilance is the giveaway.

## Samara Weaving's childhood was four countries and no fixed address

She was two weeks old the first time she left the country she was born in. "I was born in Australia but I left it two weeks old and we grew up in Fiji, Singapore, Indonesia, a little bit in Italy, and then moved back to Australia," she told Josh Horowitz in 2019. Her father worked in film. Her mother is a curator with a PhD in Peranakan art, hired by museums that happened to be in whichever city came next.

The moving worked on her the way it works on a quiet kid. She stopped talking. "I wouldn't speak to other children for, you know, weeks," she said.

Sit inside that for a second. A six-year-old walks into a classroom in a new country, and every child already in the room has a history with every other child in the room.

<p class="inner-thought">There are eleven of them and one of me. They already know who sits where. If I say the wrong thing on the first day, that's who I am for the whole year. Better to find out how this place works first. I can start tomorrow. Or the day after.</p>

Her parents' fix was to enroll her in drama class at five. The stated goal was to get her to open up, and it worked in a way that is worth noticing carefully. What she found there was a room where the words arrive in advance and somebody else is responsible for what happens next. "That's where I can just be a lunatic and don't feel judged," she has said about acting.

That is the whole engine, installed at five years old. Give her the script and she will do anything in front of you. The Enneagram calls this the search for solid ground, and Weaving's version is unusually literal: she grew up on ground that was scheduled to move.

The wanting came later, and not from the uncle everyone asks about. She knew Hugo Weaving was an actor, but his films were rated too high for her, so the family DVD shelf stayed mostly theoretical. Then the Lord of the Rings adaptations arrived, and her father had already read the books aloud to her and her sister, and she sat down expecting to be transported.

"You're kind of lost in this beautiful magical world," she told McCarthy, "and then your uncle, silly uncle Hugo's got a wig on and pretending to speak Elvish, and you're just going, okay well I'm out now. This is so annoying."

The famous relative was a spell-breaker. The film that actually made her want the job was _Pirates of the Caribbean_, which she saw with no personal connection to anyone in it, and which sent her home asking her dad whether that was something a person could do.

It was. The job came with a side effect nobody had prescribed.

## Why Samara Weaving stopped taking her anxiety medication

The line that made headlines in 2025 was cheerful and slightly reckless. "Being able to scream and run and chase people and fight back kind of shook the anxiety out of me," she told _The Independent_ in September 2025. "After that, I noticed I didn't need my meds!"

The full quote does something the headline version leaves out. She frames the episode as a discovery about herself rather than a discovery about pharmacology: "I was discovering this inner strength that I didn't realise I had."

It has now happened twice on the record, and both times she reported it with something close to surprise.

**Entry one. _Ready or Not_, shot late 2018.**
The job: play a bride hunted through a mansion by her new in-laws. The work: screaming, sprinting, bleeding, fighting back, for the length of a shoot.
The result: she came off the medication and stayed off it.
Her note on the mechanism: "Your body doesn't know. You're just in a state of panic and real fear the whole time, and my brain is like, hey, it's not real. But it does feel the next day like you've been chased by a leopard."

**Entry two. _Babylon_, shot 2021.**
The job: one high-pressure scene for Damien Chazelle, on a stage crammed with equipment.
The condition: the migraine, the IV, the ambulance idling off-stage.
The result: the nerves went quiet, for the reason she gave in the ambulance. One problem in front of her instead of all of them.

Both entries hold the fear constant. Weaving is frightened either way. What changes is the scope. When her body has one urgent, concrete, survivable problem in front of it, the diffuse background dread has nowhere left to live. The leopard collapses everything into a single question with a single answer: keep moving. She had spent years being told, in effect, that her alarm system was miscalibrated. Given a threat with an actual shape, it turned out to have a job.

Anxiety [shows up differently in every type](/enneagram-corner/anxiety-and-enneagram-types-guide), and the version that runs on doubt has a specific relationship to action: the dread is worst in the waiting room. Weaving found a career that is almost entirely not-the-waiting-room. On a set there is a call sheet, a scene, a mark, and a job for every pair of hands.

This is also why she prepares obsessively and then tries to give the rest away. "For my personality and for my mental health, it's best if I just show up and do my job, which is act, and that's all I can control, and then really just relinquish the rest." She will happily rehearse the arc of every scene in advance, as she did mapping Grace's escalation through _Ready or Not_ before shooting started. She will not produce. She tried it. "It's not for me," she told McCarthy. Owning all the variables is the exact shape of the thing that keeps her awake.

She is careful about the boundary now, and she should be. A film wrap is not a treatment plan, and she has never claimed otherwise. What she reports is narrower, and it has a cost attached. "Being terrified for weeks on end is just exhausting," she told _Coveteur_ in June 2026. "Being exhausted is useful for the character, but it's not useful for me."

### Samara Weaving's Wing: 6w7

Weaving reads as 6w7 with a wide wing. The core is fear management, loyalty, and vetting. The outward strategy is comedy, charm, and a taste for the absurd. She gravitates to material that laughs at horror rather than wallowing in it, from _The Babysitter_ to _Ready or Not_ to _Bill &amp; Ted Face the Music_, and directors describe her as somebody who wants permission to be strange on camera. Radio Silence gave it to her, in her retelling, with a note that amounted to: you're a weird gal, lean into the weirdness. That appetite for play under pressure is the Seven side doing its job. A 6w5 in her position would retreat into technique and research. She retreats into being funny. See the <a href="/enneagram-corner/enneagram-wings-complete-guide">wings guide</a> for how the neighbors reshape a core type.

### Samara Weaving's Instinctual Subtype: sp/sx

Self-preservation leads. Her stated ambition has always been the working life rather than the visibility, and she says so in exactly those terms. She married a writer she met on set, works repeatedly with the same directors, and describes the two-year drought after _Home and Away_ in survival economics rather than wounded ego. The sexual instinct runs second and shows up in the intensity of her chosen pairs, on screen and off, and in the pull toward roles that put her body under real physical strain. The social instinct is the quiet one: the gumnut mafia functions as a small trusted unit rather than a network, and she does not appear to work it for anything. More on the three drives in the <a href="/enneagram-corner/enneagram-instinctual-subtypes">instinctual subtypes guide</a>.

### Stress and Growth Arrows

The Six's <a href="/enneagram-corner/enneagram-connecting-lines">connecting lines</a> run to Three under pressure and to Nine in growth. The Three move is the decade she spent taking whatever was offered, which she now summarizes as "anyone who wants to hire me, I'll do whatever." That is the image-managed, keep-the-résumé-moving response, and she names it as the phase before she had leverage. The Nine move is what her recent language sounds like. "Relinquish the rest." "If I put too much pressure on the outcome, I'm in it for the wrong reasons." That is a Six arriving at settledness, and it is notably the vocabulary of the last two years rather than the last ten.

### Counterarguments: Why Samara Weaving Might Not Be Type 6

Type 9 is the strongest alternate, and the main body handles it: the conflict-aversion and the unhurried public affect are real, but Nines achieve peace by declining to look, and Weaving audits every collaborator before she signs.

Type 4 gets raised because of the genre affinity and the outsider childhood. It does not survive contact with her own material. She has no romance about her own suffering and no investment in being singular. Her stated goal is a good room with hard-working people in it, which is a security aim.

The honest limit of the type is not a rival type. It is appetite. Her rule that fear is the reason to take a script selects for instability, which no fear-management model predicts. The _Azrael_ section below makes the case.

What would change our mind: sustained evidence that her caution is disinterest rather than vigilance, or an account of her decision-making that runs on identity rather than on trust. Neither has surfaced.

## The horror movie where Samara Weaving does not speak

Even so, she kept choosing the hardest available version of the job.

In March 2024, at SXSW, she premiered a film in which she never says a word.

_Azrael_, directed by E.L. Katz and written by Simon Barrett, is set in a world where nobody speaks. Weaving plays a woman who escapes a devout community that intends to sacrifice her, then spends the film being hunted through the forests of Estonia. She carries the entire thing on her face and her body. IFC Films and Shudder released it that September, and it sits at 71% on Rotten Tomatoes across 77 reviews, most of them pointing at her performance as the reason to watch.

Hold that against the childhood. The girl who walked into each new school and said nothing to anyone for weeks grew up to headline a movie where speaking is not available and silence is the entire discipline.

Then hold it against the method. Everything Weaving says about surviving a set runs through preparation: know the lines, know the accent, leave nothing to chance. _Azrael_ removed the thing she prepares. "At first it was scary because I thought, 'How am I going to communicate with an audience using just my face and limbs?'" she told _Screen Rant_ at the festival. "Instead of learning lines, I had to learn so much choreography, which was harder because I needed an Epsom salt bath afterwards."

She took it anyway, and her stated reason is the most useful sentence she has given about her own decision-making.

"I was really afraid, which is a good sign for me to do something. Usually if I'm like, 'Oh, yeah, I can do this,' I shouldn't do it."

That is not fear management. That is fear used as a compass, pointed at whatever she is most likely to fail at. She said the same thing in plainer terms two years later: "When I read a script and I get nervous because it's something I haven't done before or it feels challenging, I want to do it. If I'm doing the things that I've done before or roles that I'd done before, it's just not as fun at work."

The security model explains why she vets people before signing. It does not explain this. Someone organized entirely around making the ground stop moving does not go hunting for the least stable footing on offer. What she is describing is appetite, and it is aimed at exactly the terrain the fear model says she should refuse. No type gets to claim all of a person, and this is the seam where Weaving's own taste is carrying weight the Enneagram cannot.

Some of it is not about fear at all. Horror, she said at SXSW, is "where you can be one of the guys. I love that; it makes me feel really powerful, and I have autonomy over it."

Off camera the older pattern is still running. "Left to my own devices, I'd be way less outgoing than I am," she told _Collider_ in March 2026. "I think we live in a culture that rewards extroverts, and I think introverts are sort of seen as weird, or strange, or even dangerous. I think a lot of introverts have to sort of copy what extroverts do in order to fit in, survive, get jobs." The easy public affect is a job requirement she taught herself. _Azrael_ is the one where she did not have to perform it.

## The two years after Home and Away when nobody called

None of that machinery was there at the start.

She started working at fourteen and got the first two jobs she auditioned for: a series called _Out of the Blue_, then _Home and Away_, where she played Indi Walker from 2009 to 2013 while still finishing school. She drew the obvious conclusion.

"I think I had a very naive perception of how the industry works," she told _New Idea_ in 2021. "Oh, I got the two jobs I auditioned for. This is easy."

Then she left Summer Bay, and the phone stopped.

"I didn't work for nearly two years," she said. "It was a real reality check in terms of the life of an actor."

Two years is a long time for anyone. For someone whose whole nervous system is organized around the possibility that the ground stops being ground, it is confirmation. She had been handed a version of the world in which effort produces work, and then the world withdrew it, and the lesson she took was arithmetic: "In the entirety of SAG, 1% of actors are working. It's hard."

The posture that came out of it lasted a decade. "When you're starting out, you're going, 'Anyone who wants to hire me, I'll do whatever,'" she told _Collider_ in June 2026.

The drought is doing quiet work in every career decision she has made since. It explains the over-preparation, which she still describes in the language of blame avoidance thirteen years later: "I prep very well so that if a take is messed up, I really don't want it to be because I don't know my lines or my accent dropped." Or more bluntly: "I hate being underprepared. I hate trying to read the scene on the way to work." It explains why she has to talk herself into believing there will be another job. And it explains the move to Los Angeles in her mid-twenties, which she describes now with a kind of retrospective bewilderment.

"I'm very grateful for my 20-year-old brain that was like, I can do this, let's move, let's go! I don't understand how brazen I was."

Listen to what she is actually envying. A version of herself who had not yet collected enough information to be frightened, and who got on the plane before anyone could hand her any.

What she assembled once she landed was small and closed and Australian. "I think we're called the gumnut mafia," she told _The Independent_, naming her circle after the expat pack that formed around Olivia Newton-John in eighties Los Angeles. Hers runs to Teresa Palmer, Phoebe Tonkin and Lara Worthington. "I'm very grateful for all my ding-a-ling girls out here." It is not a network and she does not work it. It is a handful of people from the same island who already know what the drought feels like.

In late May 2026 she and Warden had their first child. She has kept nearly everything about it private, including the name, though the pronoun escaped in June: "She's number one. As long as she's fine, everything's fine."

"It's the most magical. It's the most terrifying, most insane experience," she told _Coveteur_. "My heart's on the outside now."

Magical and terrifying, in that order, in the same breath. It is the exact pair she has been using about her work for a decade, arriving now in the one part of her life she cannot prep for. The woman raised on ground that was scheduled to move is the one holding it still for someone else.

## Why Samara Weaving keeps making the same movie

That instinct to keep the circle small runs her career too.

In March 2026 she released _Ready or Not 2: Here I Come_, in which she once again spends a night being hunted by wealthy people and once again screams a great deal. It landed at 73% on Rotten Tomatoes across 82 reviews, down from the original's 89%.

The sharpest criticism went straight at the repetition. "Samara Weaving's guttural screams can cover a multitude of sins," wrote Zachary Lee in _The Wrap_, "but even her fully embodied performance and powers can't save a movie that mistakes stilted recurrence for high-octane throwback."

He is right about the film. The charge worth answering is the one underneath it, which follows her everywhere: that she has one register, keeps returning to it, and has built a career out of a single sustained scream.

Put the label to her directly and she takes it. Asked whether she minds being called a scream queen: "Yeah, why not? 'Scream Queen' is great. I'll take it!"

Here is what that repetition actually is. Weaving decides on jobs by asking whether she can trust the people running them, and that question takes a full production to answer honestly. Once a set of people has answered it, they become one of a very small number of rooms in the world where she already knows the floor holds. Matt Bettinelli-Olpin and Tyler Gillett answered it in 2018. Her husband, Jimmy Warden, answered it before that, on _The Babysitter_, where they met, and he later wrote her a lead and directed it himself. "I wrote it with her in mind," Warden told _People_ in 2025. "I put all of her into all of these scenes when they were conceived."

Going back to those rooms is what loyalty looks like when trust is scarce and slow to earn. It reads as a lack of range from outside. From inside, it is the only reliable inventory she has.

She is also, notably, spending it down on purpose now. Her stated rule as of June 2026 runs on two words: anything short of a "fuck yes" gets a "fuck no." The thing she is correcting is specific. "I think before I could talk myself into a role or I could get convinced by other people, or I'd try and tell myself, 'This is good for me,' instead of sticking with my gut." She spent most of her career handing the decision to whoever in the room sounded certain. The rule is a Six taking her own authority back.

And the people in those rooms describe her as the opposite of a limited performer. "The linchpin that holds it all together is Samara," Bettinelli-Olpin said of the sequel. Kathryn Newton, playing opposite her, put it in the terms that actually matter to Weaving: "She's way cooler than me. She's a little older than me, she's a little more worldly. Sam made it really easy for me to be myself."

The woman who spent weeks not speaking at new schools now specializes in making the new person in the room comfortable.

Which is what makes the last week of July 2026 the most interesting thing that has happened to her. _Deadline_ and _Variety_ both reported that Marvel had cast her as Emma Frost in Jake Schreier's _X-Men_. Per the trades, finalists tested in early July and her read was the one nobody could beat. Marvel declined to comment. She has said nothing publicly.

None of that job is available to the audit. You cannot interview a room that has not been assembled yet, and the fame she says she was never striving for is not an optional feature of playing a mutant in that franchise. It is the terms.

So the trust-inventory reading, honest as it is about the first fifteen years, does not cover the next one. The other engine does. She has been consistent for a decade that the correct signal to take a job is being afraid of it, and there is no bigger available version of that than this. Whether the woman who needs to know the floor holds can work on a floor nobody will let her inspect is now the open question of her career. The film does not have a release date. The answer is a few years out.

## The photograph that isn't Samara Weaving

About ten years before Marvel called, in an airport, a stranger walked up to Samara Weaving and told her she was [Margot Robbie](/personality-analysis/margot-robbie).

She could have said no. It would have taken a second and cost nothing.

"I just couldn't break it to them that I wasn't," she told _People_ in May 2026, "so I posed for a photo with the fan, saying that I was Margot."

She has thought about it since. She is not sure the fan ever worked it out. "They might have looked at it later and been like, that's not… that's some random lady."

Somewhere there is a phone, or a hard drive, or a shoebox, holding a photograph of Samara Weaving smiling next to a happy stranger, filed under another woman's name. She let it happen because in the half-second available to her, giving away her own name was the cheaper thing to do.

Ten years on, audiences buy tickets to watch her refuse to hand over anything at all.
