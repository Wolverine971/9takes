<p class="firstLetter">Scott Galloway has signed off hundreds of newsletters with "Life is so rich," and a striking number of them are about who owes whom. The old owe the young. Big Tech owes taxes. Men of his generation owe young men. And Scott, by his own accounting, is still in the red.</p>

He would also be the first to say he's not a neutral bookkeeper. He calls himself a narcissist, says he's "addicted to money and the affirmation of strangers," and has argued that mental health influencers treat many of life's obstacles as "traumas to be addressed for $200/hour." So this page skips the wounded inner child. It sticks to his words and his numbers.

One more rule. His Pivot co-host [Kara Swisher](/personality-analysis/kara-swisher) is also an Enneagram Type 8 on this site. Anything below that fits Kara just as well is the type talking. Whatever only fits Scott is the man.

## What is Scott Galloway's personality type?

### Scott Galloway is an Enneagram Type 8

Scott Galloway is an [Enneagram Type 8](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-8), the Challenger. Eights sit in the gut triad, the instinctive types, where the core emotion is anger, as opposed to the fear behind the head types or the shame behind the heart types.

For an Eight, anger is mostly about fairness and control. Who's taking more than their share? Who's getting pushed around? Who's going to stop it?

Scott doesn't dress that up. "I struggle with anger and hold grudges against people, places, and things," he wrote in 2022. In 2024 he described what happens at home when his kids are "disagreeable (i.e., awful)" or his partner is "upset/angry": "I often respond as if it's a threat to my authority or value. I reflexively escalate and get back in their face(s)." He was writing about learning to stop. The reflex is the Eight part.

So is the job he gives himself. "I can take arrows, get shot in the face, and never lose sight of my role as their protector," he wrote in the same post. When Anne Morriss, co-host of TED's Fixable podcast, told him in 2024, "You are fiercely competitive. You have lots of protector energy. You're also very willing to cry in public," he answered, "You're being generous."

He also won't let anyone call his bluntness a virtue. "Because I'm successful, people often recast it as honesty, or even leadership," he wrote about his habit of saying the wrong thing at the wrong moment. "No, it's just being an asshole. Working on it."

Run the Kara test. Kara picks fights with billionaires too, and she has no patience for the timid. That's the Eight they share. What doesn't fit Kara is the bookkeeping.

## Visible at 13

Scott's parents split when he was eight or nine. His father, a Royal Navy veteran raised in Scotland, left for Ohio. His mother, a secretary who had sheltered in the London Tube during the Blitz as a girl, raised him in Westwood. "It was me and her against the world," he wrote.

When he was 13, his mother's boyfriend, Randy, put two $100 bills on the dining room table and told him to go buy stock. "You're bright enough to figure it out, and if you don't by the time I'm back, I want my money back."

Scott walked into Merrill Lynch first. "I sat in the reception area, and ... I was invisible." So he crossed the street to Dean Witter, where a broker named Cy Cordner took him seriously. They bought 13 shares of Columbia Pictures at $15 3/8. For the next two years, Scott called Cy from a payphone with two dimes every weekday to talk about his portfolio. Cy did one more thing Scott still brings up. He called Scott's mother, who had no money to invest, "to let her know what we discussed in the calls and say nice things about me."

What Scott took from it wasn't the stock.

> "At 13, I was visible. Visible and worthy of an impressive man's time, every day."

In his forties, Scott wrote, he "became more aware of my deficits," from friends to the California taxpayers who paid for UCLA. "Taking, always taking." For a decade he offered his students a $5,000 bounty to find Cy. In 2018, three of them did. Their lives turned out to rhyme: UCLA, financial services, divorce, two kids, entrepreneurship. When Pivot's live tour reached Los Angeles in 2025, Cy was in the audience.

He turned it into a formula he repeats in post after post: remarkable men become "irrationally passionate about the well-being of a child ... that isn't theirs." He uses the same phrase for his mother.

## The debts he keeps paying

Fans tease Scott for telling the same stories over and over. Look at what the stories are, though. Nearly every one is a debt.

UCLA rejected him the first time. On September 19, 1982, nine days before classes started, an admissions director called to say they were letting him in on appeal, as "a son of a single mother and the great state of California" ("no joke, her exact words").

He graduated with a 2.27 GPA after four rounds of academic probation. A fraternity big brother told him to cut back to three classes and stop smoking so much pot. "Without a network of other men serving as guardrails for me, I likely would not have graduated." Total tuition for both degrees: about $8,800.

Now look at what he does with the money. "I've given approximately $20 million to the University of California, and I still owe, as I didn't climb, but was lifted," he wrote in July 2026, a week after [Gavin Newsom](/personality-analysis/gavin-newsom) appointed him a UC Regent. "This is the only board I have ever wanted to serve on. Let me be clear, I am not a humble person."

He has already rewritten his origin story in public, to credit the people who built it. "Until 40, my story was that I was the son of a single immigrant mother who lived and died a secretary. I overcame those humble beginnings to achieve significant success because, you know, I'm awesome." After 40, he wrote, he could see clearer: "I was given a rocket ship, built by others. To be clear, I'm talented and navigated the ship well ... but I wasn't going to soar without the sacrifice and talent of millions of others."

The young-men campaign runs on the same accounting. Introducing *Notes on Being a Man* in November 2025, he wrote: "Men of my generation have a debt to these young men. Our unfair advantage must be paid forward (or backward)." Even his definition of manhood is a balance sheet. When critics pushed back on his "providers, protectors, and procreators" framing, he wrote "Fair" and offered a simpler one: "Men should add surplus value. Give more than they take."

## The fights: who's taking

If you keep a ledger, you notice who's skimming.

In his 2017 TED talk he called the EU's fines against Facebook and Google "25-cent parking tickets on a meter that costs 100 dollars an hour." In 2024 his TED talk on how the US is destroying young people's future opens and closes with the same question: "Do we love our children?" In between, he lays out the bill. "When I applied to UCLA, the admissions rate was 76 percent. Today, it's nine percent." Universities sitting on billion-dollar endowments, he says, are "a hedge fund offering classes."

On January 30, 2026, he turned the newsletter into a strike. Resist and Unsubscribe asked readers to cancel tech and AI subscriptions for February, a "monthlong national economic strike" meant to pressure Trump over ICE, with ICE contractors as a second target. "The most radical act in a capitalist society isn't marching, it's not spending." Three weeks and a 30-outlet media tour later, he reported more than 1.5 million visits to the site "without spending a penny on ads." Meta and Google had rejected the ads his team tried to buy as political.

Ten years into the newsletter, he summed up the arc: most business leaders went quiet after 2016. "I've grown louder." In 2026 he told listeners his theme for the year was "zero fucks given." He has a line for what follows: criticism "is the receipt for actually saying something." He adds, in the same paragraph: "Also, often, I just plain get it wrong." That part passes the Kara test. Two Eights at one microphone, both sure the powerful are getting away with something.

## His father, and putting away the scorecard

The ledger has one account Scott spent decades trying to close.

His father was a charmer. In the seventies the two of them would sneak onto exclusive Ohio golf courses at dusk with a 5 iron and a putter. When Scott came out of the bushes with lost balls, his dad would "point at it, nod, and then mess up my hair." Messing up his hair was "his primary vehicle for affection. He did it often, and it felt wonderful." His summary of the man: "Nothing can get you into trouble with women who aren't your wife, or out of trouble with service workers, better than a strong jawline and a Glasgow twang."

His father was also, in Scott's telling, married and divorced four times, and he left his last wife a year before she died of Parkinson's. As an adult, Scott held it against him. "I didn't speak to my father for years at a time because I would get very angry when I would think about he could have made my mom and my life much easier and he didn't," he told an audience at the 92nd Street Y in November 2025. "I had a scorecard around every relationship and including my father."

Then, "not until I was about 40 or 45," he decided to stop. "Rather than constantly revisiting if/when my dad was there for me as a kid, I now just focus on being the son I want to be. It's liberating," he wrote in 2024. He was already giving the same advice in 2018, in a newsletter written as the wedding toast he'd give friends: "Don't keep score."

Scott tells this as settled by a decision. His father died in 2025, at 95. Scott eulogized him on Pivot and broke down at the end. "Almost made it through," he said. Kara: "Scott, this is your best quality. Don't think it's not."

Seven months later, on an Office Hours episode of The Prof G Pod, a listener asked how he was doing. "The reality is I'm fine. I sort of mourned my dad's passing for the last five years of his life. And this is gonna sound, this is gonna sound harsh, but my dad got a lot more from our relationship than I got from our relationship." Then he said his father "wasn't a bad father," that as adults "we were more like friends," called his own childhood "remarkably unremarkable," and said he is still more affected by his mother's death, 22 years on.

"My dad got me working out when I was like nine years old," he said on the same episode, with "this little Royal Navy fitness handbook." He hopes his sons keep it up.

The anger was real, and he reached for the bluntest tool available: he cut his father off. Then he made himself stop. That's an Eight's arc. In a 2024 newsletter he put the old habit plainly: "For most of my life, relationships have been transactional. And I was either on the right side of the ledger," meaning "getting more than I receive," "or dissatisfied."

## He tells on himself first

Scott's best-known confessions are before-and-after stories, and he tells them on purpose.

"It cost me my hair. It cost me my first marriage. And it was worth it," he told the Rational Reminder podcast in 2024, describing years ("maybe even decades") of working until 10 or 11 at night. People quote that line against him. They usually stop before the next sentence: "And I know how awful that sounds." He asked for the divorce. His verdict on his early-thirties self: "So. Fucking. Selfish." He lists it among "the most disturbing periods in my life": "when I acknowledged my own immense shortcomings after telling my wife I wanted a divorce."

The crying story works the same way. "I didn't cry from the age of 29 to 44. I didn't cry when my mother died. I didn't cry when my, I got divorced. I didn't cry when my company went chapter 11. For 15 years. I didn't cry. I forgot how," he said on TED's Fixable in 2024.

The window moves between tellings: 34 to 44 in a 2017 newsletter, 29 to 44 on Fixable, and in a 2018 post he stretched the dry era to 25 to 40. The turn always comes in his forties. "Since my mid-forties, something strange: I. Cry. All. The. Time." Asked what changed, he said, "It wasn't any one thing."

That 2018 post also gives his explanation for the dry years, and it's ambition. "From the age of 25–40, my range of emotions could generously be described as 'narrow.' I loved nobody, and the only real emotions I felt were satisfaction or disappointment from my ambition as I howled in the money storm."

He's candid about why he shares all this. "As I've gotten older, I've started to embrace the personal. It comes easily, as I'm a narcissist. But also, people are looking for a connection, and a good way to connect with several hundred strangers at once (or with just one person) is to be vulnerable." Kara once described him to the New York Times as an "open wound." His own description was competitive: "I can't out-CNBC them, but I can talk about my kids or my up-and-down relationship with my dad."

So the vulnerability is a craft, and he doesn't claim there's a vault behind it. He put off ketamine therapy for years, he wrote, because "I was wary that something unwelcome would surface. Whatever trauma or demons lie in my subconscious, I've managed to (mostly) suppress them." He was fine leaving it there: "let sleeping dogs lie. But here we go." He went, in 2024. "During my trip, there was nothing new. No discovery, just clarification and illumination."

What he keeps in the present tense is a short list. He says he still struggles with anger and depression. On Fixable he said he has "trouble forgiving myself" and would "beat myself up all fucking night" over two mistakes he'd made on stage that day. And he calls himself "a middle-aged man who hasn't gotten over the death of his mother." He spent eight months living with her in Nevada before she died in 2003. "Other than my kids, the thing I am most proud of is giving my mom a good death."

Run the Kara test here and it fails, which is the point. Kara made her name asking powerful people hard questions. Scott made a good part of his asking them of himself.

## Preparing his sons for others

Scott had his first son at 42. "I wasn't in love when they first came out of the womb," he has said. "I fell in love with my kids." He rarely names his sons in the newsletter and keeps his partner's name out of it. He films one second of family life every day with an app, then watches "the six minutes that was our year" with his boys. On Rational Reminder he did the math out loud: "I got another five Christmases with my youngest, another two with my oldest." Then he corrected himself. "And, actually, I'll probably have zero with my oldest who's already at boarding school. I wasn't ready for him to leave this soon."

His oldest started college in September 2026. In a post called "Preparing men for others," Scott wrote that he's been "struggling with my purpose moving forward," and offered his own test for purpose: negative ROI. Raising boys, he wrote, is "years of investment that will never register a traditional return. I doubt they'll ever be up at 2 a.m. worried about me." For a man who measures everything in returns, that's the tell.

In the same post he described a ring his partner gave him, engraved with a line from *The Little Prince*: "What is essential is invisible to the eye." "I've spent most of my life chasing the visible: the house, the businesses, the stuff, the books, the status. All of it, I now recognize, was the means." His oldest son "put a thread through the ring and wears it around his neck."

## The column he's still filling

"Everyone needs to feel relevant, to have a stage where they hear applause. For me it's been work and school," he wrote in 2018. As a kid, he told Fixable, "I wanted to be rich and awesome. Literally, that's what I wanted." On several podcasts since, including Kara's, he has said some version of "I'm addicted to money and the affirmation of strangers."

He can joke about it. In 2017: "I never got the jet, but have achieved Mosaic status on JetBlue." In January 2026: "I'm going to Davos next week on my own plane, but I got there by waiting tables, carrying groceries, and hauling golf bags 5 miles in the humid Ohio summer."

He has started putting some of it in the past tense. "My purpose as a younger man used to be how can I be more rich and more awesome," he said in 2026. Still, that's how a [Type 3](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-3) talks, and if you read him as a Three, you have his words on your side. Our case for 8 is narrower: he names his anger as anger, he cut off his father over a scorecard, and when an audience and a loyalty collide, he keeps the loyalty. More in the Rabbit Hole.

<div class="panel">

**Wing: 8w7.** The 7 wing is the appetite and the jokes. "I want more, fucking more," he wrote of his 25-to-40 era.

**Instinct: probably social, with a heavy self-preservation streak.** Social Eights fight for a group, and Scott's fights are mostly collective: young men, the young versus the old. See the [instinctual subtypes guide](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-instinctual-subtypes).

**Stress and growth.** Under stress, Eights can [move toward Type 5](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-types-in-stress): withdrawn, detached, in their heads. After his divorce he moved to New York "to be all about Scott": "I was an island living on an island." In growth, Eights move toward [Type 2](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-2), where protection becomes care. He gives away at least what he spends each year, and says giving makes him "feel my strength and skills are protecting and providing."

**Why not Type 3?** This is the real contest, and Scott supplies the case. His first girlfriend, at UCLA, was "the first woman I was 'me' around, instead of trying to be someone I thought she'd like." He told Andrew Huberman in 2026 that he's "trying to portray myself as being better than I actually am." His dry decade, spent on ambition, is the classic Three habit of parking feelings to perform.

The honest problem for 8 is shame. Threes sit in the heart triad, where shame is the core emotion, and Scott tells his worst moments in its vocabulary. After the 2008 crash, falling short of the Manhattan life he "envisioned for my son" "seriously fucked with my sense of why I was here ... and my worth as a man." In the same post, he put that standard down to ego. Finding his mother "trapped in a place that reeked of urine": "I. Had. Failed." And much of the anger he confesses is aimed at himself: "I have trouble forgiving myself." We read the anger as the engine and the shame as what it costs him. A Three reader would reverse that, and they'd have a case.

Two things still tip it to 8 for us. First, his anger goes outward as well as in: grudges "against people, places, and things," escalation when his authority is challenged, years of not speaking to his father.

Second, when an audience and a loyalty collide, he tends to keep the loyalty. In September 2026, on Raging Moderates with Jessica Tarlov, he argued with Michigan's Democratic Senate nominee, Abdul El-Sayed, over whether Israel's war in Gaza is a genocide, and a run of one-star reviews from his own listeners followed on Apple Podcasts. His rule, from that March:

> "If you have economic security and people who love you unconditionally ... you have an obligation to speak your mind."

A Three's instinct is to read the room and win it. His is to get louder.

**What would change our mind:** fights that track the audience, picked when they pay and dropped when they don't. If that's what you see, call him a Three running an Eight's script. He did write it.

**Why not Type 6?** He sought out mentors and says he has been "anxious all the time" since having kids. But he doesn't test authority to see if it's trustworthy. He tells it what it owes.

**Why not Type 7?** The appetite is real, which is why the 7 wing fits. But Sevens steer around pain, and Scott keeps a ledger of his. He says he's "in the past too much."

Scott would probably call this page a Ouija board, which is what he calls his own annual predictions. Fair. He also reads the comments.

On the newsletter's tenth anniversary he explained why he keeps writing. "My boys have little interest in my work; I doubt they've ever read a post. But they will." When they do, he hopes it will "cement what they feel but may not know" about "how much I think about and love them."

It's the one debt he can't settle himself. He's betting they'll collect.
