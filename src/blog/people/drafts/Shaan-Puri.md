> "I think it's because a part of me feels like life is short - that Disneyland will close soon and I wanna ride all the rides before we get kicked out." Shaan Puri, ["who's your Elon?"](https://www.shaanpuri.com/essays/whos-your-elon), 2024

<p class="firstLetter">In March 2024, Shaan Puri brought a personality test onto My First Million and took it apart on air. Retake Myers-Briggs a few weeks later, he told Sam Parr, and about half of people get a different label. Critics say it runs on the Forer effect, "the same thing that astrologers fortune tellers and other pseudo scientists use." Then he read out a grit test. The first item was "new ideas and projects sometimes distract me from previous ones." He answered, "guess what it's very much like me." He added, "I have a half half written book over there." He scored 2.5 out of 5, called the test "not science," and joked, "I'm in denial right now I'm bargaining."</p>

Then he handed the test to Sam. Same question. Sam: "false."

That exchange is the method for this page. A vague label fits everyone, so every claim below has to pass the Sam test: it should fit Shaan and not his co-host. If it describes [Sam Parr](/personality-analysis/sam-parr) just as well, it's a horoscope and it should go.

Here's the claim. Shaan is quick to quit experiments and slow to quit people. He walks away from things that feel like a trap, including things that are working. He stays for years with the people who make the work feel like play. And when something hurts, his first move is to make it interesting.

## What is Shaan Puri's personality type?

### Shaan Puri is an Enneagram Type 7

Shaan Puri is most likely an [Enneagram Type 7](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-7), the Enthusiast, probably with a 6 wing. Sevens need to stay free, stimulated and out of pain. Shaan leaves anything that feels like a trap, turns his work into play, and turns hurt into a framework.

Sevens sit in the head triad, the thinking types, where the core emotion is fear. (The gut types run on anger and the heart types on shame.) A Seven's fear rarely looks like fear. It looks like plans, options, jokes and the next exciting thing. Underneath is a fear of being stuck: in pain, in boredom, in a life that has already been decided.

Shaan writes about that fear as if it were a villain. "Safety murders your ambition. Murders your sense of adventure. Safety is a serial killer." The word "later" gets the same treatment: "Whenever I hear it, I immediately put my dukes up ready to fight."

His life motto is borrowed from a stranger. "I had heard a quote from a guy I met at a casino: 'I don't know much, but I know one thing: the ordinary life isn't for me'." In 2024 he wrote that the line "stuck with me for the 16 years since I heard it."

Run the Sam test on that and it mostly passes. Sam wants freedom too, but his version is about not being controlled: savings that let him tell people off. Shaan's version is about not being bored, or boxed in by a path someone else could predict.

The clearest tell came in August 2024. After a real fight with Sam, Shaan came on air with notes, apologized first, and walked Sam through what he'd learned: Gottman's Four Horsemen, the 5-to-1 ratio. Then he read out a heading from his own notes, "what did I learn about myself?"

> "my coping mechanism was like, oh, this research is fascinating. Ooh, this framework is cool... But I'm kind of distancing myself from the cold hard facts of my own behavior."

That is the Seven's defense in one sentence, named by the Seven. A top comment under the episode put it more bluntly: "Shaan is the type of guy who will create a 5 step plan after having an argument and make a pod about it."

## The two weeks that killed the doctor plan

"I spent 10 years telling everyone I wanted to be a doctor - sports medicine, to be exact." He did pre-med at Duke, took the MCATs and got into medical school. What ended it was a wedding, where he met an orthopedic surgeon and asked to shadow him for two weeks.

"Right away, I realized that I did NOT want to be a doctor." The job had "very little creativity." His conclusion is the most Seven sentence in his origin story: "I realized creativity and variety matter a lot to me."

The push came from an easy-A class called Getting Rich. The professor had taken a Duke Mandarin degree to China on a one-way ticket, built a business there and retired by 30. When he pitched a sushi startup over medical school, every adult he asked said restaurants fail. "She was the only one who was like this sounds awesome you could totally do this."

Sabi Sushi, built with two Duke friends, was pitched as "the Chipotle of sushi." It launched in Denver in 2011 as a delivery-only "restaurant without a restaurant." It made a little money and it was miserable. Shaan grades it "A for effort, but this business was an F": 10% margins, working "morning, afternoon, and night," about $20,000 of profit for a year of full-time effort. What he remembers deciding is this:

> "...we also were like, this is a trap. Every day that this succeeds is another day we're going to be in this business. And I just want out."

He added that he didn't "need to have a life sentence in this business." A Three might have tried to scale it anyway. Shaan heard a cell door.

Around the same time he spent a year he calls "strategically broke": no job and no plan, splitting a Boulder apartment three ways at $466 a person, with a free pet mouse named Marty. He tutored and coached basketball at a school for autistic children for $35 an hour. He entered a dozen business-plan competitions, won two, and brought in more than $25,000 for the group.

"At the time, I felt like I was striking out. Over and over again. But I was having fun, so I kept going."

## Shaan Puri's B+ decade before the podcast

Then came a well-paid job abroad. "When I was 23, I was living in Australia, making a very cushy $120k/year." He quit and moved to San Francisco knowing nobody. "I'm interviewing for literally two jobs. One was a job at Stripe, which I got rejected for." The other was Monkey Inferno, Michael Birch's idea lab. He joined as a PM, "the youngest guy in the company." Within six months he was CEO of "a 20 person kind of company."

What followed was the stretch he calls pushing "a boulder up a mountain." Blab, a live group video app, reached 3.9 million users in under a year, but only about 10% came back. The team turned the tech into streaming tools for gamers under the Bebo name. Twitch bought Bebo in 2019. TechCrunch reported a price of up to $25 million. Shaan later said on MFM that "the price was not $25 million. It was less than that," and that "every time it benefited me, I felt guilty."

Those years carry the strongest case against this page. "I just wanted to be successful really badly," he said in 2023. One year he slept in the office about 275 nights. His own grade for the decade: "For 10 years, I tried to make being a founder my thing. I was a B+ at it. But in the real world, B+ doesn't get you jack sh*t."

That sounds like a Three, the type that runs on achievement and image. When Andrew Warner suggested in 2024 that Shaan was still driven by feeling like a failure, Shaan shut it down. "No, no, that whole narrative. I don't know where you got that... You didn't even know me." Then, in the same answer, he conceded most of it for the years before the sale: "I desperately wanted to have a win, kind of like what you described. My motivation was like, I think I'm good, but the evidence says I suck." What changed after Bebo, he said, was this:

> "I got these chips now, these tokens, and I'm at Chuck E. Cheese. Which machines do I want to go put the chips into? That, that one looks fun. That one looks fun. And I don't have to play for tickets."

He stopped forcing the thing he was B+ at.

## Uncle Shaan sets up the game

The podcast started as a side quest. During the Twitch deal, "I can't start a company but I can start a podcast during this time... I had the itch to start something and this was my something." On May 13, 2019, he messaged Sam: "I want the hustle to be the publisher I'll do all the work." After about 30 guest episodes, the show turned into the two of them talking.

Shaan says it worked because it was play. "I love talking to my friend, Sam. We nerd out about business stuff. We make dumb jokes. We did this for fun, with no microphone." And: "Hard work? This feels like play. I can't believe my luck. I get paid to do this?!"

His thing turned out to be explaining. "I always wondered why other people are so bad at explanations." Many fans found him through a viral thread, like the 2021 Clubhouse one that opened "everyone seems to think clubhouse is the 'next big thing' - but I think it's going to fail."

Watch enough episodes and you notice Shaan treats the mood of the room as part of his job. He wrote that down himself at 28. One of his five priorities was "being someone who lights up a room, because it feels good to make others feel good." Another line from the same list: "Laughter is my vacation."

He works at it like a craft. He tried to give himself a nickname, "Shaan P! You know, like Gary V." It flopped. A friend started calling him Uncle Shaan, and that one stuck: "If I can be the internet's fun rich uncle, that'd be grand." He has described his default comedy as "cocky funny," and he took a note from Hasan Minhaj that low-status comedy lands better.

Sam is fun too, and his laugh is how listeners know a bit landed. The difference is construction. Shaan builds the bits: the bet, the fake feud with [Jason Calacanis](/personality-analysis/jason-calacanis). "I would love to have some fake Internet beef," he said. "This is how rappers for Generations have been getting famous."

The best example is his own embarrassment. Shaan bet [MrBeast](/personality-analysis/mr-beast) $10,000 that he could hit a half-court shot first at Duke's Cameron Indoor Stadium, and won. He joked for months that he hadn't been paid. MrBeast had paid him in Bitcoin, to a fresh wallet Shaan had set up and never checked. Shaan turned it into an on-air apology and a bit.

A steady stream of fan reviews says he takes too much airtime. He has described his own style in words that concede the point: "I'm a monologue okay I got something interesting to say I know that's not how this is supposed to work but I'm a monologue... trust me you're gonna like it by the end."

That last clause is the Seven's promise: the good time is coming. Under stress, the same move turns up in his fight notes. Make it interesting.

## Shaan is the reframe, Sam is the reaction

Shaan has described the difference himself, in a riff about where each of them is shameless. "I'm a monologue... your thing is like you'll just ask a blunt question you'll just be like well dude you keep saying this like what is it how much what's in your pocket right now." Sam reacts. Shaan sets up the bit, the story or the framework, and turns it into something you'll remember.

He also credits Sam for the part he's weakest at. "Because I do this with you and you are a more structured disciplined guy you're like let's set recording dates and let's never miss them." By late 2023, about 530 episodes in, he said they recorded "like clockwork same time same days every week." The show has passed 850 episodes since.

The 2024 fight shows the dynamic under pressure. They never said what it was about. Shaan opened "Is This The End Of The Pod?" by asking, "Can I start with an apology?" He'd been "a little pissy." He said he agreed with his own words and disagreed "with all of my delivery."

Then he described his temper. "I am like a firecracker... I'm extremely tolerant and unbothered until the moment I'm bothered and then I'm a big whiny baby." He's so rarely upset, he said, that "when I am bothered, I'm so poor at handling it."

Sam's read was sharp. He told Shaan "you are quite logical," that "most people, myself included, are more emotional than logical," and that Shaan was "a little more comfortable with confrontation." Later he added: "because you're pretty emotionally healthy, that is actually a weakness."

Sam wouldn't let him take all of it: "I want to let you know that everything is mutual." Run the Sam test on that hour and the split holds. Sam takes a fight hard: it "ruins my week." Shaan answered with how fights go at home now. A disagreement "used to ruin our week. Then it started to ruin our couple days. Now it ruins literally 90 seconds." (He has said he stole a 90-second rule for suffering from [Tony Robbins](/personality-analysis/tony-robbins) "like it's Ocean's 11.") A Seven doesn't skip the pain. He learned to ration it.

Which one are you, by the way? The one who asks what it makes, or the one who explains why it works?

## Quick to quit experiments, slow to quit people

Shaan doesn't hide the quitting. On books: "There is no honor in finishing a bad book. I am a strategic quitter." On his own habits, in 2023: "I am much more of a go for the Home Run try to do something incredible and then if it doesn't work or even if it's like you know you find something that's good ah just don't really follow through and this is like a leak in my game in a way but I kind of took some pride in this."

His count of the early years: "12 different failed companies in the 10 years." In the seven years since, he said in 2025, "I think I've gone like five for five."

The [Milk Road](https://theygotacquired.com/content/milk-road-acquired-by-bitfo/) is the speed run. He and Ben Levy launched the daily crypto newsletter in early 2022, grew it to about 250,000 subscribers, and sold it to Bitfo in December 2022. He gives little credit to the famous stunt, a $1 million public crypto wallet: "this did not drive that much growth." He calls it "a one year project," the best return on time he'd had, after "grinding away for seven, eight years."

Ben stayed. Shaan wrote a whole essay about him, built around the word "down." The day Shaan pitched him "a random side project idea," Ben skipped sleeping on it and went to buy the domain. "We've worked together for nearly 5 years, and I can't wait to do another 20." And, in a very Shaan aside: "Everyone who meets Ben loves him (usually more than they love me, which is annoying, but proof that he's great)."

His definition of winning, from the fight episode: "find somebody amazing, just do projects with them for 20 years." The experiments rotate. The people stay.

## Half ass, baby: the season he stayed

The clearest change is a basketball season. Shaan had long called coaching a high school team a bucket-list item. In late 2025 his own basketball trainer became head coach at a small high school, and Shaan offered to help. He knew how that usually goes:

> "I just thought this would be something I would do just the way I do everything. Half ass, baby. I thought that I would make a fun commitment in the moment and then sort of back away over time... But this year, I'm trying to be a different guy."

He also started practicing piano scales four times a week. "I'm doing the boring things and I'm sticking with it."

"I stepped down as CEO in my companies," he said that December, "and I'm now a unpaid high school assistant basketball coach." He put in about 15 hours a week, with a 30-to-40-minute drive. "I commute, which I would you know me, I would never commute." "I didn't even know you had a driver's license," Sam cut in. The team got smashed by 30 in its first preseason game and won its first real game by 30, then dumped Gatorade on the head coach for his first win. Shaan broke down game film and helped a kid with math homework to get him back on the team. "When I tell you that this is the most fun thing I've ever done in my life, I am not lying."

Coaching taught him that telling the kids something once doesn't work: "the job is to drill this 50 times." His takeaway sounds like a man arguing with his own wiring: "reminders matter a hell of a lot more than some new strategy."

In October 2026 he said he had recently sold a company and made more money than he'd ever made. "Who am I, if not the guy who's chasing that? The dog who was chasing the car, and then I caught the car, and I have no idea why I was barking at this car the whole time." So he hired a coach.

<div class="panel">

**Wing: 7w6, tentatively.** The 6 wing shows in his loyalty: Ben for more than six years, Sam for seven, "20 years" as his definition of winning. The case for a 7w8 is the swagger: the half-court bet, "cocky funny," the $100,000 heads-up poker challenge he threw at the All-In hosts. See the [wings guide](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-wings-complete-guide).

**Instinct: unclear.** He gets "drained at social events" and energy "from reading books," which is unusual for a Seven. His stimulation is often ideas. See the [subtypes guide](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-instinctual-subtypes).

**Stress and growth.** Under stress, Sevens can move toward Type 1: keeping score, correcting, turning hurt into a procedure. In the 2024 fight Shaan said "the cardinal mistake was I was measuring what I was giving," and he came with a written list of lessons. In growth, Sevens move toward Type 5: depth, and staying with one thing. That's the piano scales, the 50 reps, and deleting his social apps at the start of 2026 to read 26 books. Treat both as a tentative map.

**Why not Type 3?** This is the strongest rival, and the best evidence for it comes from him. In his twenties he wanted "to be successful really badly." In October 2026, in a solo episode about embarrassing moments, he went further: "I think in every social situation, I am fundamentally, at some level, just actively trying to avoid embarrassment. I'm more than trying to avoid pain and death because I don't even think about those, but I definitely think about ways I could be embarrassed."

The first story is one he tells against himself. A coach's personality test scored him off the charts on one trait. "It just says, 'Narcissism.'" "As a narcissist, I was like, 'That's right. I win.'" The trait, he told listeners, is "not a judgment or an insult." The coach described people with the trait as feeling "the rules of the world don't fully apply to them." "I've always taken great pride in that," Shaan said, "of not wanting to be ordinary." That's the casino motto again. Hosting a room of the biggest founders in the world, he said, "every so often in this event, I feel this pang of insecurity and I want them to know how awesome I am... I've gone to heaven and I'm feeling hell."

That's heart-triad material, and it's why this call is close. The motive can look like a Three's. The mechanism looks like a Seven's, and two things tip it, narrowly. First, what he does once he has the chips. After Bebo he went looking for the machines that "look fun." In late 2025 he said he had stepped down as CEO of his companies and become an unpaid high school assistant basketball coach. Second, what he does with a sting. A Three is more likely to bury a score like that. Shaan made it the first story of a solo episode and ended it on "somebody held up a bit of a mirror and maybe I don't love what I see, but at least I see what it is." On pain, an earlier interview cuts the other way: "I'm actually less tough. Like, I have less tolerance for pain." In the 2024 essay about the "decade of suck," he wrote that the people who make it through "don't play to win, they play to play."

**Why not Type 5?** The introversion and the research reflex are real. A Five conserves energy and pulls back. Shaan monologues, bets and broadcasts. The Five shows up as his growth direction.

**Why not Type 9?** "Extremely tolerant and unbothered" sounds like a Nine. But a Nine avoids conflict to keep the peace, and Shaan trash-talks for sport. Even Sam says he's "a little more comfortable with confrontation."
