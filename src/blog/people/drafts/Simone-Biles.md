> "My knees are fine. My brain hurts." — Simone Biles, on what she told her family from the back room in Tokyo, _Call Her Daddy_, 2024

<p class="firstLetter">Aimee Boorman coached Simone Biles for twelve years, and she used to tease her about the ground. Did she look at the beam on the way down? At the floor? At the bars? Boorman recalled the answer on Glenn Zweig's <em>The Art of Excellence</em> in October 2025. "No, that's scary. I don't want to look at it. That's scary."</p>

She never looked. She just always knew where it was.

Boorman calls her an air savant. Asked what she perceives during a triple-twisting double back, Biles described not a picture but a sound, quoted back by her coach on that same 2025 <em>The Art of Excellence</em> episode. "It's more of a whoosh that I hear in my ears."

So here is a woman with 11 Olympic medals, 30 World medals and five skills named after her in the code of points, who has worked above a hard floor since she was six on the authority of a signal she cannot see or explain. She is 29, she has not said whether she is going to Los Angeles in 2028, and the reason she names first is mental. On July 27, 2021, in a Tokyo stadium with no fans in it, the signal cut out.

The most decorated gymnast in history is known for the day she stopped, and that is the part almost everyone reads wrong.

## What is Simone Biles's personality type?

### Simone Biles is an Enneagram Type 6

Search her name with the word "personality" and the internet hands you three answers. So Syncd says 3w2. Personality Database crowd-votes ESFJ. One Enneagram site says Six. Nobody shows their work. Here is the work.

In 2015 Biles won her third consecutive World all-around title, which no woman had done. Boorman still names it the proudest competition of the twelve years, and not for the score. She told it on <em>The Art of Excellence</em> in 2025. "At the end of it she came up to me and said, 'did I do it?'"

There were judges, a scoreboard, a medal, and a stadium of people watching. She walked up to the one adult whose read she trusted and asked whether she had done it. Boorman's own gloss on that same 2025 <em>The Art of Excellence</em> episode puts the question somewhere other than the medal: "It was about the process that it took to get there." No scoreboard could certify that, which is why she needed a person.

Eight years later, preparing for Paris, she was doing it again. Her therapist had already worked out what caused the collapse in Tokyo. Biles kept reopening it, and told _Call Her Daddy_ in 2024 how those sessions went: "I'm like, hey, look, it's Olympic year. Did we figure out why that happened? And she's like, Simone, we figured out why this has happened. I was like, are we sure? Are we sure? cuz it can't happen again."

Two sentences, eight years apart, running the same errand. A [Type 6](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6) does not doubt the evidence. She doubts whether it covers everything, because what she is really asking is whether she is safe now, and no scoreboard has ever answered that. Anxiety by itself is not a type. All nine produce anxious people. What marks a Six, the Enneagram's Loyalist, is the shape of the fear: aimed at a named future event, and quieted only by a person she trusts.

The Type 3 reading is the popular one and it is not stupid: the medals, the branding, the endorsements, and her own line to _Call Her Daddy_ in 2024, "I really need to find out who I am without gymnastics." In that same 2024 <em>Call Her Daddy</em> interview she finds the attention baffling. "How's that the biggest thing for you to follow me because I won this? It was kind of weird."

The split is in what each type asks after a win. A Three asks whether the result was enough, and it never is; 9takes' own [Type 3](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-3) page calls it a moving finish line. Biles takes the win as given and goes straight to whether the thing that broke can break again. No Achiever reading explains why the threat rather than the verdict is what she cannot close.

**Confidence: high on Six over Three, moderate on that tiebreaker,** which rests on one coach's recollection and one therapy exchange she reported herself. What would overturn it: she rebuilds without a trusted circle, or competes the double pike unspotted while the deduction still stands. The wing, the subtype, the arrows and the full argument against are further down the page.

## The room Simone Biles kept leaving at night

She was three years old when the state took her.

Her biological mother, Shanon, was struggling with drug and alcohol addiction, and calls were made. Simone, her sister Adria and their older siblings Ashley and Tevin went into foster care in Ohio, in a home that separated children by sex, girls in one room, boys in another.

Biles remembers almost nothing from that period. She remembers one thing, told to _Call Her Daddy_ in 2024.

"From my knowledge that sometimes during the night or during the day they would take kids out and replace them into a different home and that's usually how siblings were separated. So I was just so terrified that if I woke up my brother wouldn't be there."

She was three. The way she tells it now, the mechanism was already clear to her: people get moved while you sleep, and you find out in the morning. So she stopped waiting for morning. "Always sneak in and go sleep with him."

She could not stop the system from moving anybody, lock a door, or ask a grown-up a question that produced a useful answer. What she could do was get out of bed, walk down a hallway, and put her own body in the room, where she would know the second anything changed. Verification was the only power on offer.

At six she was adopted, with Adria, by her grandfather Ronald Biles and his wife Nellie, who raised them in Spring, Texas. Nellie described the beginning to _Romper_ in 2021 without sanding it down: "I knew I had my own barriers because these were not my biological children. You do everything that's nurturing, that's mothering, but emotionally, you still have to be there 100%."

The bond arrived. Biles calls them mom and dad without qualification, and when a gym full of kids once stopped practice to gawk at the news, her confusion was total. "Well, yeah, aren't you? Where'd you come from?"

Her clearest statement about any of it, in that same 2024 interview, is actuarial. "I have no idea where I would be without being adopted. Unfortunately, I might end up the same statistic that other foster kids unfortunately end up... I fear for what my life would have looked like."

Three Olympics later, describing her own childhood, she reaches for the word statistic. The hallway is the earliest place this pattern is visible, which is not the same as its cause; Adria went through the same placement and came out someone else. The girl who checked the room at night grew into a woman who still checks.

## Why Simone Biles asked her coach "Did I do it?"

She has never once told the story of how she found gymnastics in the singular. "We got into gymnastics. I say we as me and my sister."

Boorman spent twelve years refusing to squeeze what she had. When a teenage Biles started phoning in her training in 2013, she let it play out. "I had to allow her and give her a safe space to fail in." Biles competed badly, disgusted herself, and came back having coached herself. Failure had to be survivable before she would risk anything, which is why Boorman was the one she went to in 2015.

Then watch what she does with her nerves. Boorman describes it as routine: "Before she competed, she was cheering for other people," and reads it as an absence: "She wasn't sitting there in her mind worrying about if she was going to fail somehow or if she was going to let somebody down."

Let somebody down. Years before Tokyo, the vocabulary was already in place.

Rio 2016 gave her four golds and a bronze on beam that she says people made her ashamed of. Here is the summit of her sport at nineteen, as she described it in 2024:

<div class="pull-quote">"I was scared for the rest of my life. Like, how can I beat this? How can I top this? I won the Olympics at 19."</div>

She read it as the opening of a decline she now had to defend. Then she went quiet about the cost, and told _Call Her Daddy_ why. "If you had something to describe me as, strong went before that word. So I felt like I could never show a sign of weakness." The person she cried in front of, when she cried in front of anyone, was Adria. "If it wasn't her, silent cries."

Five years later, in Tokyo, she lost the ability to keep any of it out of sight.

## Simone Biles's Tokyo vault, one beat at a time

An Olympic team final is a procedure: event order, warm-up length, touches on the apparatus, who can replace whom, all fixed before anyone walks in. Here it is, with her own account of what was happening inside it, given to _Call Her Daddy_ in 2024.

<div class="timeline">

**Back of the arena.** She has been getting lost in the air in training, what gymnasts call the twisties, and pushing through it. She tells her teammates: "I'm fighting demons. I'm fighting demons right now, but I'm going to do it for you guys." She has carried a formless dread since before the plane landed. "I knew something was going to go wrong. I couldn't pinpoint what it was or when." She tells no one else. "They're looking up to me and I'm guiding them."

**The one-touch warm-up.** You sit forty-five minutes, then get exactly one practice vault. She goes over the table, does a one and a half, lands wrong. "What the... like what was that?" Her teammates crowd her. "Are you okay? Are you okay?" There are no more touches.

**Chalking up.** She does not know what she is about to do, and runs the options where she stands: a double pike she has not warmed up in four days, the two and a half on the scoresheet, a double back she rules out because "my coach will kill me. My team will never forgive."

**The vault.** She salutes and runs. Mid-air the twist count leaves her. She opens early, comes down on her feet, and grins like somebody who has gotten away with something.

**The landing.** Not relief. Not pain.

<p class="inner-thought">America hates me. The world is going to hate me. And I can only see what they're saying on Twitter right now.</p>

**The back room.** She tells her coach she is finished. Cecile asks if she is sure. For once, she is. "If I survived that, I don't know how much else I can survive. I'm a cat with nine lives, but I think that was my ninth."

**The phone call.** Her parents are in Texas, kept out of Tokyo by COVID rules. Adria, the sister she went into foster care with and cried in front of, had decided to nap through it. She wakes when the home phone rings, sees the name, answers, and Biles comes apart.

> "Mom, come pick me up, I'm scared."

</div>

She is twenty-four, the most decorated gymnast alive, 10,700 kilometers from Spring, Texas, and the sentence her body produces is the one a lost child says. Her parents had assumed a broken bone.

## Why Simone Biles didn't quit on her team in Tokyo

Within hours Charlie Kirk was on his podcast calling her "very selfish, she's immature and she's a shame to the country," adding "she's totally a sociopath." [Piers Morgan](/personality-analysis/piers-morgan) wrote that mental health had become the go-to excuse for poor performance in sport: "What a joke."

Neither made the argument that deserves answering, so here it is. An Olympic team is three other women who trained five years, and a decision Biles made in a hallway cost them the gold they were favored to win. That cost landed on real people, and Biles has never argued otherwise.

What the critics did not know is the arithmetic she was running. An Olympic team final has no drop score, every routine a team puts up is added in, and Biles was slated on all four apparatus. She told _Call Her Daddy_ in 2024 what she believed that meant: "If I got hurt on that vault, they couldn't replace me... We would have never won a medal. But since what happened happened, we went to the back. At that point, they could rule it as a mental injury and all of that stuff and physical, we got to put Jordan in."

In her account there was one window. Stop before an injury and the other three rebuild their lineups around the gap; Jordan Chiles, already on the team, ended up competing all four events. Take one more vault, catch the floor with an ankle, and four women go home with nothing. Whether she ran that in the hallway or assembled it three years later is not recoverable. If it was live, she made a team calculation while shaking; if it came later, she needed the decision to have been one. Either way it had to come out defensible to three other people. The United States won silver, and Biles spent the rest of the night as, in her words, "their loudest and best cheerleader."

Read it as loyalty because of that night. She was free to leave and stayed, and within three days the first reason she gave the public was the team's: "I simply got so lost my safety was at risk as well as a team medal." The remorse corroborates it: "That's something that I'll never forgive myself for." Making the correct call and still refusing to forgive the outcome is loyalty running ahead of self-regard, the least glamorous and most reliable thing about this type.

Coverage welded her withdrawal to <a href="/personality-analysis/naomi-osaka">Naomi Osaka</a>'s at the French Open weeks earlier. Osaka removed herself from a room that was extracting something from her; Biles stayed, cheered through the final, and carried the failure as a debt.

## Why Simone Biles stayed at USA Gymnastics after it failed her

She was in that stadium for a reason, and it starts three years earlier. In January 2018 she disclosed that she was one of the survivors of Larry Nassar, the USA Gymnastics national team doctor convicted of sexually abusing hundreds of athletes. She had waited on purpose: "I wanted to be in a good enough place and to have the proper help lined up before I spoke out."

Every other survivor who had come forward was retired. Biles kept competing, and in September 2021 she told the Senate Judiciary Committee why:

> "I worked incredibly hard to make sure that my presence could maintain a connection between the failures and the competition at Tokyo 2020."

Her plainer version: "If there weren't a remaining survivor in the sport they would've just brushed it to the side."

Her testimony assigns blame twice, and the second half shows the personality. "To be clear, I blame Larry Nassar, and I also blame an entire system that enabled and perpetrated his abuse." She named USA Gymnastics, the USOPC and the FBI.

A Six's relationship to institutions is a standing argument between wanting a protector and knowing protectors fail, which is why Sixes so often stay inside organizations they have stopped believing in. Leaving ends the watching, and the watching is part of why she stayed. It is the hallway at three years old, scaled up to a federal hearing room.

## How Simone Biles rebuilt her twisting, and what it costs per vault

Ask her for the lowest point of the Tokyo era and she does not name Tokyo. To _Call Her Daddy_ in 2024 she named the gym.

"There were so many days I would come back in the gym and it was like one step forward, five steps back. As soon as I got lost one time, get lost, pack my bags, I'm out of there."

Laurent Landi kept walking her back to the pit and making her twist. She got through World Championships still praying mid-routine, and says one more day would have broken her.

The therapy is not decoration. On her arena tour she booked sessions in the ninety minutes before showtime: "I'd be bawling my eyes out trying to put on my makeup because I'm talking to my therapist about the Olympic experience." Her frame for it: "I'm an open book anywhere else, but with trauma, it has to be by layers."

When she finally asked her coaches to take her back to the Olympics, she brought reinforcements. "I brought my husband, you know, my love blanky." They said no, and counter-offered: back in the gym, rebuild the twisting, revisit the Olympics later.

She considered it for one beat, four words long: "Do I get new coaches?" Then, immediately: "oh, you're right." Growth on a Six does not look like confidence. It looks like a woman who has stopped requiring certainty before she moves.

The love blanky has a name. Jonathan Owens, an NFL safety she married in April 2023, told <em>The Pivot Podcast</em> in December 2023 that he had not known who she was when they met, and called himself "the catch." Her account of the pile-on runs in a fixed order: "I thought it was hilarious at first," then "they hurt my feelings." Then the ruling, which concedes nothing: "he has to have his moments too and I let him have it." The rule underneath is the one she has run since she was three. "Talk about me all you want, but don't come for my family."

She answers fear by putting a body in the room. Her brother's, then her husband's. On the vault, her coach's.

The Yurchenko double pike is the hardest vault any woman has competed. At the 2023 World Championships in Antwerp, on her way to having the skill named after her, Laurent Landi stood on the landing mat and did not touch her. His presence alone was an automatic 0.5 deduction under the code. Cecile Landi's reasoning: "one mistake can have serious consequences."

Half a point, in a sport decided by hundredths, on a vault nobody else has competed, bought against what happens if she cannot.

The deduction is disputed inside the sport: Chellsie Memmel, USA Gymnastics' technical lead, has asked why a spotter is legal on bars and illegal on a double-flipping vault. She has a case. Biles is also the only woman who has had to make the choice.

## What Simone Biles won at the Paris Olympics, and what it didn't settle

She got there hurt. Warming up floor in qualification on July 28, 2024, she felt her left calf go, taped it, competed all four events anyway and posted the top all-around score in the field. Asked if she was okay: "as good as I can be."

On the morning of the all-around final she had a therapy appointment. She had been booking sessions around the time difference all Games, uneasy about saying so, knowing which 2021 headline it would be filed under. She has said it was a point of strength.

Then she went and did it. Team gold on July 30, all-around gold on August 1 (eight years after the last one), vault gold on August 3. Her account of what had changed is not about her legs. "There was always a part of me that thought what if? And I think that was my anxiety talking."

The last afternoon is the one worth watching. She came off the beam in the event final and placed fifth, and her explanation went straight to the room: silent arena, no music, spectators shushed for cheering. Exact about conditions, which is what she does with an outcome she did not choose. Then the floor final, silver behind Rebeca Andrade. On the podium Jordan Chiles asked whether they should bow; Biles answered in one word: "absolutely." The three of them made the first all-Black gymnastics podium in Olympic history, a result that did not hold: Chiles's bronze went to Ana Barbosu days later and is still being fought over. Biles spent her press conference on the woman who beat her. "She's queen."

Now hold that against 2015. A third consecutive World title produced "did I do it?" Three Olympic golds produced a woman who, by the summer of 2026, still could not say whether she was finished.

## Simone Biles left the answer at 50-50

In April 2026, at the Laureus Awards in Madrid, CNN asked her about Los Angeles. "I feel like we're still at a 50-50." She was specific about which half was open. "Mental health plays a big role in it because, physically, my coaches will get me in shape." The coaches she means, Cecile and Laurent Landi, left World Champions Centre in December 2024. In an L'Équipe interview of uncertain date she said she will be in Los Angeles either way, "whether on the apparatus or in the stands," and put the body back in the frame: "my body is aging. I felt it in Paris."

In June 2026 Biles was hospitalized for something she has not named, posted a photo of her wristbands from bed, called it one of the scariest experiences of her life, and thanked "my close circle." On 22 July she posted again, from a second procedure, Owens beside her, and said she would explain later. As of mid-August 2026 she had not, and this page will not guess.

In August 2026 she posted a picture of her locker at the World Champions Centre. Two words: "Just how I left it."

Not a comeback. Not a retirement. A photograph of a place she checked on, which is what she has done since she was three, walking down a hall in the dark to see whether the room was still the way she left it.

Every gymnast alive will tell you Simone Biles's gift is her air awareness, that impossible sense of where the ground is at every instant of every rotation. Almost nobody notices the other half of what Boorman said. She always knew where the ground was, and she never once looked at it. She was too scared to look.
