> "My true wildest dreams were to make $65,000 a year playing B-level clubs."
> — Stavros Halkias to Slate, October 2025

<p class="firstLetter">In his best year on the podcast that made him famous, Stavros Halkias cleared $30,000. He did not spend it on himself. "I put it on a down payment on a house," he told Slate in October 2025. "I bought my family a house."</p>

He has also said what he would have done for the same money on a different day. "If you told me I could have put on a ski mask and fucking hit a guy with a baseball bat for $30,000, I would've done that," he told Slate in that same 2025 interview. "I was broke as fuck in my mid-20s. I had nothing. I would've done crime, dude."

Both sentences describe the same year and the same man.

## What is Stavros Halkias's personality type?

### Stavros Halkias is an Enneagram Type 7

Ask him what the best part of the job is and he does not name a milestone.

"A new joke in a shitty bar feels ten times better than selling out a 3,000 seat," he said on the [Joe Rogan](/personality-analysis/joe-rogan) Experience in December 2023, the same month Netflix released _Fat Rascal_. Later in the episode he named the thing that actually scares him: "One day I'm going to run out of new ideas. One day I'm going to run out of new jokes."

That is [Type 7](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-7) stated plainly by a man with no interest in the framework. The Enthusiast's engine is appetite, and the fear underneath appetite is that the supply is finite. Achievement does not settle it, because a sold-out theater is a thing that already happened. Only the next unfamiliar thing proves the well still has water in it.

His stated ambition matches. "My true wildest dreams were to make $65,000 a year playing B-level clubs," he told Slate in October 2025. Nobody chasing status picks that number. It is the number you pick when the goal is a life that does not require a boss.

He is equally specific about the alternative. On the Are You Garbage podcast in June 2020, three months into lockdown, he put it in one line: "If the quarantine's taught me one thing, it's that if I ever have to work a regular job again, I'll fucking shoot myself." He is describing a life sentence, and he picked the phrasing on purpose.

## Why Stavros Halkias Didn't Know He Was Poor

"To my mom's credit, I didn't realize we were poor for my whole childhood," he said on Are You Garbage in June 2020. "I just did not know."

He found out filling out the FAFSA. The form asks for the family's expected contribution, and he assumed his mother had misread it. "I was like, oh, this is a language barrier thing," he said. She had not misread it. "She was like, I know it's zero. Like we're in debt."

Ponca Street, Greektown, two blocks from St. Nicholas: the eldest of three brothers born to a Macedonian mother and an Athenian father. He went to Greek school. He played football at Baltimore Polytechnic Institute. His mother, in his telling, "was one of those moms that thought I was like going to Harvard at 11."

Then he says the part that organizes everything after it. "I was the oldest, and I was considered the meal ticket. They were hoping I'd be like a lawyer or something."

His father is a woodworker. Halkias has said that essentially every Greek restaurant within about a hundred miles of Baltimore has something in it his dad built. Booths, trim, counters, the parts of a room nobody photographs. Halkias knows those rooms from the inside, which is why his best food joke is a warning: "There is no regard for human life when a Greek person is chef. It'll be delicious, but you'll be like, I can't feel my toes anymore."

Speaking on the Joe Rogan Experience about voting rules in December 2023, he mentioned his father almost as an aside. Roughly forty years in the country, a green card, no vote, and "I don't think my dad is ever going to become a citizen."

Forty years of building the rooms other people eat in, and the status never converted.

A child raised inside that arrangement learns something specific about how a life can go. You can work the whole time, be good at the work, be loved, and still never quite arrive. For a Seven, whose core dread is a future with the supply cut off, that is what the dread looks like up close: a life that stays provisional no matter how much you put into it.

The eating makes sense from there. So does the volume, the yes-to-everything schedule, the four careers running at once. A man who watched his father's forty years stay unconverted does not build one thing. He builds several, and he owns them.

And the meal ticket did pay out. It paid out in a currency nobody in that house would have accepted as an offer: no law degree, no salary, a podcast so vile he assumed it would end his prospects. Thirty thousand dollars, one year, and a house.

## The Ten Years Stavros Halkias Spent in Baltimore Bars

That payout took ten years to arrive.

He started in college, at UMBC, where he hosted a monthly showcase and finished a few credits short of a degree. In 2012 Magooby's Joke House named him Baltimore's New Comedian of the Year. That is the last piece of official recognition he got for roughly a decade.

The DMV circuit in those years was bars, back rooms and drive time. He worked it. He also took three amateur kickboxing fights while still doing stand-up, then stopped. His reason, on the Joe Rogan Experience in December 2023: "I wasn't all in in fighting or comedy."

The line that gives the decade away is one he delivers as a joke and clearly does not experience as one. "I quit being an after-school tutor in Baltimore City to focus on open mics," he said on Are You Garbage in June 2020. "By definition, I'm a garbage human being."

That is the meal ticket leaving a job teaching kids in his own city to go tell jokes about his penis in bars while the man who left Greece watched.

Then 2020 arrived and the whole thing stalled at once.

"During the pandemic I had gained a ton of fucking weight and I felt like my career stalled, everything," he said on the Joe Rogan Experience. "I just went to Baltimore, I rented a place, I worked out with my brother, and I was off my phone. I was just recharging."

<p class="inner-thought">

</p>

Somewhere in there a friend handed him a Murakami book. He has since said Japanese storytelling still takes him back to that period, which he calls "that healing point in my life." Fifteen months later he walked away from the biggest thing he had.

## Why Stavros Halkias Left the Podcast That Made Him

_Cum Town_ was three friends being deliberately vile into microphones starting in 2016, and it was enormous. It was also, by Halkias's accounting, the escape route.

"I did a dumb podcast with my friends that I was sure was going to fail," he told Slate. "It took me out of poverty. I didn't give a fuck if there were going to be think pieces written about me."

Everyone arguing about that show skips that sentence. He was getting out. In October 2021 he put the same logic on X, aimed at people chasing institutional comedy jobs: "I feel bad for the talented people w/no connections or safety net that have had to work way harder... My advice: start a bad podcast that makes u rich."

He built the exit before he took it. On June 5, 2022, he put _Live at the Lodge Room_ up himself, free, on his own YouTube channel. No distributor, no gatekeeper, no revenue share. It hit two million views in three weeks.

Twenty days after it went up, he announced he was done with the podcast. He gave no reason, only thanks: "As much as we joked about hating the fans and the podcast itself, the show changed my life and I'll always be grateful for that." Nick Mullen posted a public farewell the same day: "A heartfelt bon voyage to @stavvybaby, off to bigger and better things."

The four years since sort cleanly by one question, which is who owned the thing. _Stavvy's World_, his own show, launched in 2022. Netflix put out _Fat Rascal_ in December 2023. In 2024 he co-wrote and starred in _Let's Start a Cult_ with director Ben Kitnick, a feature made for under a million dollars that Dark Sky Films put in theaters on October 24, 2024, and that Deadline reported Hulu buying that January for a February 4 streaming premiere. Only after all of that come the parts someone else cast him in: a main role in Netflix's _Tires_ opposite [Shane Gillis](/personality-analysis/shane-gillis) through a third-season renewal, and Casey the cop in Yorgos Lanthimos's _Bugonia_.

He is not solemn about the last one. Asked about working with the most acclaimed Greek director alive, he told Slate: "Yeah, my great-grandfather owed his great-grandfather a goat, and now we're even."

Sevens are usually read as people who bail when a thing gets boring. The podcast had converted from a lifeline into a room with a fixed shape, and he had just spent a pandemic learning what it felt like to not be inside one. He left at the peak and took the audience with him.

Sam Morril, a friend and fellow comic, described the underlying machinery to Baltimore Magazine in September 2025: "A lot of people think just being funny is enough. It's not. You really have to work at it, and he does. He's got every quality you would want in a comedian. He's hilarious. He's a generous laugher. He writes great jokes."

_A generous laugher._ It is the most useful two words anyone has said about him. Sevens are the type that gives laughs away, because a room where everyone is having a good time is a room where the supply is obviously not running out.

## Why Stavros Halkias Is Losing the Weight His Act Is Built On

The one thing that got him into those rooms is not in the inventory. He carries it.

He was bullied for his size as a kid. He has spent his adult life getting paid for it.

The body is the premise of the act. The Netflix hour is called _Fat Rascal_. The persona is a man with no brakes, and the audience buys a ticket to watch someone eat, drink and say the thing they would not. Gluttony is the [Type 7](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-7) passion in the literal, technical sense, and he is the rare person whose brand is a one-word match for it.

Then he started subtracting.

He finished a tour at 290 pounds, under a goal he had set at 300, and told Baltimore Magazine in September 2025 that it was "the first tour of my life where I lost weight." The same profile catches him in the act of maintaining it: in his own city, on a night off, walking to Fells Point for salmon at Sal & Sons instead of pit beef and fries. He has kept a public count going on TikTok, where he posted "I lost 31.5 lbs last season, but can I keep the momentum going on tour?"

Do not resolve this too quickly. The tidy version says the weight loss is growth and the eating was the wound, which flatters everyone and explains nothing. The harder reading is that both are the same instinct.

The appetite was never really about food. It was about supply, and about a house where the supply was thinner than the kid was told. He did not stop wanting in his thirties. The body just became the one input he could still meter once everything else had gone unlimited: the tour, the money, the calendar, the yeses. A Seven who has finally secured the supply has to find something to ration, or the fear comes back with nothing to attach to.

And then he did the most Seven thing available. He turned the rationing into material, and named the tour he is on right now Dreamboat.

## Why Stavros Halkias Won't Cut Off His Right-Wing Friends

He can subtract from his own plate. There is one subtraction he refuses, and it is the fair place to push on him. The sharpest version of the charge comes from a reporter who covers the comedy industry for a living.

In October 2025, Seth Simons wrote at Humorism that "Halkias's politics contain an enormous carveout for his Nazi friends." Simons grants the sincerity first: "He positions himself as a woke dirtbag, and in almost every aspect of his public life he appears to live up to that positioning." Then he lands it. When Halkias suggests that his friends in the [Andrew Schulz](/personality-analysis/andrew-schulz) and Gillis orbit are playing characters or bending to career pressure, Simons calls it what it looks like: "Halkias is completely delusional here: podcasters in the Gillis and Schulz crowd are not putting on personas when they enthuse over Donald Trump."

Simons is right about the effect. A popular leftist comic vouching for the good character of the biggest right-wing podcasters in the country provides cover, and no amount of sincerity elsewhere cancels that.

That cover is being provided by a man who hedges nothing else. In the same weeks that piece ran, Halkias was working [Zohran Mamdani's](/personality-analysis/zohran-mamdani) mayoral campaign in New York. The Baltimore Sun covered it on October 29, 2025: the "New York Is Not for Sale" rally in Queens alongside [Bernie Sanders](/personality-analysis/bernie-sanders) and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, Mamdani called a leader "for the people," an early-voting sticker posted with instructions to go do likewise. He has said comedy's job right now is "to stop fucking doing propaganda, to stop being a useful idiot for a horrible administration." He says the loud part with his name on it. He just will not do the subtraction.

The harder charge is the one Simons never makes. Before the friendships there was the material. _Cum Town_ spent six years resurrecting long-deprecated slurs and passing racialized mock accents around a table, and Halkias was one of the three men doing it. Asked in 2025 whether any of it weighs on him, he declines to perform contrition: "Saying some fucked-up jokes did not weigh on me at all. But also, I do think it's really important for younger people to get fucked-up jokes out of their system in a healthy way." Then he gives Slate the sentence that is either the entire defense or the entire problem: "We had it good when it was comedians pretending to be Nazis as opposed to actual Nazis with the levers of power."

He does draw a line sometimes, and where he draws it is the tell. In September 2025, on episode 145 of _Stavvy's World_, his friend and fellow comic Jordan Jensen reached for an anti-trans slur and he pushed back in real time. The clip went everywhere. Slate put it to him a month later and he did not dress it up: "I was fatigued. I just saw it as my friend bringing up a hack topic." The carveout runs on a rule about venue. He will do it to your face, on his own platform, on the record. What he will not do is remove a man from his life by press release.

The reason is in how he describes the run itself. "Everything was house money to me," he told Slate in October 2025. A man who describes his career as winnings behaves like someone who got waved into a room he had no business being in. The tutoring job he quit. The dumb podcast he was sure would fail. Every good thing in his life arrived through a door somebody else held open for a fat kid from Greektown with no connections and no safety net, which is his own phrasing for what he lacked.

Being asked to close that door on people is being asked to become the one thing his whole biography is organized against. So he takes the hypocrisy charge instead, which costs him articles and costs him nothing else.

That trade is worth naming plainly. It is cheaper for him than for the people the carveout protects, and Simons's point stands. The behavior stops being mysterious the moment you quit looking at their politics and start looking at the door.

## The Rug They Gave Him at The Lyric

He was supposed to tape the special in April 2026. He broke his arm in a scooter accident and pushed it four months.

In July he did it at The Lyric, across four shows, in Baltimore. The material was what it always is. His weight. His years of partying. Being a test-tube baby. Being a deadbeat uncle. The City-Poly football game. The accent, worn on purpose, in front of the only audience that would clock the difference.

Then the venue gave him a rug. The Baltimore Sun ran the picture on July 20, 2026: Halkias holding a commemorative rug for the more than 25,000 tickets he has sold in that building over the years.

A rug is what you put down in a room when you intend to be in it for a while. He drove it back to a rowhome in Canton, two miles from Ponca Street. His father's booths are still holding up the lunch rush in Greek diners for a hundred miles in every direction, and not one of those rooms is his.

Forty years and no citizenship for the man who built the rooms. Thirty-seven years and a rug for the son who talks in them.

He kept it.
