"It's a running joke in my family that I was born in a bad mood," Drew Afualo told Rolling Stone's CT Jones in 2024 ([Rolling Stone, July 22, 2024](https://www.rollingstone.com/culture/culture-features/drew-afualo-comment-section-loud-comedy-1235064190/)).

The family has a story to back up the joke. When she was in school, a boy slapped her older sister Deison on the butt. Afualo jumped him in the hallway and was sent home. By her account, when her mother picked her up, she was punished not for the fight but because so many people were around to see it. "We're very family-oriented," she explained. "We care a lot about taking care of each other and making sure we're all good."

Years later she built a career on the same reflex: finding men on TikTok who post contempt for women and taking them apart, often with a joke about their hairline. Men have called her fat and ugly every way the comment box allows. She says it doesn't touch her.

Something else does. "Do my feelings get hurt when you call me a fat buffalo a million times? No," she told Refinery29 in 2022. "But when I have women coming after me, that's the only kind of hate that really hurts me because I feel like I'm on your side, girl. I'm trying really hard to fight for you. That's the only time that I don't know if I want to do this anymore." ([Refinery29, May 11, 2022](https://www.refinery29.com/en-us/2022/05/10959441/drew-afualo-tiktok-nfl-interview))

That split is the key to her. The enemy's insults barely reach her. Her own side can.

Born September 18, 1995 ([her book's opening](https://academic.macmillan.com/academictrade/9780374614058/loud/); [27th-birthday TikTok](https://www.tiktok.com/@drewafualo/video/7144785755565493547)), Afualo grew up in Southern California, the middle child of Tait and Noelle Afualo in a Samoan family. Her mother worked in PR while her father, cut by the Arizona Cardinals after two years because of an injury, stayed home with the kids. He felt cheated by the NFL, she said. So when she landed a social-media job there after the University of Hawaiʻi, part of her thought, "I'm going to do this for us."

She was let go in February 2020, three days after the Super Bowl ([Rolling Stone](https://www.rollingstone.com/culture/culture-features/drew-afualo-comment-section-loud-comedy-1235064190/)).

She joined TikTok that March. A video listing "very specific red flags" in men went viral in early 2021, and her replies to the hate comments became the show ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drew_Afualo)). Then came the podcast _The Comment Section_ and the New York Times bestseller _Loud_ (July 30, 2024). She had more than 8 million TikTok followers in December 2024 ([PEOPLE via AOL](https://www.aol.com/drew-afualo-tearfully-announces-shes-174701231.html)). In August 2026 she announced she had married Pili Tanuvasa in Hawaii ([Wedded Wonderland](https://weddedwonderland.com/trending/drew-afualo-wedding-was-a-tropical-dream)).

## What is Drew Afualo's personality type?

Drew Afualo is an Enneagram Type 8, the Challenger. Her anger goes straight into action, she has stood up for people who won't swing back since grade school, and she says misogynists listen only when forced. Insults from enemies rarely get through her armor. Criticism from the women she fights for does.

This is a reading of her public words and choices, not a diagnosis. Most of the evidence is her own account, and it is labeled that way below.

## Anger first, and in the body

The Enneagram sorts the nine types into three centers. Head types (5, 6, 7) run on fear, heart types (2, 3, 4) on shame, and gut types (8, 9, 1) on anger. A [Nine](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-9) numbs it, a [One](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-1) turns it into correction, and an [Eight](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-8) lets it out.

Afualo lets it out, and fast. In a January 2026 conversation with Kyle MacLachlan on his podcast _What Are We Even Doing?_, she said that when she is disrespected she bristles and reacts immediately, and that she has always been that way ([YouTube, January 8, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DQWsOCaPQvQ)). In 2022 she told PAPER: "I just had no fear of danger or consequences, but now that I'm older it's a little different." Then: "I've just always been very passionate about sticking up for others." ([PAPER, June 8, 2022](https://www.papermag.com/drew-afualo-tiktok-2657470726.html))

The hallway fight alone proves little; plenty of kids would defend a sister. What makes it count is that she describes the same reflex across her life, starting before anyone was watching. She calls the headstrong child she was "Baby Drew." "I've always been so strong-willed. I'm a Virgo, so I was bossy. As a child, I was a know-it-all," she told the New York Times ([New York Times, May 31, 2022](https://www.nytimes.com/2022/05/31/style/drew-afualo-tiktok.html)).

Here is how an Eight works, step by step, in her record.

**First, she spots someone using power on someone who can't fight back.** She traces this to Deison, who she told MacLachlan was quiet, trusting and bullied a lot; Afualo grew up protecting her and people like her ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DQWsOCaPQvQ)). In 2022 she told HuffPost she detests when people "pick on others who would never swing." ([HuffPost via Yahoo, September 6, 2022](https://ca.news.yahoo.com/tiktoker-drew-afualo-knows-exactly-140000886.html))

**Second, she hits back right away, and harder than she was hit.** On Maggie Winters's podcast in February 2026, she described answering a man who had insulted her looks with a joke about his chest and, by her count, about nine other insults. She called it mean and also hilarious, and said that anyone who comes after her had better be able to finish the job ([YouTube, February 4, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2omT91twB0E)).

**Third, she keeps her own hurt away from opponents.** She learned that at home.

**Fourth, she decides when the force comes out.** She has worked on that step most, and it comes last.

She credits culture for the first two steps. Samoan culture, she told MacLachlan, is built on service, giving without expecting anything back, and her large family closes ranks when anyone comes at one of them. In Samoan families, she told WIRED, "one of our love languages is making fun of each other. It's roasting each other... Quite frankly, that's how I got good at it." ([WIRED, July 26, 2024](https://www.wired.com/story/dial-up-drew-afualo/))

Culture explains the skills. It does not explain the heat. Her mother, she said, is just like her, fiery and protective. Her father is gentle and patient, and her sister, raised in the same house, is very reserved and very quiet. Only some of them came out swinging.

## "My job is not to educate you"

Most online activism tries to persuade. Afualo does not. "My content is not for you. It's at your expense. My job is not to educate you. It's to humiliate you," she said in a TikTok the New York Times quoted in 2022, noting she finished with "a gleeful cackle."

Her reason is about power, not manners. She laid it out for WIRED's Jason Parham in 2024:

> "A misogynist is never going to see me as an equal, let alone listen to me, right? The only time they do listen is by force."

That is an Eight's theory of conflict. Respect is not earned by being reasonable. It is taken.

It is a claim about misogynists, not men. When men tell her she must hate them because she had a bad father, she told MacLachlan, she answers that her father was wonderful and that she was raised by many kind, supportive men.

Asked why she gets called a bully, she told PAPER that freedom of speech "does not mean freedom from consequences." Then, with what the columnist called a sly smile: "And I'm a tangible consequence."

Her signature move is to take the sting out of an insult by owning it. "If I take the power from a word, it can't hurt me," she told Rolling Stone. Laughing at insults, she told WIRED, "has taken all of the power away from them and given it back to myself." The word she keeps reaching for is power.

The method draws a real charge. At its strongest: a body-positive creator who mocks men's height, hairlines and chests repeats the body-based ridicule she opposes, and points millions of viewers at individual men. The chest roast above set off one such campaign, she told Winters: another creator argued that mocking a concave chest hurts men who have that disability or deformity. She answered that her target did not have it.

Her general reply, to Insider in 2022: "You made a joke and I made a joke, but somehow mine's mean, and yours isn't? OK." ([Insider, January 6, 2022](https://www.businessinsider.com/misogynists-delete-their-tiktoks-after-drew-afualo-comes-for-them-2021-12)) Whether roasting a stranger's body is fair play is a real argument. In the interviews this profile opened, from 2022 to 2026, she has not conceded it, though she calls some of her own lines mean. That fits the pattern and does not settle the argument.

## Skin like armor

The fearless version of Afualo was built, and she says so.

"When I was younger, I used to be very sensitive. I'm still pretty sensitive, contrary to popular belief," she told WIRED. "Even the most lighthearted, silly joke that my family would make, I would get so angry, frustrated and so emotional." Her mother told her that if she wanted to tell jokes, she had to learn to take one.

The family then made a bit of it, she told MacLachlan: nobody make fun of Drew, she doesn't like it. It gave her FOMO. She wanted to be in on the laughing. She called getting over it an ego thing and said her skin is now like vibranium, though a good insult has nicked her now and then, and she respects the art when it does.

Notice the order. The hurt came out as anger first. Then she trained the hurt out of view.

"Even if I did feel emotional about something, I'll cry in the privacy of my own home. That could be me being a Virgo or mentally ill," she told Elite Daily in 2025 ([Elite Daily, August 26, 2025](https://www.elitedaily.com/entertainment/drew-afualo-tiktok-book-wedding-interview)). When she has shown the hurt in public, as with Refinery29 and in the tearful break video of December 2024, it came from her own side.

That is the Eight's guarded heart. The armor faces outward, and the heart stays open to the circle she protects. So the injury that lands is the people she shields turning on her, which feels like betrayal. A Two would hear the same criticism as ingratitude and work to win them back. Her fights with her own side, below, test which it is.

Rolling Stone's Jones saw both sides. At an axe-throwing range with her mother and sister, Jones wrote, Afualo seemed "less concerned with an interview than with making her sister smile." The first time they met, at a Forbes party, Afualo "yelled at an entire room full of influencers to 'shut the fuck up'" when they interrupted Jones's friend's speech. That is an outside witness to the same move off camera.

The heat has also landed on the people it protects. In _Loud_, Rolling Stone wrote, she is "the sister who regrets being upset when Deison came out to her" and "the daughter who is conscious of how her fury made childhood difficult." That is the Eight's usual cost: force meant for outsiders spills onto the inner circle.

Threats to herself, she absorbs. In 2021 came rape and death threats aimed at the people around her. "Threatening my life is one thing, but threatening the lives of people I love was really the only thing that made me feel like, 'I don't know if I can do this.'"

That was the other time she nearly quit, and it was a different kind of hurt: not wounded feelings but danger to her family. An Eight's line is drawn around her people. Cross it, and the fight stops being fun.

She jokes about grudges, too. On Nicole Byer's podcast in May 2025 she called her romance with Tanuvasa an enemies-to-lovers arc: she stopped speaking to him for about two years over something she can no longer remember, and held on to it anyway ([YouTube, May 9, 2025](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mM7FvATy35g)). She told it for laughs.

## When her own side pushes back

A woman outside her camp got the troll treatment: when anti-feminist YouTuber Pearl Davis compared her to Fiona from _Shrek_ in July 2024, Afualo and Tefi Pessoa roasted Davis back ([The Mary Sue, July 16, 2024](https://www.themarysue.com/what-is-going-on-between-drew-afualo-and-pearl-davis-explained/)). Two fights, in 2022 and 2026, show what happens when the criticism comes from inside. They end differently.

**Shein, 2022.** On March 16, 2022, she posted a paid haul from Shein's SheinX line with a discount code ([Daily Dot, April 7, 2022](https://dailydot.com/tiktok-influencer-drew-afualo-shein)). Shein has been accused of exploiting its workers, and some of her own followers objected. TikToker Alice March put it plainly: "Promoting any fast-fashion brand goes directly against what your TikToks are about." ([The Independent via Yahoo, April 11, 2022](https://www.yahoo.com/news/influencer-drew-afualo-faces-backlash-160454981.html))

Afualo replied in her comments that shopping sustainably is "not a privilege afforded to all" and that plus-size women "deserve to dress cute too." On Twitter she wrote: "Watching thin women try to call my feminism performative after seeing one brand deal they don't like, truly is so rotten."

The Daily Dot reported that a tweet telling her to "keep [her] foot on their necks" appeared in her likes. Climate activist Karishma Porwal said she got messages telling her to kill herself for criticizing Afualo, and that Afualo blocked her; nothing in the coverage ties those messages to Afualo. Porwal later apologized for "speaking for" plus-size women like Afualo and assuming her "financial situation," while keeping her objection to fast fashion. No apology from Afualo was reported in the coverage this profile opened.

There is a real point in her defense: plus-size women have fewer cheap options, and she was speaking for them. But look at the shape of the response. Her own camp pushed, and she answered with force instead of repair. "Truly is so rotten" is a hurt line, delivered as a counterpunch: the childhood order again.

**E.R. Fightmaster, 2026.** On July 24, 2026, Afualo shared a clip from an April 2, 2025 episode of _The Comment Section_ ([PinkNews, August 11, 2026](https://www.thepinknews.com/2026/08/11/drew-afualo-er-fightmaster-trans-womanhood/)). The full interview covered a lot, including fighting back against TERFs, the trans-exclusionary radical feminists who reject trans women as women. The viral clip was 40 seconds. In it her guest, the nonbinary actor E.R. Fightmaster, said: "My truest thought on this is I think trans women have a more beautiful understanding of what womanhood is." PinkNews reported that Afualo agreed throughout.

Right-wing MAGA accounts recirculated it in bad faith, them reported, and many cis women stitched it, reposting it with their own reactions. Some took it to mean trans women are "better than" cis women and asked for an apology to cis women; some answered with anti-trans talking points ([them, August 14, 2026](https://www.them.us/entertainment/celebrity/controversy-drew-afualo-er-fightmaster)).

On August 9 (PinkNews says August 10) she hit back at the critics she called TERFs: "You guys saw one alt-right MAGA-loving creator make a video espousing TERF rhetoric and now you want to get clicks and likes, too." She restated the point herself: "The conversation being had is about how trans women most oftentimes identity with femininity and womanhood sometimes even better than cis women do." The rebuttals about periods and childbirth, she said, were "bigoted and transphobic."

Then trans women creators said the conversation had been about them without one of them in the room. Selyna Brillare added that such talk can "throw a jab at cisgender women as if we're in competition with them." Zaya Perysian opened hers with: "She has stood ten toes down for my community."

On August 12 Afualo posted again:

> "At the end of this entire discourse, trans women are being harmed and targeted and I am the metaphorical gun pointing at them."

The critique that "there should have been a trans woman in the room if you're going to talk about trans women's experiences" was "fair to say." She was "so incredibly sorry to the trans women who are now being targeted because of this entire discourse."

Two responses, days apart. To the critics she saw as attackers, many of them cis women, a counterpunch. To the trans women taking the blows, a concession. As of them's August 14, 2026 report, she had not apologized to the critics she called TERFs. This profile does not judge whether the apology was sincere.

In 2022 she held her ground against her own side. In 2026 she conceded to part of it. The constant is who she thinks is being hurt: her people are whoever is taking the hits. A Two could draw the same line, so this episode doesn't separate the types. It explains the sequence. It does not excuse what she conceded: a discussion of trans women's experience with no trans woman in it.

## The break

On December 9, 2024, Afualo posted a tearful video saying she was stepping away from TikTok. It is her most exposed public moment, and it cuts against the Eight reading. It was not, she said, "because of hate I receive from bigots or misogynists." The problem was the people she fights for:

> "My validation now is no longer coming from me. It feels like I am consistently pursuing it from people who love me or care about me or are in the groups that I feel so driven to protect. And that again is an unhealthy and unsustainable way to view myself. Because now I feel as if I am constantly trying to fit into everybody's idea of perfection."

She had been "violently ill," but she couldn't stop "because I never want anyone to feel like I abandoned them." Now, "I need to protect myself too because I matter, too."

"I found the exit. I've opened the door and I'll hold it open," she said. "But at the same time, I'm also getting burned from both sides." ([PEOPLE via AOL, December 10, 2024](https://www.aol.com/drew-afualo-tearfully-announces-shes-174701231.html))

The break lasted about seven weeks. "I feel like myself again," she told BuzzFeed in May 2025 ([BuzzFeed, May 9, 2025](https://www.buzzfeed.com/morgansloss1/drew-afualo-update-mental-health)).

Read as an Eight, the door-holder is a guard, holding the exit for others while the fire burns her. But "validation" and "perfection" are her words too, and they point toward another type.

## Why not Type 2?

The [Type 2](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-2), the Helper, is the strongest rival, and the break video is its best evidence. A Two earns love by giving, and the Two's injury is giving everything and hearing it wasn't enough. "I consistently do not feel like enough for the groups that I feel so driven to protect," Afualo said.

The theme runs further back. Explaining to WIRED why men's insults can't hurt her, she said: "Learning to laugh at it means that you no longer draw validation from the opinions of men who are far beneath you anymore. And so if you don't draw validation from them, their words can't hurt you." She told MacLachlan that in her angriest arguments with men in her early twenties, what she wanted underneath was for them to see her, hear her and validate her.

Her daily affirmations, as she listed them for the New York Times: "I say that I'm worthy," she said. "That I'm valid. That I'm a good person. That I'm going to be successful because I'm a good person. And that I'm worthy of all the success that I'm having."

All of that is real. An exhausted Eight and an exhausted Two can both say "I'm not enough." The difference is what each does with anger.

The serious rival is not a soft Two but an aggressive one, who fights hard for her own. Her anger defends the relationship and her place in it, and when her own side criticizes her, she moves toward them to repair it. A Two under stress can also look like an Eight for a while.

Afualo's record breaks that pattern in three places. Her anger came first, before anyone was watching or rewarding it: "Baby Drew," "bossy," "a know-it-all," the hallway, "no fear of danger or consequences." That is a baseline, not a stress state.

When her own followers turned on her over Shein, she did not repair. She told them they were wrong.

And when the validation-chasing broke her in 2024, she did not give more. She left, saying she mattered too. Even her validation lines describe needing it as the problem: something she learned to stop wanting from men, and then caught herself wanting from her own side. "No longer coming from me" assumes it once did.

## What the Eight doesn't explain

Some of her record fits neither type cleanly.

The first is an achiever streak. "One thing I held a lot of my worth and value in was my accomplishments," she told Wondermind in 2023. Her last job before TikTok, which by the dates was the NFL one, "was the first job that made me feel like I was not a good worker, like I was not smart." ([Wondermind, April 3, 2023](https://www.wondermind.com/article/drew-afualo/))

In the break video she said getting fired "felt like it proved to me that I was not enough." The success half of her affirmations points the same way. That is the language of a [Type 3](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-3), the Achiever, though she frames it as a past self she worked on in therapy, and she tied that job to her family as much as to herself.

She likes attention and says so: "Clearly you have to love attention to some extent to do this for a living," she told Rolling Stone. But when her image was attacked, in 2022 and again on August 9, 2026, her first move was to defend her position, not reshape it.

The second is fear. "I'm a Virgo. So I worry for a living, too," she told WIRED, about losing her platform. A counterphobic [Type 6](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6), the kind who charges straight at what scares her, would fit a story she told Byer. On a high-school date with a boy who had lied to her, she rode the roller coaster she had always been too afraid of, because she was angry and knew he wouldn't follow.

But in that story the anger came first, and the fear lost. Her fights are about who is being pushed around, not about finding safety.

She also reminds people that the intensity is partly a job. "I'm a lot more laid back than people think I am," she told the New York Times. "I'm only like that because that's what I do — react to that terrible content." That is why this profile leans on what she did before the job existed, and off camera.

## "But I get to decide"

"Having no fear is not a good thing," she told Rolling Stone in 2024. "As I've gotten older, I've realized that not everything is worth that type of rage. It doesn't serve me. Does that mean I won't fight? No. If I have to turn into the Incredible Hulk then I will. But I get to decide."

An Eight that grows doesn't give up the force. It takes charge of it. Her parents, she told MacLachlan, saw early that her energy could be used for evil too, and set out to aim it. The girl in the hallway had no off switch. The woman in that interview keeps the switch.

The armor is real, but it was built. What she has, in her own account, is a decision she keeps making: to stand in front of someone, and to choose when to swing.
