Ella Beatty beat the reporter to the Carlyle.

Vogue's Elise Taylor arrived at the Manhattan hotel at 2:58 p.m. for a 3 p.m. coffee in February 2024, expecting to wait. Beatty was already there, her coat neatly folded over her arm, following one of her father Warren Beatty's cardinal rules: "Always be on time." In her case, early. Two and a half years later she would be the title star of Netflix's _Monster: The Lizzie Borden Story_.

Then, before Taylor could ask, Beatty raised the subject every story about her would raise. "I totally acknowledge that there is an inherent privilege and exposure that comes from having well-known actors as parents," she told [Vogue](https://www.vogue.com/article/meet-ella-beatty-the-breakout-star-of-feud-whos-now-heading-to-broadway). "And I really hope that I can offer something meaningful." Taylor's words for her were "Exceptionally poised."

Sarah Paulson, who starred opposite her on Broadway in _Appropriate_, told [Town & Country](https://www.townandcountrymag.com/leisure/arts-and-culture/a73571951/ella-beatty-lizzie-borden-interview-2026/) in September 2026, "There is something unnervingly, dazzlingly self-assured about Ella."

Beatty describes both. She told [Variety](https://variety.com/2026/tv/features/monster-lizzie-borden-ella-beatty-1236866041/) that month that she made her audition tape thinking, "I understand this person." Then: "When I found out that I had gotten the part, I was over the moon and also really scared."

She is the youngest of four children of Annette Bening and Warren Beatty, raised in Los Angeles, where she did summer-camp and community theater as a child. She trained at Juilliard from 2018 to 2022 and, as she told Paulson, "didn't work professionally at all until I graduated from acting school." Since January 2024 she has gone from FX's _Feud: Capote vs. The Swans_ to Broadway, replacing Elle Fanning in _Appropriate_; to her 2025 film debut in _If I Had Legs I'd Kick You_, Ibsen's _Ghosts_ at Lincoln Center Theater and _Sexual Misconduct of the Middle Classes_, a two-hander opposite [Hugh Jackman](/personality-analysis/hugh-jackman); and to the lead in _Act One_, which premiered at Tribeca in June 2026.

_Monster_, Ryan Murphy and Ian Brennan's Netflix anthology, premiered on September 17, 2026. The real Lizzie Borden was [tried and acquitted](https://www.netflix.com/tudum/features/ella-beatty-monster-lizzie-borden-story) in 1893 of murdering her father and stepmother, and the series is an interpretation. In its second week it drew 14.5 million views and topped Netflix's global English-language TV list, [TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/creative-content/tv-shows/monster-the-lizzie-borden-story-ratings-viewership-week-2-netflix/) reported. She also appears in Sarah Polley's film of _The Bell Jar_, opposite [Billie Eilish](/personality-analysis/billie-eilish), which had no release date as of October 18, 2026.

Since the premiere, people have been looking her up. They find a mostly promotional record that begins in January 2024, with a gap running through it: colleagues describe composure, while she names fear beside the excitement. This is an open case: a leading read held loosely, the alternatives beside it, and what would decide between them.

## What is Ella Beatty's personality type?

Leading read: Enneagram Type 6, held loosely at low confidence. Type 9 is the main alternative; Types 2 and 3 remain in play. She talks about responsibility (to her castmates, her family's legacy, the real Lizzie Borden) and meets it by showing up off book. Her public record starts in 2024, so nothing yet separates these reads.

Those four types span all three of the Enneagram's triads. A [Type 6](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6) is in the head triad, whose core emotion is fear, and looks for ground to trust in preparation, allies and the people in charge. A [Type 9](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-9) is in the body triad, whose anger stays out of sight, and keeps the peace. [Type 2](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-2) and [Type 3](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-3) share the heart triad, where the emotion is shame. Twos secure a place by being needed, Threes by succeeding.

Of those three emotions, fear is the one she names outright, usually when asked about pressure and usually beside excitement or luck. That is why Six leads, and why it can't lead by much: fear is also the ordinary weather of an actor in her first lead.

## The name, and the tapes

She has never pretended the name is irrelevant. In a Zoom conversation recorded in May 2025 and published by [Interview](https://www.interviewmagazine.com/theater/meet-ella-beatty-daughter-of-hollywood-royalty-and-sarah-paulsons-kindred-spirit) that June, Paulson asked whether she had ever thought she shouldn't use her last name. Beatty said no.

> "It really never occurred to me until I started working. I do feel a kind of responsibility towards an artistic legacy that I don't feel ashamed of... I'd hope that my work would be enough to make me separate, but I also understand why people do that."

The answer hasn't changed shape since Vogue: name the advantage, don't argue with it, promise work in return. It is also the answer any famous child would be advised to give after the "nepo baby" backlash, so on its own it cannot show motive.

The casting stories come from people who are not neutral. For _Appropriate_, Paulson and her co-star Michael Esper read with several actresses. "When you walked out, Michael and I turned to each other and we're like, 'It's got to be Ella Beatty,'" Paulson recalled in that conversation, in which she calls Beatty "a person I'm wildly close to." She also told her, "I had no idea who your parents were."

_Monster_ came from a self-tape. Max Winkler, who directed six of the eight episodes, told [ScreenRant](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RooR7nJOmiM) that after watching a tape from casting director Tiffany Little Canfield he called Murphy and said, "We got her." Murphy watched all seven tapes without being told which one, then called back and asked, "Is it Ella?" Both men are selling the show, and she had already worked for Murphy on _Feud_, so no one can measure what the name did before the tape. The accounts agree that a tape was required. No opened source describes how she was cast in _Feud_, her first job.

"I really care what they think and feel," she told Town & Country of her parents, "and I think they have an awareness of that and they're good at staying out of it." Whose approval matters is where the live types would part, and the line doesn't say which.

## "Really scared," then off book

_Monster_ shows the full pattern. "I felt a huge responsibility to the part, to the anthology, to the real person," she told Variety. At Netflix's September 9 press conference, [reported by Showbiz Junkies](https://www.showbizjunkies.com/tv/netflix-monster-lizzie-borden-cast-press-conference/), she said, "I was so excited. I was over the moon. And then I also felt, 'How am I gonna do this and what does it mean?'" So she started before pre-production, because that work "can help you feel like you're starting." She told [Collider](https://collider.com/monster-lizzie-borden-story-netflix-ella-beatty/) that she "listened to every single podcast and video that's ever been published in the world. I couldn't be stopped if I had a WiFi connection." Then she narrowed to "what was on the page."

_Appropriate_ was the same move under more pressure. She told her friend the acting teacher Anthony Meindl, on his [_In The Moment_ podcast](https://soundcloud.com/anthonymeindl/anthonyinterviewsellabeatty) in February 2025, that she had about two days of table work before taking over, learned the blocking from a video and the understudies, and "came in off book, so that would never be an issue." She opened after a few days of tech at the Belasco Theatre. The director, Lila Neugebauer, told her from the start to find her own version. "Because I felt that assurance from her going into it, I didn't feel a pressure to replicate anybody else's," Beatty said.

First she removed the risk she could control. Then she took permission from the person in charge. Then she brought herself.

On nights off from _Sexual Misconduct of the Middle Classes_ in 2025, she missed the play. "Sometimes we have four or five days between performances and I get really nervous," she told Paulson, so she took home the director's "diary book" from rehearsals. Her approach as a newcomer, she told Town & Country, was to "sit very quietly, watch, and pray, and then work extremely hard and show up off-book."

The record pushes back too. Asked by Paulson what pressure she had felt coming into _Appropriate_, she named other people before fear: "The pressure that I felt had to do with the responsibility to honor what the other actors are doing. You don't want to leave people in the lurch." Later in the same answer: "I didn't have a lot of time to be scared because I got the job and then we started." The people who work with her see composure. Jackman told Town & Country she is "always prepared, kind, funny, and fearless." Paulson's word is "self-possessed."

Why the fear doesn't show is a question the record leaves open. In a Six reading, preparation is the reason: by the time anyone sees her, the risk she could control is gone. In a Nine reading, the fear is real but goes quiet once the work absorbs her.

## No time to worry

Talking to Meindl during _Ghosts_ rehearsals, she found an upside in the rush of the _Appropriate_ takeover. "Part of what was so great about it is that I didn't have time to worry about it," she said. "I didn't really have time to get self-conscious or, um, you know, self-involved about it." She had "so many things to serve and so many people to please." Then she turned Meindl's point about wasted worry on herself:

> "Being present and kind of being where your feet are, it's something I try to try really hard and fail at often frequently to live by. It's just like, you know, so much of what we worry about in life, so many of the things that we are so, so emotionally fraught about, you know, you can't handle until you're there. And I think trusting yourself to have, you know, the tools and the ability to handle it when you're faced with it is, um, something I'm still really trying to do. And God knows that was something I did in that play."

Read as a Six, that is the type's growth task in her own words: learning to trust that she can handle what she fears before it arrives. Read as a Nine, the telling detail comes first. The rush felt good because it left no room for her. Read as a Two, the clause that matters sits in between: "so many people to please."

Credit runs the same way. "I feel like the luckiest lady in the world," she told [Entertainment Tonight](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DdDLa9vctOA) before the premiere. Of every project she has done, she told Meindl, "there's a part of me that feels like, Oh my God, I can't believe they picked me." Asked by Town & Country how she had changed, she flagged the reflex first: "I'll answer authentically even though I want to deflect." That fits a Nine's self-discounting or a Six's expectation of the worse outcome better than a Three's ownership of results.

## The people she calls

She keeps a small, close circle. Asked on [TODAY](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Q6n83ZQZX2w) in September 2026 how Paulson supports her, she said, "We call every 15 minutes," and that Paulson is "there for advice at any minute." With her mother it's "once a day, debrief," she told [Drew Barrymore](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GYkLldtGQ3s), adding that her mother is "big on not giving you the answer." She ties the circle to pressure most clearly around auditions, telling Meindl that it helps to have "a friend to call to be like, Oh my God, that audition was a nightmare."

Winkler sees a version of this from the outside. "There's a wide-eyedness to Ella that can sometimes conceal how devastatingly perceptive she is," he told Town & Country. "She is completely tuned into what everybody else in the room is doing." Each live type can claim that line: a Six scanning the room for safety, a Two for what people need, a Nine merging with it, a Three reading the audience. It is also the craft rule she says her parents taught her. "What the other person is doing is always more interesting than what you can create," she said on TODAY.

## Six, Nine, Two or Three: weighing the case

### Leading read: Type 6

The Six case rests on a word she keeps choosing. She talks about responsibility: to the other actors in _Appropriate_, to "an artistic legacy," to "the part, to the anthology, to the real person," to "what was on the page." Then she secures the ground before she brings herself. Duty to the group, and the cost of letting it down, sits near the center of Six. In February 2025 she named the Six's growth task, trusting herself to handle what she worries about, as "something I'm still really trying to do." And the one report of nerves with nothing at stake, between performances in 2025, is hard to put down to a first lead.

Against it, nearly all of this is her own account in promotional settings, and the two longest conversations were with friends. Some preparation she frames as excitement, not fear: "I was so excited to immediately just delve into every podcast," she told [No Intervals](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZPPP5dTamkM). She shows none of the testing of authority that often comes with Six, and she attaches fast: "She's one of my very best friends in the world... we just kind of fell in love immediately," she said of Paulson on TODAY. Town & Country's Naveen Kumar wrote that a thoughtful relationship to uncertainty is something she "seems to be comfortable with—and maybe even covets in her work." That is one writer's impression, but it suits a Nine better than a Six seeking solid ground.

### Closest rival: Type 9

The Nine case is how she presents: credit to luck and others, "a certain amount of discomfort about being a public person" and a wish "to be able to disappear into roles," as she told Town & Country in 2026. She added, "There is also joy in sharing your life with people." What strains it is that Nines tend to turn fear down, and she names it and goes to work. In the same Variety answer as "really scared," she said, "I think immediately I just wanted to start working on it." Her ambition is old, too: asked by Paulson whether she had always wanted to act, she said, "Always, always." And the body triad's buried emotion is anger, of which the record holds no account from her.

### Also live: Type 2

The Two case is the warmth: fast attachments, praise for collaborators, and a job on _Monster_ she described to [Gayety](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Kwtu_M-LkBQ) as "to love and advocate for the person." Her accounts of pressure point outward, to "so many people to please" and not leaving people "in the lurch." What the record lacks for Two is care she gives unasked, or resentment when it goes unnoticed. In it, she is mostly the one looked after.

### Also live: Type 3

The Three case is the polish: the fast start, "Exceptionally poised," the nepotism question handled before anyone asks, the hope that her work will "make me separate." Against it are the deflection she admits and her discomfort with being public, but both come from press interviews, where a Three would also tilt toward humility. No choice on record yet shows her taking a role for its size.

Types 1, 4 and 7 were tested and set aside: nothing shows a One's inner critic, she doesn't cast herself as set apart like a Four, and she volunteers the painful feelings a Seven tends to steer around.

## What would settle it

Under the pressure on record (a first lead, a two-day put-in, a famous name), the reads look alike, since a stressed Nine can worry and prepare as hard as a Six. What the record lacks is pressure of other kinds, and quiet.

1. **Her answer to the critics.** Reviews of _Monster_ split. On September 17, 2026, [Seat42f](https://seat42f.com/monster-the-lizzie-borden-story-review/)'s Thomas Miller called her Lizzie "an impressive television lead turn." A day later [IndieWire](https://www.indiewire.com/criticism/shows/monster-season-4-review-monster-the-movie-aileen-wuornos-1235217589/)'s Ben Travers, grading the season D+, described "a perpetually blank Ella Beatty, the daughter of Warren Beatty and Annette Bening." No response from her had been found as of October 18, 2026. Does she doubt herself and prepare harder (Six), let it pass (Nine), or point to results (Three)?
2. **Worry after success.** Golden Globe nominations are due December 7, 2026, according to the [Golden Globes' timetable](https://awardsradar.com/2026/05/07/84th-golden-globes-timetable-eligibility-rules-and-campaign-guidelines-announced/), and _The Bell Jar_ had no release date as of October 18, 2026. Does she start owning the work (Three), or are nerves and self-trust still her subject once she is no longer new (Six)? If the worry fades with the newness, that favors Nine.
3. **A disagreement.** As of October 18, 2026, no account of one from her had been found. Does she test whether she can trust the director (Six), or go along and keep the peace (Nine)? What she does with her own irritation would say the most.
4. **A choice against her circle.** She has worked twice for Murphy and twice with Paulson, so going back to them would show little. The telling case is a choice against their advice or her parents', and the reason she gives: whom she trusts (Six), what the role can do for her (Three) or whom she wants to work beside (Two).

Some of this is already settled. She was early to the Carlyle and off book at the Belasco. She calls her parents "loving, encouraging," and says they "want me to feel like I have my own experience." When Town & Country pointed out that in one year she played a daughter dragged away from acting by her parents and one who, in the series, kills her father and stepmother, she didn't offer a theory. "What is the universe saying? I don't know," she said with a chuckle. "I'm going to be examining that."
