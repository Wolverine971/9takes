Mike Markkula, a 33-year-old early retiree from Intel, was ready to bankroll Apple on one condition. Apple was still a small partnership selling Steve Wozniak's Apple I, and Wozniak had designed the Apple II on his own time. Before the company incorporated in January 1977, Markkula wanted him to quit Hewlett-Packard and work for it full time ([Fast Company](https://fastcompanyme.com/technology/how-apple-became-apple-the-definitive-history-of-the-companys-earliest-days/)).

Wozniak thought it over and said no. He told the story to Jessica Livingston for [_Founders at Work_](https://www.foundersatwork.com/steve-wozniak.html) (2007). There was an "ultimatum day," and he met Markkula and Steve Jobs at Markkula's cabana in Cupertino:

> Eventually we got around to it, and I said, "I've decided not to do it, here are my reasons." Mike just said, "Okay." Steve was a little more upset.

The next day the phone started ringing. His parents called and told him he really ought to do it. Then friends called. Randy Wigginton, a teenager writing software for Apple at the time, told Newsweek in 2011 that the calls were not a coincidence ([via 9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2011/10/11/some-notable-quotes-from-apple-employees-1-and-6-on-the-early-days-with-steve-jobs/)):

"I'll never forget the night Jobs called all of Woz's friends and wanted us to call Woz and tell him to quit HP and start Apple. Woz wanted to stay at HP. So we did it. Until that point, Woz was undecided."

Wigginton remembers an undecided Woz; Wozniak remembers a firm no. The call that worked came from his friend Allen Baum. "Look, you can start Apple and go into management and get rich, or you can start Apple and stay an engineer and get rich," Baum told him. Wozniak's response: "As soon as he said it was okay to do engineering, that really freed me up." He called Jobs that evening and said yes.

What changed his mind was not the money. It was permission not to be in charge.

Stephan Gary Wozniak was born on August 11, 1950, in San Jose, California. He designed the Apple I and the Apple II and formed Apple with Jobs and Ronald Wayne on April 1, 1976. He stepped away after a 1981 plane crash, came back in 1983 and left Apple's workforce in early 1985.

## What is Steve Wozniak's personality type?

Steve Wozniak's personality type is Enneagram Type 9, the Peacemaker. Nines keep their inner peace by refusing the fight: they defer, play their anger down and step around confrontation. Wozniak turned down running Apple, shrank his grievances against Steve Jobs and took leave in 1981 rather than ask for it. Inside his own designs, he would not budge.

[Type 9](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-9) sits in the instinctual, or gut, triad: the three types organized around anger. An [Eight](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-8) lets the anger out. A [One](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-1) turns it into rules and correction. A Nine goes to sleep on it. The anger is there, but it gets shrunk, explained away or walked around until there is nothing left to fight about.

Wozniak had more reason than most to be angry at his partner. Before Apple, Jobs brought him in on a job for Atari and, by Wozniak's later account, kept part of the pay. In 2006, NPR's [Deborah Amos](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/6179983) asked about talk of a feud. "There's never been a Jobs versus Wozniak issue," he said. When she raised the Atari money:

> "He ripped me off maybe when we were younger. It could be that he was paid more than he told me. This is such a little diddly bit of the world..."

That is the Nine's move with anger, in one breath: name it, soften it with "maybe," then make it small. ("Maybe" can also be honest doubt about a deal from 1975, and he raises that doubt himself.)

The mechanism, in plain steps:

1. He avoids the argument. He defers to forceful people, or he quietly does what he wants without a fight.
2. He names a grievance, then shrinks it until there is nothing to fight about.
3. He guards one territory where nobody can push him: the design itself.
4. When he disagrees with where things are going, he keeps quiet and steps back instead of contesting it.

## "I wasn't going to tell other people how to do things"

Telling Livingston about the ultimatum, Wozniak gave two reasons for his first no. One was Hewlett-Packard: he "really wanted a job as an engineer forever at a great company." The other he called his "psychological block," and it was about force:

> "My psychological block was really that I didn't want to start a company. Because I was just afraid. In business and politics, I wasn't going to be a real strong participant. I wasn't going to tell other people how to do things. I wasn't going to run things ever in my life. I was a non-political person and I was a very non-forceful person."

He traces it to "a lot of things that happened during the Vietnam War," the years when, he told Guy Kawasaki, he "became a pacifist." The fear he names is of being the one who pushes.

He has given that reason for decades. On NPR's [Science Friday](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/6167297) in 2006: "I just was non-political and didn't see myself as a person who could push people around, make their decision, you know, and tell them how lousy their work was."

A line repeated on the speaking circuit for decades can harden into a talking point. Repetition shows a consistent story, not a proven motive. His choices are what make it more than a story.

Before Apple, he offered the computer to his employer. "My loyalty to that company was extreme," he told [Guy Kawasaki](https://guykawasaki.com/steve-wozniak/). HP had to turn it down first, and did. "That was the first of five turndowns from Hewlett-Packard," he told Harry McCracken for Fast Company's 2026 [oral history of Apple](https://fastcompanyme.com/technology/how-apple-became-apple-the-definitive-history-of-the-companys-earliest-days/).

Ronald Wayne, the third founding partner, who left the venture quickly, drew up the founding contract. He told Fast Company:

> "I think it took me about 25 minutes to get Woz to understand the importance of having his circuit designs be a part of the company after he had created them."

Wayne's memory suggests a man who did not think of his designs as property to protect. Some of that may be hobbyist culture: Wozniak handed his schematics out with no copyright notices.

## Eight slots and no argument

For Wozniak, the going-along stopped at the circuit board.

Jobs wanted the Apple II to have two expansion slots, one for a printer and one for a modem. Wozniak wanted eight. In _Founders at Work_ he gave the reason plainly: two slots "wouldn't save me a single chip. And I wanted to show off that I had 8 slots and so few chips."

> "I was really dead set to hold my chip count, so I said, 'If you want 2 slots, get another computer.' That was the only time we had a real argument."

Livingston asked whether Jobs kept pushing. "No, he had no choice. I gave him no choice." Allen Baum told Fast Company the slots were something "which Steve Jobs didn't want at all." They mattered: Fast Company's history credits them with making the Apple II "more readily expandable than the TRS-80 or PET."

The stubbornness is real, and it belongs to the type: Nines are hard to move once they have settled somewhere. But Wozniak is not defending a title or his standing against Jobs. He is defending a design and his pride in it.

That pride runs all through the early record. He brought the Apple I to the Homebrew Computer Club "to boast, to be clever, to get acknowledgement for having designed a very inexpensive computer." He wanted credit, but from other engineers, for cleverness, and he gave the designs away: "I passed out my designs with no copyright notices," he told Fast Company. The design was where he spoke, and the one place he would not be moved.

## The Breakout money he wouldn't fight over

In 1975, Jobs brought Wozniak in to design the hardware for Atari's arcade game Breakout in four days. In December 1984, Wozniak described the deal to [BYTE](https://www.apple2history.org/museum/articles/byte8412/) as he then understood it: "So he said if we could design a hardware Breakout in under 50 chips, we'd get 700 bucks; and if it was under 40 chips, we'd get $1000." Their design came in at 44 chips. "So we only got 700 bucks for it."

He says he learned the rest "12 years later": Jobs "got paid a lot more than he told me, and he only gave me half of a smaller amount." [Wikipedia's history of Breakout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breakout_%28video_game%29), drawing on Atari-side accounts, records his statement that he received $350 and describes a $5,000 chip bonus that Jobs kept secret. Jobs died in 2011, and his response is not quoted here. Wozniak flags the limits of memory himself: "And what if I remembered something wrong too? It's so long ago."

The money, he insists, was never the point: "I would have done it for 25 cents. So that wasn't the point." The dishonesty was. To Wharton he said:

> "I think Steve needed money and just didn't tell me the truth. If he'd told me the truth, he'd have gotten it."

Then he explains Jobs's side for him. "He was a friend. You help your friends." To Newsweek in 2011: "The legend is true. It didn't matter to me. I had a job."

He did not want the story in his 2006 memoir, _iWoz_. When Amos pointed out that it was there, he answered: "Yes. I didn't want to. You know, I didn't want to. And I had a co-author and she said, you should speak it anyway. And I speak the truth and it went in the book."

That last sentence matters. Wharton read him a line from _iWoz_, "The most important measure of a person was truth," and he answered, "I believe that to this day." He does not hide the grievance; he has told it to Livingston, Wharton and Newsweek.

What he does is frame it small, every time: "maybe," "He probably needed the money," "such a little diddly bit of the world." Some of that is a generous man with a steady paycheck. But the framing suggests the second step of the pattern: the grievance is spoken, then sized so it never has to become a fight.

## "The plane crash was a good excuse"

On February 7, 1981, Wozniak crashed the small plane he was piloting soon after takeoff from Scotts Valley, California.

In BYTE's January 1985 issue, shortly before he left Apple's workforce, he was asked why he had left the company after the crash. His answer, given close to the events and long before the later retellings:

> "We had a hundred engineers at that point, and I was no longer really important to the company. I didn't want to be a manager: I was just an engineer, and I wasn't really needed there. But I didn't feel comfortable going to Steve Jobs or Mike Markkula and saying I wanted to take off. The plane crash was a good excuse. After five weeks of amnesia, I simply didn't go back. I decided that if I was going to take a year off I might as well finish college."

He enrolled at Berkeley as Rocky Raccoon Clark, because by then, [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Wozniak) notes, his own name was well known. He staged the US Festivals in 1982 and 1983, and later in 1983 he returned to Apple product development as an engineer.

There are other explanations for 1981. He no longer felt needed. A head injury can change a person: the Apple history _Infinite Loop_ describes the crash as "like flipping a reset switch in Woz's brain." Not wanting to bother the bosses could be ordinary politeness. What makes it read as a pattern is the sentence in the middle: he wanted time off and did not feel comfortable asking for it.

The same interview has a second case. When Apple turned toward business buyers and the Apple III, he says, "I kept my mouth shut because I was only one out of the staff of 15."

The 1985 exit cuts the other way. Before leaving he had "gone on a whiteboard and shown all the Apple executives" his remote-control idea. Press coverage quoted him saying Apple had "been going in the wrong direction for the last five years," and he did complain that Apple II engineers were "being treated like they didn't exist." He has objected to that framing since: he says he told the Wall Street Journal he was leaving "to start a new startup company to build a remote control." That is not a man drifting out without a word.

The one blowup he describes from those years did not happen in front of him. After he took his remote-control design to Frog Design in 1985, he says, Jobs saw the work there, threw it against the wall and told the firm it could not work for him. His summary to Newsweek: "That was the only time there was ever a fight between us, but it wasn't actually between us."

He spoke up for other engineers. When the conflict was between him and Jobs, he moved it offstage.

## "I was his resource"

The arrangement started before Apple. From late 1971, Wozniak built "blue boxes" for making free long-distance calls, and Jobs sold some two hundred of them and split the profit with him.

People who watched the partnership up close describe something lopsided. "It was more like Woz would put up with Jobs," Wigginton said. Wozniak describes it the same way, without complaint: "Anything we did, I give it all to Steve if he wanted it," he told Kawasaki.

After Jobs died in October 2011, Wozniak told Newsweek he had turned down Walter Isaacson's request to talk for the official biography: "I didn't want to talk about Steve. I was afraid he wouldn't want it."

In March 2026, for Apple's 50th anniversary, CBS Sunday Morning asked him how the company started. He joked that it "kind of started when I was born," then put it simply ([via 9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2026/03/09/steve-wozniak-on-apple-at-50-we-didnt-foresee-the-future-but-we-took-the-first-step/)):

> "Steve Jobs wanted a company and he did it, and I was his resource."

Anniversary modesty may be part of it, but it matches the record from the blue boxes onward: Jobs wanted things, and Wozniak went along. (Our [Steve Jobs analysis](/personality-analysis/steve-jobs) tells the story from the other side of the partnership.)

## Smiles minus frowns

Wozniak says he settled on a philosophy of life around age 20. On Science Friday in 2006:

> "I figured out that it was better - less good to be successful and better to have a laughing life, laugh more than you frown all through your life."

He turned it into a "happiness equation," as [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2017/06/01/why-apple-co-founder-steve-wozniak-is-the-original-geek/) reported from a 2017 talk: H = S – F, happiness equals smiles minus frowns. In August 2025 he wrote on Slashdot ([via Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Aug/15/steve-wozniak/)): "Life to me was never about accomplishment, but about Happiness, which is Smiles minus Frowns. I developed these philosophies when I was 18-20 years old and I never sold out."

Half of that equation is subtraction. He told Kawasaki which half he was better at: getting "rid of frowns was where I was even more successful. Don't ever argue with anybody." He traced the rule to becoming "very non-competitive" as a pacifist, and to a lesson from his father: "Somebody dents your car, don't go looking to blame somebody and jumping out and screaming and getting upset." His summary: "Only take the progressive steps, the constructive steps, not the disruptive ones."

That is a self-report, not proof of motive. But it says plainly what the frowns term is for.

Money got the same treatment. "Pretty much I gave it all away to charities, to museums, to children's groups, to everything I could," he told Livingston. "It almost was like an evil to me."

Some went to colleagues. When Apple went public in 1980, five early staffers, including Wigginton and Chris Espinosa, had no stock, and Wozniak gave them some of his own. He also sold shares to about 40 employees instead of to an outside investor, and used that money to buy a house. Apple's lawyers worried about the sale, and Markkula, he says, felt some of the recipients didn't deserve stock. "But I disagreed with him on that. Nobody stopped me, so I did it."

That was a real override of Apple's chairman, with a fortune at stake, and it counts against any reading of him as simply compliant. Its shape is familiar, though. As with the slots, he did not try to win Markkula over. He acted, and nobody stopped him.

After leaving Apple's full-time staff, he went back to a plan he had made as a child. "I had wanted to teach my whole life," he told Wharton. He taught computer classes to fifth through ninth graders, for eight years by his count.

## Why not Type 7?

The strongest alternative is [Type 7](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-7). Sevens belong to the head triad, where the core emotion is fear. They handle it by staying in motion: they chase the next good thing, keep their options open and reframe pain so they never get stuck in it. The prankster record is long, and the witnesses are independent.

Bill Fernandez, Apple's first employee, told Fast Company that "Woz has always been a prankster." He told [Y Combinator](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/employee-1-apple) in 2016 about "Ramar the Mystic," a Berkeley phone prank that ended in a water balloon.

Add Dial-a-Joke, the Bay Area joke line he ran. Add the US Festivals. Jobs told him, "That music concert is not your thing. You're a computer designer." Wozniak disagreed: "I thought fun and entertainment's all part of life." Add _Dancing with the Stars_ in 2009, and, in 2025, "I am the happiest person ever."

After 1985 he founded CL 9 to build a universal remote, then Wheels of Zeus in 2001 (NPR introduced him in 2006 as its "founder and CEO"), Acquicor in 2006, Woz U in 2017, Efforce in 2020 and Privateer Space in 2021. By his memoir's account, as Wikipedia summarizes it, he left Apple in 1985 mainly to recapture the fun of building new technology. That is a Seven's reason.

The play is real, and it belongs to Wozniak, not to a mask. So is the appetite for new ventures, the part the Nine reading explains least. The Nine case rests on what he does when what he wants meets resistance.

A Seven in that spot pushes past the limit and argues for it if cornered. Wozniak steps around the argument.

At the ultimatum, he moved once Baum took away the part he feared, running people. He took leave in 1981 rather than ask for it. He did the concert and the stock gifts over objections from Jobs and Markkula, by his account without trying to win anyone over. Even the slots, "the only time we had a real argument," ended in one sentence, not a campaign. He declined to give Isaacson his version because Jobs might not want it. And his public accusation during _Dancing with the Stars_, that the show had lied about the vote count to keep him on, ended with a retraction and an apology.

The reframing works differently too. A Seven reframes pain to keep moving toward the next good thing. Wozniak shrinks the grievance to keep the relationship exactly as it was.

One thing the Nine reading does not tidy up. Talking to Wharton in 2008 about how humor norms had changed since his youth, he said he had told Polish jokes on Dial-a-Joke. Wozniak is of Polish descent and took Polish citizenship in 2017, but the defense he gave was general:

> "I have always felt the laughter and humor you generate in people almost always outweigh the negative of a person who feels offended — whether rightly so or not."

That is not someone avoiding friction at any cost. For him, a joke seems to sit outside the rule against imposing on people. The type does not excuse that view, and it counts against the Nine case.

A [Six](/enneagram-corner/enneagram-type-6) reading has evidence too. He called HP "my job for life," a company that "treated us like we were a community and family." He needed HP to turn his computer down before he felt free to build it elsewhere, and he stayed on Apple's payroll so he would "never depart."

Baum's call was, in part, reassurance from a trusted friend. But a Six under pressure tends to hold tighter to the job and the people that keep them safe. Wozniak walked away from Apple's security twice and gave most of his fortune away. His cure for worry is not preparation or backing. It is, in his words, "largely not worrying."

## Woz U and the name on the door

In October 2017, Wozniak founded [Woz U](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woz_U), an online coding school. A year later, CBS News reported complaints from more than two dozen current and former students and employees, as summarized by [MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2018/10/01/steve-wozniaks-woz-u-blowback/) on October 1, 2018. They described pre-recorded "live" lectures, unqualified mentors, missing instructors and sales pressure. One student, Bill Duerr, had enrolled in the $13,200, 33-week program. "I feel like this is a $13,000 e-book," he said.

These were allegations against the company, reported by CBS. Wozniak told Business Insider by email that he had not seen the report and was "not involved in any operations aspects" of Woz U. Woz U president Chris Coleman acknowledged errors in course content and denied that students were pressured to enroll. He also said Wozniak reviews all of the curriculum, which sits uneasily with Wozniak's account. According to Woz U's Wikipedia entry, Wozniak "declined interview requests, then dodged a reporter's unannounced appearance at a conference."

The same entry cites an Arizona Republic report that a complaint over the school's quality was dismissed. In May 2019, Arizona regulators voted not to renew Woz U's license, citing concerns about its ownership ([Higher Ed Dive](https://www.highereddive.com/news/dream-center-ties-could-spell-the-end-for-coding-school-woz-u/555013/)). If Wozniak's account of his role is right, it matches the Apple pattern, where he left the running of things to others, and the declined interviews fit a man who steps around confrontation. ## The engineer at the bottom of the org chart

For decades, Wozniak kept one thread connecting him to Apple. "I stay like with the minimum salary on the payroll continually just so the computer records I'm still an employee," he told Science Friday in 2006. "Of course, I'm always at the bottom of the org chart."

That arrangement gave him the belonging with no one to push around, and nobody could push him out of the work he loved. Looking back on how Apple began, he told Fast Company in 2026:

> "I never, ever once tried to start a business or an industry. I just wanted other engineers to look at my designs and say, 'Whoa, he thinks differently. He has a clever mind.'"
